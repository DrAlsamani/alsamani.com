Chapter 8

Marketing for New Ventures

An excellent product nobody knows about will not succeed, while an ordinary product with smart marketing may spread widely. But marketing in new ventures differs from marketing in large companies: the budget is small, the brand is unknown, and every customer matters. In this chapter we cover the foundations of marketing, from choosing the customer and positioning, to the marketing mix, the customer journey, digital marketing and measuring results.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Apply the marketing mix and the segmentation, targeting and positioning process.
  2. Write a positioning statement for your venture.
  3. Build a simple digital marketing plan and measure its results.

8-1What Is Marketing?

Many people reduce marketing to advertising or posting on social media, but advertising is a small part of it. Philip Kotler, one of the best-known authors in the field, defines marketing as the process of creating value for customers and building strong relationships with them, in order to capture value from them in return. In other words, marketing begins long before advertising: with understanding the customer, choosing whom to serve, designing an offer that suits them, delivering it, and then keeping them.

Marketing conditions in a new venture differ from those in large companies: the budget is small, the brand is unknown, and customers do not trust something they have not tried. A new venture therefore needs focused, smart marketing more than broad, expensive marketing.

8-2Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning (STP)

Before deciding what to say and where to advertise, you must decide whom you are talking to, and how you want them to see you. This is the STP model, three consecutive steps:

1. Segmentation

Dividing the market into groups with similar needs and characteristics, using the bases explained in Chapter 5: demographic, geographic, behavioral and psychographic.

2. Targeting

Choosing the segment (or segments) you will focus on. The target is chosen with three questions: Is the segment large enough and profitable? Can we reach and serve it? Can we outperform competitors in it? Trying to target everyone makes your message generic and noticed by no one.

3. Positioning

Positioning is the place you want your venture to occupy in the customer's mind relative to the alternatives. Al Ries and Jack Trout explained in their famous book Positioning that customers are exposed to thousands of messages, so they file products in their minds under a single simple word or idea: this one is "the cheapest," that one "the fastest," a third "the most luxurious." A venture that does not define its position is placed wherever the customer pleases, or forgotten.

Positioning is usually defined by one main advantage, not a long list of features. This template helps you write a positioning statement:

"For [target customer] who [need or problem], [venture name] is the [product category] that [main benefit]. Unlike [main alternative], it [reason it stands out]."

Example: "For shop owners who lose customers when the air conditioning fails, Baroda is a regular maintenance subscription that guarantees an emergency visit within hours. Unlike the technician you call after a breakdown, we prevent the breakdown before it happens and know your units."

A positioning statement is not an advertising slogan to publish, but an internal compass for the team: every decision about pricing, design and advertising should be consistent with it.

8-3The Marketing Mix (4Ps)

Once you have defined the customer and the positioning, you translate them into practical decisions about four elements, formulated by E. Jerome McCarthy in the 1960s and popularized by Kotler:

1. Product

What exactly are we offering? This includes the product itself, its quality, design, packaging and name, and the services that accompany it. The most important question here is: what is the benefit, not the specification? A customer does not buy "a 20-liter waterproof backpack"; they buy "a bag that protects your laptop and books in the rain." That is why it is said that people do not buy a drill; they buy the hole in the wall.

2. Price

How much does the customer pay, and how? Price is not just a number but a marketing message: a high price suggests quality and distinction, while a low price suggests economy and may suggest poor quality. Price must be consistent with positioning. We discuss pricing methods in detail in Chapter 9.

3. Place (distribution)

Where and how does the customer get the product? In a shop, online, by delivery, or through a distributor. The rule is to be where your customer is, and to make getting your product as easy as possible. Every extra step the customer needs in order to buy reduces the number who complete the purchase.

4. Promotion

How does the customer learn about your product and become convinced by it? This includes advertising, content, public relations, offers, personal selling and digital marketing. We expand on it in the fifth section.

Extra elements for services

Because a service cannot be touched before it is bought, researchers added three elements for service marketing, making the mix seven elements (7Ps):

  • People: whoever delivers the service is part of it. The manner of the employee or courier makes or breaks the experience.
  • Process: the steps of delivering the service: How does the customer book? How long do they wait? How do they pay? Every step is part of the experience.
  • Physical evidence: what the customer sees and touches and uses to judge the quality of a service they cannot see: the cleanliness of the premises, the packaging, the website design, the uniform, the invoice.

8-4The Customer Journey

Customers do not buy in a single moment. They go through stages, from the first time they hear about you to becoming a regular customer who recommends you. Understanding these stages helps you see where you lose customers:

StageWhat happensA question for the venture
AwarenessThe customer learns you exist for the first timeDo our target customers even see us?
InterestThey want to know moreIs our message clear, and does it speak to their problem?
EvaluationThey compare you with the alternativesWhy would they choose us? Do we have evidence and trust (reviews, a trial)?
PurchaseThey decide and payIs buying easy? How many steps does it take?
ExperienceThey use the product or serviceDoes the experience deliver on the promise?
RepeatThey come back to buy againWhat makes them come back?
RecommendationThey talk about you to othersIs the experience worth talking about?

Note that keeping a customer is much cheaper than attracting a new one, which is why marketing does not end at the sale; it starts there.

8-5Digital Marketing for a New Venture

Digital platforms have let small ventures reach their customers at low cost, but they have also made competition for attention fierce. Here are some practical principles:

1. Focus on one or two channels

Do not try to be on every platform with poor quality. Choose the platform where your target customer spends time, and master it. Gabriel Weinberg explains in Traction that most successful ventures grew at first through one main channel they discovered by experimenting.

2. Content before advertising

Useful content builds trust before the sale: show the problem and how you solve it, behind-the-scenes work, real customer stories, and useful tips in your field. People follow those who help them, then buy from those they trust.

3. Short video

Short video has become one of the most widespread forms of content on the platforms. It does not need professional equipment; clarity, honesty and a good idea matter more than filming quality.

4. Personal recommendation

In close-knit societies like ours, buying decisions are strongly influenced by the opinions of relatives and friends. A satisfied customer who talks about you is more powerful than any advertisement. You can encourage this with an experience worth talking about, or with a referral program that rewards whoever brings in a new customer.

5. Micro-influencers

Accounts with fewer followers who are interested in your field or in your city are often more useful, cheaper and more credible than celebrities. Make sure any collaboration with them is clearly disclosed, in compliance with regulations and out of respect for the audience.

6. Customer service is part of marketing

A fast, polite reply and solving the problem when it happens create loyalty. A complaint handled well can turn an angry customer into one of your most loyal.

8-6Measure, Don't Guess

One advantage of digital marketing is that it can be measured. The most important metrics for a new venture are:

  • Conversion rate: the share of those who completed a purchase out of those who viewed or visited. If 500 people visit your page and 25 buy, the conversion rate is 5%.
  • Customer acquisition cost (CAC): total marketing spend in a period ÷ the number of new customers in the same period. If you spend SAR 3,000 and gain 60 customers, the cost per customer is SAR 50.
  • Customer lifetime value (LTV): the average a customer pays over their whole relationship with you. If a customer buys a monthly subscription at SAR 100 and stays six months on average, their value is about SAR 600 in revenue.
  • Retention rate: the share of customers who come back or renew.

The basic rule: customer value must be clearly greater than the cost of acquiring the customer, or every new customer loses you money. In the example above, if customer value is SAR 600 and acquisition cost is SAR 50, the model is healthy. But if a customer buys once for SAR 40 and costs SAR 50 to acquire, the venture loses money with every customer.

8-7Honest Marketing

Marketing that succeeds over the long term rests on trust. So avoid exaggerated promises you cannot keep, fake reviews and ratings, undisclosed advertising presented as personal opinion, and hidden fees or conditions. Comply with the regulations governing advertising, e-commerce and consumer protection, and respect your customers' data and privacy. A reputation built in a year can be destroyed in a day, especially in a society where news travels fast.

Chapter summary

  • Marketing is understanding the need, designing an offer that meets it, delivering it to the customer and keeping them.
  • Marketing starts with choosing the segment, then defining the positioning relative to the alternatives.
  • The marketing mix: product, price, place and promotion, with people, process and physical evidence added for services.
  • A new venture focuses on one or two channels, and measures customer acquisition cost and lifetime value.

Key terms

Segmentation, then targeting, then positioning.
