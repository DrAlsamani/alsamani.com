Chapter 1

Entrepreneurship: Concept and Importance

Entrepreneurship is talked about so much in the media and in support programs that the term has come to be applied to every commercial activity. But the concept has a more precise scientific meaning, shaped by a century of research in economics and management. In this chapter we start by defining the concept and its schools of thought, then distinguish the entrepreneurial venture from others, present the types of entrepreneurship and their role in the economy, and end with the features of the entrepreneurial ecosystem in Saudi Arabia.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Define entrepreneurship and distinguish it from traditional trade and small business management.
  2. Name the types of entrepreneurship and give an example of each.
  3. Explain the role of entrepreneurship in the Saudi economy and its support ecosystem.

1-1What Is Entrepreneurship?

The Arabic word for entrepreneurship, riyada, comes from al-ra'id: the scout who rides ahead of the tribe to find water and pasture before everyone moves. That old meaning brings us close to the modern concept. The scout goes where others have not gone, bears the hardship and uncertainty of exploring, and comes back with something that benefits the group. The English word comes from the French verb entreprendre, "to undertake" or "to take something on."

Definitions of entrepreneurship vary in the scholarly literature because people look at it from different angles. Economists look at its effect on markets, management scholars at how resources are organized, and psychologists at the person who does it. Yet these definitions meet at one core, which we can put this way:

Entrepreneurship is the process of discovering an opportunity to solve a problem or meet a need, and organizing the resources required to pursue it in a venture that adds value for people, while bearing a calculated degree of risk and uncertainty.

Let us take this definition apart, because every word in it carries meaning:

  • "Process": entrepreneurship is not a single moment of inspiration. It is a series of steps that starts with observation, moves through testing and adjustment, and continues as the venture grows. That is why it can be learned step by step.
  • "Discovering an opportunity": the starting point is neither money nor a company. It is an opportunity: a problem that has not been solved well, a need that has not been met, or a change in society or technology that opens a new door.
  • "Organizing resources": the entrepreneur does not need to own every resource, but must know how to gather and organize them: money, people, knowledge, relationships and time.
  • "Adds value": an entrepreneurial venture makes someone's life better. It saves them time, money or effort, or offers something that was not available before. Without that value, nobody will pay.
  • "Calculated risk and uncertainty": entrepreneurs work in conditions where the outcome is not known in advance. But they do not gamble; they reduce risk through study and small experiments.

1-2Three Schools for Understanding Entrepreneurship

You do not need to memorize many theories, but knowing three main schools of thought helps explain why we talk about entrepreneurship the way we do.

1. The innovation school: Schumpeter

The Austrian economist Joseph Schumpeter was among the first to place the entrepreneur at the heart of economic theory, in The Theory of Economic Development (1934 in its English translation). Schumpeter argued that economies do not develop through the accumulation of capital alone, but through people who bring "new combinations" to the market. He named five forms: a new product, a new method of production, a new market, a new source of raw materials, or a new organization of an industry.

Schumpeter also coined a famous idea, creative destruction: every successful innovation pushes aside what came before it. When smartphones with good cameras spread, sales of compact digital cameras fell; when streaming services appeared, video and disc rentals declined. This destruction hurts the old firms, but it is "creative" because it moves the economy to a better level of service.

2. The practice school: Drucker

Peter Drucker, one of the leading management thinkers of the twentieth century, moved entrepreneurship from a mysterious trait to a systematic practice. In Innovation and Entrepreneurship (1985) he describes the entrepreneur as someone who "always searches for change, responds to it, and exploits it as an opportunity." Drucker's most important contribution is that entrepreneurship is not limited to small new businesses: a hospital, a university or a government agency can be entrepreneurial if it manages innovation systematically. We will return in Chapter 3 to the seven sources of opportunity he identified.

3. The opportunity school: Shane and Venkataraman

In 2000, Scott Shane and S. Venkataraman published an influential article proposing that the subject of entrepreneurship research is the opportunity: how it is discovered, evaluated and exploited, and by whom. The focus shifted from "Who is the entrepreneur?" to "How does a particular person meet a particular opportunity?" This view is useful for students, because it means entrepreneurship does not require an extraordinary personality. It requires an eye that notices opportunities and the ability to move toward them.

The three schools in brief: the entrepreneur creates something new (Schumpeter), does so through a systematic practice that looks for change (Drucker), and all of this revolves around an opportunity that is discovered and pursued (Shane and Venkataraman).

1-3The Entrepreneur, the Trader and the Small Business Owner

Many people confuse entrepreneurship with owning any business. In fact, the difference lies neither in the size of the venture nor in its type, but in two things: the degree of innovation (does the venture offer new value or a better way?) and the ambition to grow (does it seek to expand, or is it content with its size?).

DimensionTraditional businessEntrepreneurial venture
Starting pointA known model, proven in the marketA problem or opportunity that is poorly served
InnovationLow: repeats what existsMedium to high: offers something different
Risk and uncertaintyLower, because the market and demand are knownHigher, because the model itself needs proof
GrowthLinear and gradualAims for fast, scalable growth
ExampleA neighborhood grocery selling what other groceries sellA platform that connects neighborhood groceries with residents and delivers orders within an hour

A real-world example makes this clearer. Someone opens a café serving the same coffee in the same way on a street that already has ten cafés. This is a legitimate and useful business, but it is not entrepreneurship in the precise sense. Someone else notices that staff at the nearby hospital work night shifts and cannot find good coffee after midnight, so they set up a pre-ordered coffee delivery service for night shifts. This is closer to entrepreneurship, because it starts from an unserved need and offers a different solution.

This does not make traditional businesses less valuable. They are the backbone of the economy and the livelihood of many families. But this course focuses on the mindset that looks for new value, because that skill serves you in any field.

1-4Types of Entrepreneurship

When we hear the word "entrepreneurship," we usually picture an app or a tech company. But entrepreneurship is much broader than that, and it can be classified by goal and by the nature of growth:

1. Small business entrepreneurship

A venture set up by a person or a family as a stable source of income, serving a limited local community: a repair workshop, a home kitchen, a small training center. Its owner does not necessarily seek large-scale expansion, but stability and quality of service. It is entrepreneurial when it offers something different in its local market.

2. Scalable startups

A venture searching for a business model that can be repeated and grown very fast, serving thousands or millions of customers without its costs multiplying at the same rate. Startups usually rely on technology, need outside investment, and accept high risk in exchange for the chance of a large return. Steve Blank defines a startup as "a temporary organization in search of a repeatable and scalable business model": it is in a phase of searching and testing, not of executing a known plan.

3. Social entrepreneurship

A venture whose first goal is to solve a social or environmental problem, such as employing people with disabilities, reducing food waste or educating underserved groups. It differs from charity in that it seeks financial sustainability: it earns enough from its activity to cover its costs and keep going, rather than relying entirely on donations. Profit here is a means, not an end.

4. Intrapreneurship

Entrepreneurship practiced from inside an existing organization, public or private. The intrapreneur notices a problem in the organization's work, proposes a new solution and leads it through to implementation: a nurse who proposes a system that reduces medication errors, a teacher who develops a way to follow up with struggling students, or a government employee who automates a paper-based procedure. This type is the closest to most students after graduation, which is why we say this course is for everyone.

5. Family entrepreneurship

Ventures owned and run by a family and passed between generations. Many of the large companies in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf began as a small shop. The entrepreneurial challenge here is renewal: how does the new generation preserve what the previous one built and develop it to fit a changing market?

6. Digital entrepreneurship

Ventures based entirely or mostly online: e-commerce stores, content creation, apps and remotely delivered services. Their advantage is a relatively low cost of starting and the ability to reach customers beyond the city limits. But competition is fierce, because entry is easy for everyone.

1-5Why Economies and Societies Care About Entrepreneurship

Governments, universities and organizations care about entrepreneurship because it plays roles that large companies cannot play alone:

  • Creating jobs: small and medium enterprises employ a large share of workers in most economies, and every successful venture creates jobs for people other than its founder.
  • Innovation: many of the products and services we use today came from small companies that were bolder and more flexible than the large ones, and then grew.
  • Economic diversification: entrepreneurship opens new sectors, such as tourism, entertainment, technology and the creative industries, instead of relying on a single resource. This is a central goal of Saudi Vision 2030.
  • Competition and better service: new entrants push existing businesses to improve quality and lower prices, and consumers benefit.
  • Local development: small ventures serve the needs of cities and villages and keep money circulating in the local community.
  • Empowering individuals: entrepreneurship gives young people, women and families a chance to build their own income and future.

1-6Entrepreneurship in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Vision 2030 made small and medium enterprises a central pillar of economic transformation, with a stated target of raising their contribution to GDP from about 20% to 35%. To get there, an entrepreneurial ecosystem has taken shape over recent years: a network of organizations that complement one another in supporting a venture from idea to growth. Its main components include:

OrganizationIts role in the ecosystem
Small and Medium Enterprises General Authority (Monsha'at)The regulator and supporter of the sector: training and mentoring programs, incubators and accelerators, entrepreneur service centers, and initiatives that simplify procedures.
Social Development BankFinances startups and freelance work, with preparation programs before financing.
Kafalah programGuarantees part of the financing that banks give small enterprises, lowering the bank's risk and making loans easier to obtain.
Saudi Venture Capital Company and Jada Fund of FundsInvest in venture capital and private equity funds, which in turn finance startups.
Incubators and acceleratorsPrograms that host ventures in their early stages, offering mentoring, space and networks, and sometimes early funding.
University entrepreneurship centersThe nearest starting point for students: competitions, workshops, mentoring and links to the ecosystem.

These organizations work as a chain. A student may start with an idea in a university competition, then join an incubator that helps build a prototype, then receive early funding from a government program or an investor, and then grow and attract larger investment.

Program details and requirements change constantly. Always check the organizations' official websites before applying to any program.

Chapter summary

  • Entrepreneurship is the process of discovering an opportunity and organizing resources to turn it into value, with calculated risk.
  • What distinguishes an entrepreneurial venture is innovation and the ambition to grow, not merely owning a business.
  • Entrepreneurship comes in many types, including intrapreneurship, practiced by employees inside their organizations.
  • Entrepreneurship plays a role in creating jobs, innovation and diversifying the economy, and in Saudi Arabia it is supported by a broad government and private ecosystem.
