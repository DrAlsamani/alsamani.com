Chapter 9

Fundamentals of Finance and Funding

Many students avoid the financial side, thinking it is for accountants. But its basics are simple, and knowing them is what separates a nice idea from a venture that survives. In this chapter we explain the types of costs, revenue and profit, contribution margin and the break-even point, pricing methods, the difference between profit and cash flow, and then the sources and stages of funding.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish between start-up, fixed and variable costs.
  2. Calculate contribution margin and the break-even point.
  3. Distinguish between profit and cash flow, and know the sources and stages of funding.

9-1Why Every Entrepreneur Needs Financial Basics

It is said that money is the language of business: every decision in a venture, from price to the number of employees to expansion, is ultimately translated into numbers. An entrepreneur does not need to be an accountant, but does need a clear grasp of a few basic concepts, because their absence is why many small ventures sell a lot and then close: their owners did not work out properly how much each product costs them, or how much they must sell to cover their costs.

In this chapter we explain the concepts one after another and apply them to a single example, so you can see how they fit together:

The example: "Hamsa," a small specialty coffee roastery that roasts its own beans and sells them in bags through an online store, shipping to customers in cities across Saudi Arabia.

9-2Types of Costs

The first step is knowing where the money goes. Costs in small ventures fall into three types, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes:

1. Start-up (capital) costs

Paid once when the venture starts, to get it ready to operate. They do not recur every month, but they can be large. They include: equipment and machines, fitting out the premises, designing the brand identity and logo, the first license, initial inventory, and the rent deposit.

In the example: a small roasting machine and equipment (SAR 20,000), fitting out the premises (5,000), designing the identity and online store (4,000), and license and procedure fees (1,000). Total: SAR 30,000.

2. Fixed (operating) costs

Recur every month and do not change with sales volume: you pay them whether you sell one bag or a thousand. They include: rent, fixed salaries, subscriptions (internet, software, platforms), average electricity and water, insurance, and loan installments.

In the example: roastery rent (SAR 3,000), an assistant's salary (4,500), and electricity plus store and platform subscriptions (1,500). Total: SAR 9,000 a month.

Don't forget your own salary. Many students count their employees' salaries and forget themselves, so the venture looks profitable when in fact it does not reward its owner for their time. If you will work in the venture, give yourself a reasonable salary within the fixed costs, then look again: is the venture still profitable?

3. Variable costs

Linked to each unit sold: they rise as sales rise and fall as sales fall. They include: raw materials, packaging, payment gateway fees, delivery charges per order, and platform commission if any.

In the example: green coffee beans (SAR 18 per bag), the bag and label (3), and shipping and payment fees (4). Total: SAR 25 per bag.

How do you tell fixed from variable? Ask: if I sold nothing this month, would I still pay this cost? If yes, it is fixed; if no, it is variable. Some costs are mixed, like electricity, which has a fixed part and a part that grows with production; in small ventures it is enough to estimate it at its monthly average within fixed costs.

Time is a cost too

One of the costs most often overlooked by people with new ideas is time. If the service requires the provider to spend time on the road, waiting or setting up, the customer pays nothing for that time, but it reduces the number of customers who can be served in a day, raising the real cost per customer. So always ask: How many customers can the venture actually serve in a day? And look back at the mobile barber example in Chapter 6, where the barber needed more than double the shop price to earn the same income.

9-3Revenue and Profit

Revenue is the money that comes into the venture from sales: unit price × number of units sold. It is not profit, but the money before any cost is deducted.

Costs are then deducted in stages, and the table that shows this is called the income statement (or profit and loss statement). Suppose Hamsa sells a bag for SAR 55 and sold 400 bags this month:

ItemCalculationAmount (SAR)
Revenue400 × 5522,000
(−) Variable costs400 × 2510,000
= Total contribution margin22,000 − 10,00012,000
(−) Fixed costsRent + salary + services9,000
= Operating profit12,000 − 9,0003,000

Statutory obligations such as zakat or tax, depending on the business's status, are then deducted from operating profit, leaving net profit. The profit margin is profit as a share of revenue: in the example 3,000 ÷ 22,000 ≈ 14%, meaning the venture keeps about SAR 14 of every SAR 100 it sells, before statutory obligations.

9-4Contribution Margin and the Break-even Point

These are the two most important concepts in this chapter, because they answer every entrepreneur's question: How much must I sell so I don't lose money?

Unit contribution margin

The difference between the selling price of a unit and its variable cost:

Unit contribution margin = selling price − variable cost per unit
In the example: 55 − 25 = SAR 30

It is called a "contribution margin" because every bag you sell "contributes" SAR 30: first to covering fixed costs, and once they are all covered, everything beyond is profit.

The break-even point

The sales volume at which revenue equals total costs, so the venture neither profits nor loses:

Break-even point (units) = monthly fixed costs ÷ unit contribution margin
In the example: 9,000 ÷ 30 = 300 bags a month, or 10 bags a day on average.

Let us check: at 300 bags, revenue = 300 × 55 = SAR 16,500, and costs = (300 × 25) + 9,000 = 7,500 + 9,000 = SAR 16,500. Profit is zero.

Monthly salesTotal contribution marginFixed costsResult

What do you do with the break-even point? Ask: Is selling 10 bags a day realistic? How many orders do we actually expect, based on our experiments? If the number is far from reality, you have three options: raise the price (if the value allows it), lower the variable cost (a cheaper supplier, simpler packaging, consolidated shipping), or lower the fixed costs (smaller premises, or renting time on an existing roastery at first instead of buying a machine). Try these scenarios in the break-even calculator in the toolbox.

Where do start-up costs go?

Notice that we did not include the SAR 30,000 in start-up costs in the monthly break-even calculation. The reason is that it is not a monthly cost but an amount paid once, recovered from profits after passing the break-even point. The time needed to recover it is called the payback period:

Payback period = start-up costs ÷ monthly profit
If the venture sells 400 bags a month (profit SAR 3,000): 30,000 ÷ 3,000 = 10 months.
If it sells 600 bags (profit 9,000): 30,000 ÷ 9,000 ≈ 3.3 months.
A common mistake is for a student to divide the start-up costs by the contribution margin and call the result "the break-even point." That result tells you how many units you need to recover the start-up costs, but it ignores the fixed costs you pay every month.

9-5Profit Margin and Net Profit: The Coffee Cup Example

Many people confuse three closely related terms: contribution margin, net profit and profit margin. To make the differences clear, let us take the simplest venture possible: a small café that sells only one product, a single type of coffee at a single price, so the calculation is easy and every concept stands out.

The example: a café that sells only one type of coffee, at SAR 15 a cup. The variable cost of a cup is SAR 6 (coffee 3, milk 1.5, cup and lid 1, payment fee 0.5). Its fixed costs are SAR 15,000 a month (rent 6,000, two employees' salaries 8,000, electricity and subscriptions 1,000).

1. Contribution margin: what one cup leaves behind

Contribution margin per cup = 15 − 6 = SAR 9. This is not profit yet; it is what remains of the cup after its variable cost, and it goes first to covering the rent and salaries. The break-even point = 15,000 ÷ 9 ≈ 1,667 cups a month, about 56 cups a day.

2. Net profit: what is actually left at the end of the month

Suppose the café sold 2,000 cups this month:

ItemCalculationAmount (SAR)
Revenue2,000 × 1530,000
(−) Variable costs2,000 × 612,000
= Total contribution margin2,000 × 918,000
(−) Fixed costsRent + salaries + services15,000
= Operating profit18,000 − 15,0003,000
(−) Statutory obligations (such as zakat), assumed300
= Net profit3,000 − 3002,700

Notice the large difference: every cup "contributes" SAR 9, but the net amount left from each cup this month is 2,700 ÷ 2,000 = only SAR 1.35. If we break down the price of a cup (SAR 15), it divides like this: SAR 6 variable cost, SAR 7.5 its share of fixed costs (15,000 ÷ 2,000), SAR 0.15 statutory obligations, and SAR 1.35 net profit. That is why it is wrong to say "I make SAR 9 on every cup."

3. Profit margin: profit as a share of sales

Net profit margin = net profit ÷ revenue. In the example: 2,700 ÷ 30,000 = 9%, meaning the café keeps SAR 9 of every SAR 100 it sells. This ratio is useful because it allows comparison between two ventures of different sizes, or between two months of the same venture.

4. What stays fixed, and what changes with sales?

If the number of cups sold changes, while the price and costs stay the same:

The last column is constant: every extra cup adds SAR 9, before the break-even point and after it; before it, the 9 goes to covering fixed costs, and after it, it becomes profit. But the average profit per cup and the profit margin rise with sales, because the same fixed costs are spread over more cups. However much sales grow, the profit margin before obligations will never exceed 60% (9 ÷ 15), because that is the most a cup leaves after its variable cost. The same happens at Hamsa: every extra bag adds SAR 30, and the average profit per bag rises from SAR 7.5 at 400 bags to SAR 15 at 600 bags.

When does the margin not stay fixed? The calculation above assumes that the price and the variable cost do not change, an assumption that holds within a certain range of sales. In reality the margin may change for three reasons: (1) a supplier discount for buying larger quantities, which lowers the variable cost and raises the margin; (2) cutting the price or running offers to sell more, which lowers the margin; (3) a jump in fixed costs beyond a certain point, such as the café needing a third employee or a second machine, which creates a new break-even point. So redo the calculation whenever any of these changes.

9-6Pricing

Pricing is one of the hardest and most consequential decisions in a venture, because changing the price by a few riyals can change profit substantially. If Hamsa raised its price from SAR 55 to 60, the contribution margin would rise from 30 to 35, and the break-even point would fall from 300 to about 258 bags. There are four main pricing methods:

1. Cost-plus pricing

You calculate the unit cost, then add a profit percentage. Example: a variable cost of SAR 25 + 60% = SAR 40, which is far less than the specialty coffee market accepts. Its advantage is simplicity and assured coverage of variable cost. Its drawback is that it ignores what the customer is willing to pay and what competitors charge, so you may sell below your real value, or above what the market accepts.

2. Competitor-based pricing

You look at the prices of alternatives, then set yours close to them, higher if you offer more value, or lower if your positioning is economical. Its advantage is realism; its drawback is that it may make you copy others without knowing whether their prices are profitable at all.

3. Value-based pricing

You base the price on the value the customer feels, not on your cost. A gift box with three types of coffee, a brewing tool and a card explaining each origin may sell for SAR 150 even though it costs SAR 60, because what the customer buys is "a special gift worthy of the person receiving it," not just coffee. This method needs a deep understanding of the customer, which interviews reveal.

4. Penetration pricing

You start with a temporarily low price to win customers quickly, then raise it gradually. It is risky if not calculated well: customers who came for the cheap price may leave when it rises, and you may lose money on every sale until then.

The most common beginner's mistake is pricing too low for fear that nobody will buy. The result is a venture crowded with orders that loses money. Test different prices on real customers before deciding; you may be surprised to find that the higher price did not reduce demand.

9-7Profit Is Not Cash: Cash Flow

A venture can be profitable on its income statement and still be unable to pay the rent. The reason is that accounting profit differs from cash flow, the actual movement of money into and out of the account at a given time.

Example: Hamsa received a large order from a company for 300 gift bags for its employees, worth SAR 16,500, with the company paying two months after delivery. On paper this is excellent profit. But the venture needs to buy the beans and bags now (SAR 7,500) and pay this month's rent and salary (SAR 9,000). If there is not enough in the account, the venture may stumble while being "profitable."

To avoid this:

  • Keep a cash reserve covering several months of fixed costs, especially at the start.
  • Ask for a down payment on large orders.
  • Negotiate payment terms with suppliers.
  • Track cash flow monthly: How much will come in, and when? How much will go out, and when?
  • Separate the venture's account from your personal account from day one; mixing the two means you never know whether the venture is making or losing money.

9-8Sources of Funding

Every venture needs money to start or to grow. Funding sources differ in their terms and cost, and in what they ask of you in return. The first question before looking for funding: Do I really need it now? Many ventures can start small with their own resources, prove their model, and then seek funding on better terms.

9-9Investment Stages and Valuation

When a startup relies on investors, funding usually comes in successive rounds, each tied to a stage of the venture's maturity:

  • Pre-seed: an idea and a prototype, with funding usually from the founders, family and grants.
  • Seed: the product exists and has early customers, with funding from angel investors and early-stage funds.
  • Series A and beyond: the business model has proven itself, the goal is expansion, and funding comes from venture capital funds.

When someone invests in your venture in exchange for a stake, they implicitly set a value on the venture. If you offer an investor 10% of your venture for SAR 100,000, you are saying the venture is worth SAR 1 million after the investment (100,000 ÷ 10%), and 900,000 before it. You must justify this value with numbers and evidence.

With each new round new investors come in, and the founders' share of ownership falls; this is called dilution. Dilution is not necessarily bad: owning 40% of a company worth ten million is better than owning 100% of a company worth a hundred thousand. But you must understand it before you sign.

Chapter summary

  • Costs are classified as start-up, fixed and variable, and each plays a different role in the calculation.
  • Contribution margin is what each unit contributes to covering fixed costs, and the break-even point = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin.
  • The contribution margin is the same for every extra unit, while the average profit per unit and the profit margin rise with sales.
  • Start-up costs are recovered from profits after passing the break-even point.
  • Profit is not cash; negative cash flow can bring down a profitable venture.
  • Every funding source has its advantages and cost, and investment for a stake means diluting the founders' ownership.

Key terms

Start-up costs
Costs paid once when the venture begins.
Contribution margin
Selling price minus the variable cost per unit.
Net profit
What remains of revenue after deducting all costs and statutory obligations.
Profit margin
Net profit ÷ revenue; it rises with sales because fixed costs are spread over more units.
Break-even point
The sales volume with neither profit nor loss.
Cash flow
The money actually coming in and going out over a period.
Dilution
The fall in the founders' ownership share as new investors join.
