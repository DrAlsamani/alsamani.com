Chapter 10

Legal and Ethical Aspects

An idea may be strong and the market promising, and then the venture stumbles because of a dispute between partners, a trade name registered to someone else, or a regulatory violation. In this chapter we introduce the legal options for starting a venture in Saudi Arabia, the importance of an agreement between founders, the types of intellectual property, personal data protection, and the ethics that preserve a venture's reputation.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Identify the common legal forms for starting a venture in Saudi Arabia.
  2. Explain the basic types of intellectual property.
  3. Commit to business ethics and the protection of customer data.

10-1Why Should an Entrepreneur Care About Legal Matters?

People with new ideas tend to postpone legal matters: "Let's start first and sort out the paperwork later." During small-scale testing this may be acceptable within limits. But ignoring these matters for long can cost a venture dearly: a trade name you built your reputation on turns out to be registered to someone else, a dispute between partners has nothing to govern it, a fine arrives because the activity needed a license you never obtained, or customer data leaks.

This chapter does not aim to make you a legal expert, but to help you know the right questions to ask, and the bodies to turn to.

Regulations and procedures in Saudi Arabia are updated constantly, and the information here is general and simplified for educational purposes. Always check the official website of the competent authority before taking any step, and consult a specialist on contracts and important decisions.

10-2Legal Forms for Starting a Venture

Entrepreneurs choose the legal form of their venture according to the nature of its activity, the number of partners, the level of risk, and their plans for growth and investment. You do not need to start with the largest form; many ventures start in a simple form and move to a larger one as they grow.

1. Freelancing

Suits those who offer services based on individual skill, such as design, programming, translation, training, photography, writing and consulting. It is practiced formally through the freelance document issued by the freelance platform of the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development. It is one of the easiest ways to test a service idea without setting up a business, and allows a student or employee to start a side activity within the rules.

2. Home-based business

Activities practiced from home, such as cooking, handicrafts and tailoring, have dedicated licenses and programs that allow legal selling under rules related to the type of activity and product safety, and some bodies offer support and financing programs for them.

3. Sole proprietorship

A business owned by one person, registered with a commercial registration. Its key feature is that the owner and the business are one legal person: the owner is liable for the business's debts and obligations from their personal assets. It is simple to set up and run, and suits small ventures with limited risk.

4. Limited liability company

The most common form for small and medium ventures with more than one owner (it can also be set up by one person). Its main advantage is that a partner's liability is limited to their share of the capital: if the company stumbles, claims do not extend to their personal assets, except in cases the law specifies. It therefore protects partners more than a sole proprietorship does.

5. Simplified joint stock company

A new form introduced by the Companies Law that came into effect in Saudi Arabia at the start of 2023. It was designed to suit startups and entrepreneurs, because it allows more flexibility in organizing shares and in investors entering and exiting, compared with traditional forms.

Registration and licenses

Commercial registrations are issued and companies are formed through the Ministry of Commerce and the Saudi Business Center platform, which brings many procedures together in one place. Depending on the activity, you may need additional licenses: a municipal license for shops, or approvals from the authorities responsible for food and drugs, health, tourism or education. So always ask: Which authority regulates this activity?

10-3Partnership and the Founders' Agreement

Noam Wasserman's research at Harvard, collected in The Founder's Dilemmas, shows that many startups stumble because of problems between the founders themselves, not because of the market. Partnerships often start with enthusiasm and trust between friends, who feel that writing an agreement is "awkward" or a sign of "distrust." Then disputes appear when the venture grows or stumbles: Who owns how much? Who decides? Why does one of us work more than the other?

So write a founders' agreement from the start: a document that clearly answers questions such as:

  • Shares: how much does each partner own, and on what basis (money, time, experience, the idea)?
  • Roles and responsibilities: who handles what? Who makes the final decision in each area?
  • Commitment: how much time will each partner give? When will each go full-time?
  • Resolving disagreements: how do we settle a dispute? By majority, or through a third-party arbitrator?
  • A partner's exit: what happens to their share if they decide to leave or do not commit? How is it valued?
  • Profits: how are they distributed, and when? How much is reinvested?

Some companies use vesting, under which a partner earns their share gradually over years of work, so if they leave early they take only what they have earned. This protects the venture from a partner who takes a large stake and then stops working.

10-4Intellectual Property

Intellectual property is the set of rights the law protects for the products of the mind and creativity. It is among the most important assets of a modern venture: your brand, your design or your software may be worth more than all your equipment. The competent body in Saudi Arabia is the Saudi Authority for Intellectual Property. Its main types are:

1. Trademark

The name, logo or symbol that distinguishes your products or services from others. Registering it gives you the right to stop others from using a similar mark in the same field. One of the most important tips: search before you choose the name. Make sure it is not registered to someone else, then register it early, before you spend on identity and marketing.

2. Patent

Protects an invention that is new (not previously disclosed), involves an inventive step (not obvious to experts in the field), and is industrially applicable. It gives its owner an exclusive right for a set period. Registration takes time and money, and it is sought only for genuine technical inventions.

3. Copyright

Protects creative works: texts, images, designs, music, software and digital content. The right arises as soon as the work is created, and it can be documented and registered to support proof of ownership.

4. Industrial design

Protects the distinctive external appearance of a product (its shape, lines and colors), not its function. For example, the shape of a bottle or the design of a piece of furniture.

5. Trade secrets

Confidential information that gives the venture a competitive advantage, such as a recipe, a production method or a customer list. These are not registered, but protected through confidentiality and non-disclosure agreements with employees and partners.

Can an idea be protected? A question that comes up often. The answer: an abstract idea cannot be protected. You cannot stop others from thinking of "an app for booking sports fields." What is protected is the specific execution: the name, the design, the software, the technical invention. So fear of your idea being stolen should not stop you from discussing it with customers and mentors; the value of an idea lies in its execution, and execution is hard to copy.

10-5Personal Data Protection

Almost every venture collects data about its customers: names, mobile numbers, addresses, preferences. Saudi Arabia has issued a Personal Data Protection Law, overseen by the Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority (SDAIA). Its most important principles include:

  • Collect only what you need: do not ask for data you will not use.
  • Consent and clarity: tell the customer what you collect and why, and obtain their consent.
  • Specific purpose: do not use data for anything other than the purpose you collected it for, and do not sell or share it without a legal basis.
  • Protection: store data securely, and restrict who can access it.
  • Data subject rights: the customer has the right to know what you hold about them, and to request its correction or deletion.

Even in a small venture: if you collect subscribers' numbers in a group, you are responsible for ensuring they are not used for any other purpose.

10-6Entrepreneurial Ethics

Ethics in entrepreneurship is not a luxury or excessive idealism, but a practical necessity: reputation is a small venture's capital, built slowly and destroyed quickly, especially in a close-knit society where experiences pass from person to person. The most important principles include:

  • Honesty with customers: no exaggerated promises, no hidden flaws or fees, no fake reviews.
  • Integrity with partners and investors: real numbers, even when they are weak. An investor who discovers you inflated your numbers will never trust you again.
  • Respect for others' rights: do not copy a design, content or image without permission, and do not use someone else's trademark.
  • Fairness to workers: wages paid on time, clear contracts and a respectful work environment.
  • Responsibility toward society and the environment: a venture is part of its community and answers for its impact on it.
  • Keeping commitments: honoring deadlines and contracts with suppliers and customers.

Chapter summary

  • You can start with simple forms such as freelancing, then move to a sole proprietorship or company as the venture grows.
  • A written founders' agreement protects both the venture and the relationship from disputes.
  • Intellectual property protects the trademark, invention, content, design and trade secrets, not the abstract idea.
  • Collecting customer data is a legal and ethical responsibility.
  • Honesty, integrity and fairness are the foundation of a venture's reputation.

Key terms

Freelance document
A document that allows an individual to offer professional services legally.
Limited liability company
A company in which the partners' liability is limited to their shares.
Trademark
The distinctive name or logo of a product, registered for protection.
Founders' agreement
A written agreement setting out shares, roles and how disputes are resolved.
