Scene from a kindergarten

On an ordinary school morning, the kindergarten teacher notices that Yousef does not turn when she calls his name, yet turns at once when he hears a packet of biscuits being opened. He spends free play lining up small cars in a straight row, and if another child moves a single car out of place, he bursts into tears. During morning circle he repeats a line from a television commercial in exactly the tone in which he heard it.

The teacher wonders: Is this autism? Or is he a shy child who needs time to settle in? Or is something wrong with his hearing?

In this chapter we set out to answer the teacher's question systematically, returning to Yousef at each stage of the decision pathway (9.5) to show how a passing observation becomes a referral, then an evaluation, and finally a decision.

Introduction

The diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder follows a long path. It usually begins with an observation from a teacher or a family, as in Yousef's kindergarten scene, and ends with a judgment by a trained specialist after evidence has been gathered from multiple sources. Each stop along the way has its own function and its own limits: red flags call for evaluation without delay; screening tools sort but do not diagnose; rating scales convey what is seen by those who know the child; and diagnostic instruments are not sufficient on their own. Precision matters all the more because the disorder may be misdiagnosed when hearing loss or a language disorder is the better explanation, and it may be overlooked when giftedness masks it or when a girl conceals her traits.

We open the chapter by defining the disorder and reading its prevalence figures (Section 9.1), then present the diagnostic criteria in DSM-5-TR (Section 9.2), support levels, specifiers, and the comparison with ICD-11 (Section 9.3), and early red flags and delayed identification in girls (Section 9.4). We then turn to the five-stage decision pathway (Section 9.5), the instrument profiles (Section 9.6), and reading the diagnostic report (Section 9.7), before applying all of this to three cases (Section 9.8). We close with differential diagnosis (Section 9.9), co-occurring conditions and medical evaluation (Section 9.10), and the Saudi and Arab context (Section 9.11).

9.1What is autism spectrum disorder?

Autism spectrum disorder is a neurodevelopmental disorder: it is tied to the way the brain develops and functions, and its first signs appear in the early years of life. It is characterized by two core features: persistent difficulty in social communication and interaction, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior and interests (Lord et al., 2020).

The word spectrum signals that the disorder does not take a single form. We may meet a child who never speaks and needs help with most daily tasks, and an adolescent who talks fluently and excels in mathematics yet cannot tell when a classmate is joking and when he is serious. Both are on the same spectrum, but their needs differ greatly. For this reason, a report needs more than the statement “has autism”: it must also describe the level of support the student needs and any other conditions that accompany the autism.

In 2013, the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual brought together under this single label several diagnoses that had been separate in the fourth edition, among them autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified. Teachers may therefore come across a diagnosis of “Asperger's” in older student files. Today it falls within autism spectrum disorder and is usually described as autism without accompanying intellectual impairment and without accompanying language impairment.

How common is autism, and how should we read prevalence figures?

In the most recent report of the US Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network, prevalence among 8-year-olds in 2020 was about 27.6 per 1,000, or roughly one child in 36, and it was about 3.8 times higher in boys (Maenner et al., 2023). In the Arab region, the figures vary widely:

  • In a national survey in Qatar of children aged 6 to 11, prevalence was 1.14%, with a confidence interval of 0.89% to 1.46% (Alshaban et al., 2019).
  • In Riyadh, the rate was 2.51% (about one child in 40) among 398 children aged 2 to 4 at five hospitals, with three boys for every girl (AlBatti et al., 2022).
  • In Makkah and Jeddah, the number of children registered at 37 autism centers came to about 2.81 per 1,000 children (Sabbagh et al., 2021).
  • A recent study reports that the official estimate in the Kingdom is about 0.6%, and estimates actual prevalence at between 1.7% and 1.8% (Khamees et al., 2025).

These differences do not mean that autism is ten times more common in one city than in another. Each figure answers a different question. A national survey that actively searches for children differs from a sample of hospital patients, which in turn differs from center registries that count only those who have reached services. Age, the screening instrument, and the case definition also differ. The first question to ask of any prevalence figure, then, is how these children were found. A low registry figure measures not so much prevalence as the gap between those who have the disorder and those who have reached a diagnosis.

Common misconceptions
  • “It is caused by a cold mother or a neglectful family.” This is an old hypothesis from the mid-twentieth century that has been shown to be false. Autism is a neurodevelopmental disorder; families do not cause it through the way they raise their children.
  • “Vaccines cause autism.” Large-scale studies have found no association between vaccines and autism, including a meta-analysis covering more than a million children (Taylor et al., 2014).
  • “Every child with autism is a genius in some area.” Exceptional abilities are present in only some. The spectrum spans every level of intellectual ability.
  • “If a child looks me in the eye, he doesn't have autism.” Eye contact is one form of one criterion. Many autistic children do make eye contact, but in ways that do not serve communication.

9.2Diagnostic criteria in DSM-5-TR

Diagnosis relies on one of two manuals: the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), published by the American Psychiatric Association in 2022 (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), and the International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11), published by the World Health Organization (World Health Organization [WHO], 2024), which is used to code diagnoses in health records in most countries. We present the DSM criteria here because they are more detailed and because most assessment instruments and reports are built on them; we then compare them with ICD-11 in 9.3. The manual requires five criteria to be met together. Two describe the symptoms themselves, and three are conditions without which the diagnosis is incomplete. Figure 9.1 summarizes this rule, and we then explain each criterion with examples.

Criterion A: Persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction

This criterion requires all three of the following forms to be present, either currently or in the child's developmental history as the family reports it. The word “persistent” matters: what is meant is a stable pattern that recurs across different situations and on many days.

Form 1: Deficits in social-emotional reciprocity

That is, difficulty with the natural give-and-take between two people. Social interaction resembles a game of catch: one person throws, the other catches and throws back. An autistic child may catch the ball and not return it, or throw it without checking whether the other person is ready. Examples at school include the following:

  • The child does not initiate conversation with classmates; when he does, it is usually to ask for something he wants, and rarely to share what interests him.
  • He does not share his interest with others: he does not show the teacher his drawing to enjoy her praise, or point to an airplane in the sky to draw the attention of those around him. Specialists call this joint attention (Joint attention).
  • In middle school, for example, he talks at length about a favorite topic without noticing that his classmate has lost interest.

Form 2: Deficits in nonverbal communicative behaviors

That is, in the body language we use without words to convey what we mean:

  • Unusual eye contact: very little, an uncomfortable stare, or gaze that is not coordinated with speech.
  • Limited use of gestures, such as pointing with a finger, nodding to agree, or waving goodbye.
  • Facial expressions that do not fit the situation, or difficulty understanding other people's facial expressions and tone of voice.

Form 3: Deficits in developing, maintaining, and understanding relationships

  • Difficulty with imaginative play with peers, such as playing “house” or “restaurant.”
  • Difficulty adjusting behavior to the situation: behaving in class as on the playground, or addressing the principal as he would a classmate.
  • Little interest in peers, or a wish for friendship without knowing how friendships are built and kept.
Why all three forms?

Because each form on its own may appear for other reasons. A shy child may avoid eye contact, a child with delayed language may not initiate conversation, and a child who has just moved to a new school may have no friends yet. It is the combination of all three, persisting across different situations, that points to a deeper pattern.

This criterion requires at least two of the following four forms, currently or in the developmental history:

In other words, what the teacher saw in a single morning falls under two forms of Criterion B. Yet this alone is not enough: we still need Criterion A in full, along with the three remaining conditions.

Criterion C: Timing

Symptoms must be present in the early developmental period. The manual acknowledges, however, that they may not become fully apparent until social demands exceed the child's capacities. Some children, particularly those with good language and intellectual abilities, do not reveal their difficulties until middle school, when peer relationships grow more complex and their rules less explicit. This is why the specialist always asks the family about the early years, even when the student arrives for evaluation at age 12.

Criterion D: Functional impact

Symptoms must cause clinically significant impairment in social, academic, occupational, or other areas of functioning. Having some traits that do not interfere with the child's life is not enough for a diagnosis. Many people have deep interests or prefer routine and lead successful lives, and this is not considered a disorder.

Criterion E: Exclusion

The symptoms must not be better explained by intellectual disability or global developmental delay. To understand this criterion, remember that in a child with intellectual disability all skills develop slowly, social skills included. If the child's social skills are consistent with his overall level of intellectual development, intellectual disability alone explains them. If, however, his social skills are much weaker than his developmental level would predict, he may have both autism and intellectual disability, and the diagnosis records both.

An example illustrating Criterion E

Two 7-year-olds, each with intellectual abilities equivalent to those of a 4-year-old. The first plays with younger children, laughs along with them, and brings his toy to his mother to show it to her; his interaction, in other words, resembles that of a 4-year-old. This is explained by intellectual disability. The second does not initiate interaction even with younger children and shares his interests with no one, which is less than 4-year-olds typically do. Here, the presence of autism alongside intellectual disability is considered.

9.3Support levels, specifiers, and comparison with ICD-11

How much support does the student need?

After diagnosis, the specialist specifies the severity level for each domain separately: social communication, and restricted, repetitive behaviors. The yardstick is how much support the student needs in daily life, regardless of the number of symptoms. The manual calls these “severity levels,” but defines each level by the amount of support required, which is why they are commonly referred to as “support levels.” We use that term in this chapter to distinguish them from the severity levels of intellectual disability, which are determined by adaptive behavior.

Level 2: Requiring substantial supportMarked difficulties even with support in placeA student who speaks in simple sentences, whose interaction is limited to his own special interests, and whose repetitive behaviors occur often enough for anyone to notice.
Level 3: Requiring very substantial supportSevere deficits affecting most areas of lifeA student who speaks very little or not at all, rarely initiates interaction, and for whom a change in routine causes intense distress that is hard to soothe.

A student may be at Level 1 in social communication and at Level 2 in restricted behaviors, so the report states both levels. The level may also change with development and intervention: it describes the current situation and is open to revision.

Accompanying specifiers

The specialist adds to the diagnosis whatever accompanies it, because this changes the student's educational picture considerably. The report states, for example, whether the autism is:

  • With or without accompanying intellectual impairment.
  • With or without accompanying language impairment.
  • Associated with a known medical or genetic condition or environmental factor.
  • Associated with another neurodevelopmental, mental, or behavioral disorder, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or anxiety.
  • With catatonia, meaning motor freezing, holding fixed postures, extreme slowing, or purposeless movements. It is uncommon, but may emerge in adolescence as a sudden decline in movement, speech, and self-care. It calls for urgent medical referral and should not be treated as ordinary “autistic behavior.”

Two students diagnosed with autism, one with accompanying intellectual and language impairment and the other with neither, need entirely different educational plans, even though the name of the diagnosis is the same. Section 9.10 returns to the common co-occurring conditions and how they are assessed.

Comparison with ICD-11

ICD-11 agrees with DSM-5-TR on the two symptom domains (social communication, and restricted and repetitive behavior, including sensory responses) and on onset in the developmental period, but differs from it in ways that affect the report (WHO, 2024):

  • Two specifiers instead of support levels: the disorder is classified using two specifiers: the presence or absence of a disorder of intellectual development, and the degree of impairment in functional language, that is, the child's ability to use language for his needs and for communication: intact or mildly impaired, markedly impaired, or virtually absent. Sara in 9.8 would thus be classified as “without disorder of intellectual development, with impairment of functional language.”
  • Loss of skills: the classification records whether the child has lost language or social skills he had previously acquired.
  • Descriptions, not checklists: ICD-11 sets out essential requirements and clinical descriptions, and does not require a fixed number of forms in each domain as DSM-5-TR does.
For the teacher

A medical diagnosis under the DSM is one thing; eligibility for special education services is another. A student may hold a medical diagnosis while the decision about educational services still rests on the disorder's impact on his learning and school performance. Your role is central in both, because you see the student in an environment the physician never sees: among his peers, in transitions between lessons, and in unexpected situations.

9.4Early red flags

The earlier autism is identified, the earlier intervention begins and the greater the chances of improvement. Since the late 1990s, medical guidelines have specified signs that, when present, call for evaluation without delay (Filipek et al., 1999), and the current guidelines of the American Academy of Pediatrics have reaffirmed them. These guidelines also recommend screening all children with an autism-specific instrument at the 18- and 24-month visits, without waiting for signs to appear, alongside developmental surveillance at every visit (Hyman et al., 2020). The presence of these signs means that waiting is no longer an appropriate option; the diagnosis itself, however, still depends on a comprehensive evaluation.

Why is autism identified later in girls?

Girls are diagnosed with autism far less often than boys. A large systematic review estimates the true ratio at about three boys to every girl, lower than the ratios found in diagnostic records (Loomes et al., 2017). Researchers believe that part of the gap arises because many girls learn to camouflage their traits (Camouflaging): they watch their female classmates, imitate the way they talk and move, and memorize ready-made phrases for social situations (Hull et al., 2017). Their intense interests may also center on topics that attract little attention, such as animals or novels, and so do not seem “odd” in the way a boy's interest in train timetables might.

As a result, a girl may appear well adjusted at school and then collapse from exhaustion at home. So when a mother says that her daughter “gets very tired after school and needs to be alone for hours,” this is information worth heeding.

9.5The decision pathway: From observation to judgment

The student passes through five stages before a decision is made, and each stage has its own question and its own instruments. The central idea of this pathway is that its stages are completed in order, so that the judgment is made only after each stage has answered its question. Hearing and vision testing comes at the second stage as a prerequisite for any instrument, because any autism measure administered to a child who does not hear well yields a doubtful result, whatever that result may be.

Stage 1: Initial indicators

The story usually begins with an observation from the family or a teacher. Here lies the teacher's most important role, because the teacher sees the student every day among peers, in situations the physician never sees in the clinic. An observation helps the team, however, only if it is documented and objective. Compare these two observations about Yousef:

An observation that does not help the team
“Yousef is odd and doesn’t like his classmates, and I think he has autism.”
An observation the team can build on
“Over two weeks, Yousef did not once initiate conversation with a classmate. I called his name from two meters away ten times, and he did not turn on seven of them, yet he turned immediately at the sound of a cookie box being opened. He lined up the toy cars in a straight row on 8 of 10 free-play days.”

The first observation is a personal judgment; it even contains a diagnosis, which lies outside the teacher's remit. The second describes a specific behavior, in a specific context, with the number of times it occurred. It also draws attention to something important: Yousef hears the biscuit packet, so his failure to respond to his name is probably not due to hearing loss, although a hearing test remains necessary. Stage 2: Hearing and vision testing

Before any autism instrument is used, the question is: Does the child hear and see well? Not turning to his name, delayed speech, and limited interaction in noisy settings can all arise from hearing loss alone. A child who is administered an observation schedule while unable to hear the specialist clearly may appear less interactive than he really is, and the result loses its validity. Passing a newborn hearing screening is not enough, since some types of hearing loss appear later or fluctuate with middle ear infections. The team therefore requests a recent, age-appropriate hearing assessment with tympanometry, together with a vision test.

The kindergarten referred Yousef for a hearing test first. His hearing proved normal in both ears, his tympanometry was normal, and his vision test was clear. Only then did screening become meaningful.

Stage 3: Screening

Screening is a sorting tool; diagnosis is a later stage that requires broader evidence. The function of screening is to determine who needs a more in-depth evaluation, so that not every quiet child is referred and no child who genuinely needs evaluation is overlooked.

An analogy that clarifies the difference

Screening is like the metal detector at an airport. If it beeps, the traveler needs a hand search to settle the matter. A high screening score works the same way: it means “look more closely,” not “he has autism.”

The accuracy of a screening tool is measured by two concepts: sensitivity (Sensitivity), the proportion of those who actually have the disorder whom the tool detects, and specificity (Specificity), the proportion of those without the disorder whom the tool correctly rules out. Chapter 2 (Section 2.6, Table 2.5) presented a worked example showing that a tool with 85% sensitivity and 95% specificity, applied to 1,000 children among whom the disorder has a prevalence of 2%, yields positive results of which roughly three quarters are false. The lesson is that the value of a positive result depends as much on the proportion of those screened who have the disorder as on the tool itself.

Real-world data tell the same story. In the validation study of the M-CHAT-R/F, administered to more than 16,000 children at primary care visits, fewer than half of those who still screened positive after the Follow-Up interview were diagnosed with autism. Most of the rest, however, had developmental delays or other concerns warranting intervention, so their results were not “false alarms” in the full sense (Robins et al., 2014):

Yousef is 5 and thus past the age range of the M-CHAT-R/F. His mother therefore completed the Lifetime form of the Social Communication Questionnaire (see its profile in 9.6). He scored 21, above the cut-off score of 15, and was referred to a diagnostic center together with his teacher's record.

Stage 4: Comprehensive evaluation

This stage is carried out by a multidisciplinary team that may include a pediatrician or psychiatrist, a psychologist, a speech-language pathologist, and a special education specialist. The team gathers evidence from three independent sources:

  1. Developmental history from the family, through a structured interview such as the Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised, ADI-R: When did the symptoms begin? What was the child like at age four?
  2. Standardized direct observation of the child, using an instrument such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, ADOS-2: How does he interact now with an unfamiliar person in situations designed to elicit social behavior?
  3. Supplementary assessments that rule out other explanations and reveal co-occurring conditions: an intelligence test to establish intellectual ability, a language assessment, and a medical evaluation that asks about seizures, sleep, feeding, and gastrointestinal problems. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends offering genetic testing to every child diagnosed with autism, beginning with chromosomal microarray and testing for fragile X syndrome (Hyman et al., 2020).

Information from the school is added to these: the teacher's ratings on the rating scale, her observation records, and samples of the student's work.

In Yousef's case, the specialist interviewed his mother using the diagnostic interview, and his scores exceeded the cut-off scores in all three domains; his mother reported that he had not pointed or waved before the age of two. He was administered Module 1 of the observation schedule, because he does not use spontaneous phrases and most of his speech repeats what he has heard; the classification was “autism.” His nonverbal ability was 88, in the average range.

Stage 5: Matching against the criteria

The team lays out all the evidence and asks about each criterion in turn: Is it met? What is the evidence for it? From which source did that evidence come? If the sources converge on all five criteria being met, the diagnosis is made, with the support level specified. If the sources conflict, or if a better explanation such as hearing loss emerges, the judgment is suspended and that explanation is investigated.

Yousef's evidence converged: the teacher's record, his mother's report, and direct observation. He was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder without accompanying intellectual impairment, with accompanying language impairment, at support Level 2 in social communication and Level 1 in restricted behaviors, and he was referred for genetic evaluation. In this way the first morning observation, once documented and followed by hearing testing, screening, and evaluation, became a decision grounded in evidence.

A golden rule

A decision not to diagnose may be the most accurate outcome of an evaluation. A student misdiagnosed with autism loses the services he actually needs, and his family bears the burden of a diagnosis that does not fit him.

A caution on test security and Arabic versions

This book describes instruments and explains how to administer and interpret them, but it does not publish their items or reproduce their record forms. The items are the property of the publishers, and publishing them undermines an instrument's validity when examinees see them in advance. The drawings representing forms in this chapter illustrate only the layout of the page; their gray lines do not represent real items.

Many instruments also circulate in unlicensed translations without local norms. Each profile therefore states the status of the Arabic version, and readers should verify a version's license and norms before relying on it.

M-CHAT-R/FModified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised, with Follow-UpScreening tool

The idea

This checklist was designed to be administered to all children at routine health care visits, including those who raise no concern at all. The aim is to identify children whom the family may have missed, at the age when intervention is most beneficial (Robins et al., 2014).

What it looks like

A single page of 20 simple questions that parents answer yes or no. They ask about everyday behaviors every parent knows, such as pointing with a finger, looking at what others point to, and pretend play. The checklist comes with a Follow-Up interview guide that the professional uses for the questions whose answers indicated risk, asking the family for real-life examples before scoring the answer.

A scene from administration

At a primary care center, the nurse hands the checklist to the mother of Raghad, a 20-month-old, to complete while they wait for the doctor. She finishes it in five minutes. The doctor tallies the answers that signal risk and counts four, so he sits down with the mother and goes through each one in detail: “When you said she doesn't point, does she point if she wants juice from the shelf? Does she point to a cat in the street to show it to you?” After the follow-up, three at-risk answers remain, and the doctor refers Raghad for a diagnostic evaluation.

SCQSocial Communication QuestionnaireScreening tool

The idea

The M-CHAT-R/F stops at 30 months. What, then, of a five-year-old, or a ten-year-old student whose teacher is worried? This questionnaire was designed to carry the questions of the diagnostic interview with the family, in condensed form, onto a form a parent can complete in minutes, sorting out who needs a comprehensive evaluation (Rutter, Bailey, et al., 2003).

What it looks like

Forty yes/no questions in two forms. The Lifetime form asks about the child's entire history, and some items ask specifically about ages four to five; this is the form used for screening and referral. The Current form asks about the past three months and is useful for monitoring. The first question establishes whether the child speaks in phrases, so the language items are dropped for a child who does not.

Its limits

The scale reflects what the respondent perceives of the behavior. A teacher with thirty students may notice different things from a mother, and an anxious family may exaggerate or play things down. A disagreement between the two ratings is therefore important information in its own right, and the scale alone is not enough for diagnosis. Independent studies have also clearly criticized the diagnostic accuracy of this scale series, particularly its tendency to underestimate the probability of autism in children who have been diagnosed (South et al., 2002). The high internal consistency of the Arabic version does not answer this question: consistency shows that the items measure a single thing, whereas accuracy in separating those with autism from those with look-alike conditions requires diagnostic validity studies. For the teacher: How do you complete a rating scale honestly?
  1. Read each statement and bring to mind actual situations you have seen for yourself.
  2. Rate what you have personally observed during the period the manual specifies.
  3. If you have had no opportunity to observe a particular behavior, tell the specialist rather than guessing.
  4. Do not try to “help” the student qualify for services by inflating the rating, or to “protect” him from a diagnosis by lowering it. An honest rating is what serves him.

9.8When is the judgment made? Three cases

Having presented the criteria and the instruments, we are left with the hardest question: how does all of this come together in a single decision? In this section we follow three cases from the moment of referral to the decision. In the first, the judgment is reached; in the second, it is not; and in the third, autism nearly escapes notice because the student's giftedness masks it.

All three cases are hypothetical, written for teaching purposes; their figures are illustrative and do not represent real children.

Case one: Sara, 4 years 6 months

When the evidence converges

The story: Sara started kindergarten at the beginning of the year. Two months in, her teacher noticed that she spent free playtime alone, lining up colored blocks in the same order every day. When another girl tried to join her, Sara pushed her away and screamed. The teacher also noticed that she cried hard whenever the order of activities changed, and that she covered her ears whenever songs were played.

What the teacher did: Rather than relying on a general impression, she kept dated notes for two weeks in a log similar to Table 9.3. She then spoke with the mother, who said that Sara had been late to talk and began using short phrases only after age three; today she uses spontaneous three-word phrases consistently, though her speech is not fluent. The mother added that Sara pulls her mother's hand toward what she wants instead of pointing at it. The teacher passed her notes to the kindergarten administration, and Sara was referred first for hearing and vision screening and then to a diagnostic center.

Case two: Fahd, 7 years

When there is a better explanation

The story: Fahd's second-grade teacher complains that Fahd “lives in his own world.” He sometimes does not turn when called, rarely talks with classmates, and sits alone at recess. The teacher had attended a training course on autism and began to see its signs in Fahd; he completed the Gilliam scale for him, and the probability of autism came out high. But the teacher had also noticed something that puzzled him: in some weeks Fahd responded well, and in others it was “as if he can't hear anyone.” He took this to mean that Fahd “chooses when to engage.”

What the team did first: Before any autism instrument, the team began with the second step of the pathway: hearing and vision screening. Fahd failed the school hearing screening in both ears and was referred to an audiologist.

The team will revisit his social functioning if difficulties persist once his hearing has stabilized.

The lesson

Had the team relied on the rating scale alone, Fahd would have been misdiagnosed and denied the services he actually needs. Had the observation schedule been administered before the hearing test, its result would have been open to doubt, whatever it showed. Sequence is part of the evidence: the senses first, then the autism instruments. Disagreement among sources and week-to-week fluctuation are two signals that call for stopping and investigating.

What evidence could change the decision? If his difficulties with social initiation and shared interest persisted for months after his hearing had stabilized, or if restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior that had not been evident emerged during follow-up, the assessment would need to be completed with the diagnostic interview and the observation schedule.

Case three: Layan, 12 years

When giftedness masks autism

The story: Layan is one of the strongest sixth graders in mathematics and science, and she was nominated for the gifted program. Her program teacher, however, noticed something striking: Layan solves problems expertly on her own but falls apart in group projects. She insists on doing things her way and cannot understand why her classmates get upset when she corrects their mistakes in front of everyone. At every opportunity she talks about astronomy in endless detail, without noticing that those around her have grown bored. Her mother says she comes home from school exhausted and spends hours alone in her room.

Why was she identified so late? Because her academic excellence led everyone to explain her behavior away as “the quirkiness of high achievers.” Her excellent language covered her difficulties in social communication. She had also memorized polite phrases and used them in the right places, so she appeared well adjusted, as we explained in our discussion of girls in Section 9.4. Her difficulties became clearly visible when relationships among classmates grew more complex at this stage, which is exactly what Criterion C means when it states that symptoms may not become apparent until social demands exceed the child’s capacities.

  • Module 3 of the ADOS-2 was administered; it includes a conversation about friendship and emotions, and it was there that her difficulties clearly emerged.

9.10Co-occurrence and medical evaluation

Differential diagnosis asks: which of the two conditions? Co-occurrence, by contrast, asks: is there another condition alongside autism? In autism, co-occurrence is the rule rather than the exception. In the 2020 U.S. surveillance data, 37.9% of autistic children with available ability data had intellectual disability (Maenner et al., 2023). A meta-analysis of published studies estimated the prevalence of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder among autistic people at about 28%, anxiety disorders at about 20%, sleep disorders at about 13%, and depression at about 11%, with wide variation across studies depending on sample and age (Lai et al., 2019). Since its fifth edition, the diagnostic manual no longer prohibits diagnosing autism and ADHD together.

Medical and genetic evaluation is part of the comprehensive assessment, not an appendix to it. Current guidelines recommend offering genetic testing to every child diagnosed with autism, beginning with chromosomal microarray and fragile X testing, and asking about seizures, sleep, feeding and the gastrointestinal system (Hyman et al., 2020). Genetic referral becomes all the more important in communities where consanguineous marriage is common, Saudi society among them; its rate reached 56% in a national sample of mothers (El Mouzan et al., 2008). The working rule is that after every autism diagnosis, the team asks two questions: Is there anything in the file that autism alone does not explain? And has that area been measured with a tool suited to the student’s developmental level?

Three observations follow from this comparison. The first is that the older Regulations’ definition restricts onset to before 36 months, whereas the current diagnostic manual acknowledges that symptoms may not become apparent until social demands exceed the child’s capacities. A student like Layan (9.8) could be excluded if the older text were read literally, even though her early indicators surfaced in the interview. The second is that treating silence as consent after fourteen days differs from the U.S. requirement of explicit consent, and it places an ethical burden on the school to make sure the family has actually understood the notice. Conclusion

The argument we have developed in this chapter centers on the idea that a judgment of autism spectrum disorder is reached when evidence from developmental history, direct observation, and information from home and school converges to show that all five criteria are met, which makes it broader than anything a single scale can say. It follows that hearing and vision testing comes before any instrument, that a screening result is treated as an invitation to comprehensive assessment, that disagreement between sources is taken as a signal to look for another explanation, and that medical and genetic evaluation forms part of the comprehensive assessment, because co-occurrence is so common. The teacher plays a pivotal role in all of this: documented, objective observation; honest completion of rating scales; and an informed reading of the diagnostic report.

Questions about the numbers and the instruments nonetheless remain open. Prevalence figures vary with the way children are sought out, so they should not be read without asking how those children were reached, and most screening and diagnostic instruments lack published Arabic norms. Caution remains essential with those who tend to be identified late, such as gifted students and girls who mask their traits: giftedness can conceal autism, and autism does not cancel out giftedness.

9.12Summary, key terms and activities

Chapter summary

  • Autism spectrum disorder is diagnosed when all five DSM-5-TR criteria are met: the three forms of social communication deficit, at least two of the four forms of restricted behavior, early onset, clear impact, and the absence of a better explanation by intellectual disability. ICD-11 classifies the disorder by the presence of disorder of intellectual development and the degree of impairment in functional language, rather than by support levels.
  • Prevalence figures vary with the way children are sought out, so the first question to ask of any figure is: how were these children found?
  • Red flags call for evaluation without delay; the loss of any acquired skill calls for it at any age; and all children are screened at 18 and 24 months.
  • Hearing and vision testing precedes any autism instrument, because an instrument’s result for a child who does not hear well is open to doubt whatever it turns out to be.
  • Screening tools sort but do not diagnose; rating scales convey what is seen by those who know the child; and diagnostic instruments are administered by a trained specialist. Most of these instruments lack published Arabic norms.
  • No single scale is sufficient for reaching a judgment. The judgment is made when evidence from developmental history, direct observation, and information from home and school converges.
  • Disagreement between sources is a signal to look for another explanation, such as hearing loss or a language disorder; co-occurrence is very common; and medical and genetic evaluation is part of the comprehensive assessment.
  • Giftedness can mask autism, many girls camouflage their traits, and autism does not cancel out giftedness.
  • The teacher’s role is pivotal: documented, objective observation; honest completion of rating scales; and an informed reading of the diagnostic report.

Glossary of terms

Joint attention
When a child directs another person’s attention to something, or follows that person’s attention to it, so that the two share an interest in it, as when a child points to an airplane and looks at his mother.
Echolalia
Repeating words or phrases the child has heard from others, either immediately or after a delay, often in the same tone of voice.
Screening
A quick procedure applied to a large group to determine who needs a more in-depth assessment; it does not yield a diagnosis.
Rating scale
An instrument on which someone who knows the child rates the extent to which specific behaviors appear, using a graded scale.
Cut-off score
The score that separates two ranges in interpreting a scale, such as a score of 30 on the Standard Version of the CARS-2.
Scaled score
A standard score with a mean of 10 and a standard deviation of 3, used on subtests to compare a student’s performance with the norming sample.
Sensitivity
The proportion of those who actually have the disorder whom the instrument correctly identifies.
Specificity
The proportion of those who do not have the disorder whom the instrument correctly rules out.
Positive predictive value
The probability that the disorder is actually present in someone with a positive result; it depends on the prevalence of the disorder in the group being tested.
Differential diagnosis
Establishing that the symptoms are not better explained by another condition.
Co-occurrence
The presence of another disorder alongside autism at the same time, such as intellectual disability, ADHD or anxiety; each is diagnosed and planned for.
Functional language
The child’s ability to use language for everyday needs and communication; it is one of the two specifiers for classification in ICD-11.
Catatonia
Freezing in movement, holding fixed postures, extreme slowness, or purposeless movements; it may accompany autism and calls for urgent medical referral.
Camouflaging
A person’s attempt to hide their traits by imitating others and memorizing ready-made phrases for social situations; it is more common among females.
Twice-exceptional
A gifted student who at the same time has a disability or disorder, such as autism or a learning disability.
