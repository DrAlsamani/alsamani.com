Two scenes in a single day

In second grade, Faisal cannot stay in his seat for more than a few minutes. He blurts out answers before the teacher has finished the question, takes his classmates' pens without asking, and forgets his notebook almost every day. His teacher says, “He never settles down.”

In sixth grade, Haya causes no trouble at all. She sits quietly, yet she misses half of what is said, leaves her assignments unfinished, and her grades have been slipping since the start of the year. Her teacher says, “Quiet and polite, but she drifts off a lot.”

Which of the two students is in greater need of assessment? Perhaps both. Faisal, however, is the more likely to be referred, because his behavior disrupts the class, while Haya may be left waiting for a long time. And what each of them is going through may stem from something quite different from what appears on the surface. We return to them in the first and second cases, and then turn to two more: a first grader and a girl in middle school.

Introduction

Restlessness, distraction, anger and sadness are a familiar part of every child's life, and the line between what is expected at a given age and what rises to the level of a disorder is not always clear. This is why hasty judgments are so common in these categories, and in two opposite directions: an active child, or one younger than his classmates, is misdiagnosed because his behavior draws attention, while a quiet girl who is struggling goes unnoticed because she causes no disruption, much as the two scenes at the opening of the chapter illustrate. The matter is complicated further by the fact that the clinical categories of the medical manuals do not map onto the educational category, and that clinical diagnosis and eligibility decisions rest with two bodies that hold different authority.

We begin the chapter with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (Section 10.1), then present its diagnostic criteria, presentations and specifiers (Section 10.2), and ask who is misdiagnosed and who is overlooked (Section 10.3). We then take up the clinical pictures of behavioral and emotional disorders (Section 10.4), the educational category of emotional disturbance (Section 10.5), the signs that warrant attention (Section 10.6), the decision pathway and universal behavior screening (Section 10.7), and instrument profiles (Section 10.8). We then apply all of this to four cases (Section 10.9), set out differential diagnosis and co-occurrence (Section 10.10), and close with the Saudi and Arab context (Section 10.11).

10.1Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder, ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity-impulsivity, or both, to a degree that is inconsistent with the child's developmental level and that interferes with functioning in more than one setting.

A large worldwide review put its prevalence among children and adolescents at about 5% (Polanczyk et al., 2007). A later meta-analysis, pooling studies from many countries, estimated the prevalence of mental disorders overall in these age groups at about 13.4%: anxiety disorders at about 6.5%, disruptive behavior disorders at about 5.7%, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder at about 3.4%, and depressive disorders at about 2.6% (Polanczyk et al., 2015). In Saudi Arabia, a meta-analysis of fourteen Saudi studies arrived at a pooled prevalence for the disorder of 12.4%, with a wide confidence interval running from 5.4% to 26% (Aljadani et al., 2023). The width of this interval means that the pooled studies differ considerably from one another. The figure should therefore be read as a rough general estimate, and the gap between it and the global estimates does not justify concluding that the disorder is more prevalent in Saudi Arabia, at least not before differences in samples, instruments and criteria across the studies have been taken into account.

To understand this disorder, it helps to remember that attention is a set of related abilities: focusing on one thing while ignoring others, sustaining that focus long enough, shifting between tasks, and holding back the first response long enough to think. These abilities are tied to what are called executive functions (Executive functions), the capacities that regulate behavior and direct it toward a goal: planning, organization, working memory and self-control.

Common misconceptions
  • “If he can focus on video games for hours, he doesn't have an attention problem.” The problem is not the ability to focus as such, but directing it where it is needed. Fast-paced, stimulating activities capture attention automatically; it is the dull task that demands effort that proves difficult. The DSM states that signs may be minimal or absent when the child is receiving frequent rewards for appropriate behavior, is under close supervision, is in a novel setting, or is engaged in an especially interesting activity. The disappearance of the behavior during a favorite activity therefore does not rule out the disorder.
  • “It's caused by bad parenting.” This is a neurodevelopmental disorder with a strong genetic component. How the child is handled at home affects the severity of the accompanying problems, but it does not cause the disorder.
  • “Girls don't get it.” Boys are diagnosed more often, but the real difference is smaller than it appears, because girls more often show the quiet, inattentive presentation that does not attract notice.

Note criterion (C) in particular: several symptoms must be present in at least two settings. A child who is very active in only one class, or with only one teacher, calls for questions about that class or that teacher before questions about the child. This is why diagnosis draws on information from both the family and the school. The criterion does not, however, require the full symptom count in every setting: six symptoms at school and three at home may satisfy it. The question for the second setting is whether several genuine symptoms appear there and whether they have an impact; reaching six is not required.

Comparison with ICD-11

The International Classification of Diseases, eleventh revision (ICD-11), describes the disorder by the same core features: a persistent pattern of inattention or hyperactivity-impulsivity, lasting at least six months, emerging during the developmental period, exceeding what is expected for age and level of intellectual functioning, appearing in more than one situation, and interfering with functioning. It also has presentations that parallel those of the DSM. The clearest difference is that it describes the manifestations and leaves the judgment of their sufficiency to the clinician, without a fixed number of symptoms. Among the disruptive behavior categories, the ICD-11 has no separate category corresponding to “disruptive mood dysregulation disorder” (10.4); instead, it adds to oppositional defiant disorder a specifier, “with chronic irritability-anger.” It names conduct disorder “conduct-dissocial disorder,” and applies the specifier “with limited prosocial emotions” both to this disorder and to oppositional defiant disorder (World Health Organization, 2024). A teacher may thus read two reports on the same student that use two different labels; the difference lies in the manual used, not in the student.

10.3Who is misdiagnosed and who is overlooked?

The relative age effect in the classroom

A single class may hold children whose oldest and youngest members are separated by almost a full year. At age six, a whole year is a large difference in the ability to sit still, pay attention and exercise self-control. When a teacher compares a child with his classmates, the immaturity of the youngest may look like a disorder. This is known as the relative age effect (Relative age effect).

Layton and colleagues studied more than 400,000 American children in states where children enter kindergarten if they turn five before September 1. They found that children born in August, the youngest in their class, were diagnosed with the disorder at a clearly higher rate than those born in September, the oldest in their class (Layton et al., 2018):

The phenomenon recurs outside the United States. In Canada, Morrow and colleagues followed nearly one million children aged six to twelve in the province of British Columbia, where the cutoff falls at the end of December. Boys born in December, the youngest in their class, were about 30% more likely to be diagnosed than those born in January, and girls born in December were about 70% more likely (Morrow et al., 2012). These are two observational studies: they establish that the likelihood of diagnosis is linked to relative age, but they do not establish that every additional case is a misdiagnosis. They do, however, make it likely that immaturity is sometimes read as disorder.

In Saudi Arabia, a child's age for admission to first grade is calculated on the first day of the school year: the minimum is six years, and a child up to ninety days younger may be admitted. The regulations for 1448 AH required that these younger children have completed a full year of kindergarten (Ministry of Education, 2025 [Arabic]; Sabq, 2026 [Arabic]). A single first-grade class may thus include one child who turns six only months after school begins and another who is nearly seven, a gap of almost a year, which may widen if a child enrolled late or repeated a grade. At the time of writing we found no published Saudi study of the relative age effect in diagnoses of the disorder, but there is no reason to assume that children here are immune to it.

The practical lesson for the teacher: before referring a child for restlessness and inattention, look at his date of birth, and compare him with the classmates closest to him in age rather than with the whole class.

Girls, the quiet presentation and adolescents

Many girls, by contrast, are overlooked, and with them boys with the predominantly inattentive presentation. Teachers tend to refer the students who disrupt the class, and a girl whose mind wanders quietly disturbs no one. Her difficulty may not become clearly visible until middle or high school, when the volume of coursework and the need for self-organization increase; her grades fall, and everyone assumes she has grown careless. A difficulty that surfaces late does not negate criterion (B), provided that several symptoms were present before age twelve, even if no one noticed them. The evaluator therefore extends the inquiry to elementary school records and the observations of her teachers there, alongside the present.

The same holds, even more clearly, for emotional problems. Anxiety and depression increase with the onset of adolescence and show up as absences, physical complaints, falling grades and withdrawal from friends, signs that are easily read as laziness or as “a phase that will pass.” We will see this in the case of Lamya (10.9).

Readers will notice that irritability recurs in more than one row of the table: in oppositional defiant disorder, in disruptive mood dysregulation disorder and in adolescent depression, and it may also accompany anxiety and trauma. Describing a student as “short-tempered” therefore calls for detail: when the student gets angry, how often, what the student is like between outbursts, when this began, and whether it represents a change from before. When the matter cannot wait for assessment

If a student expresses, in speech, writing or drawing, a wish to harm himself or herself or to end his or her life, shows signs of injury, or discloses having been harmed, these are not data to be added to the assessment file. They are a situation that calls for immediate action under school procedures: informing the school counselor and the school principal the same day, not leaving the student alone, and involving the family and the competent authority as the procedures require. Completing the assessment comes afterward.

10.5The educational category: emotional disturbance

The disorders in Table 10.2 are clinical diagnoses. The education system, however, asks a different question: does this student need special education services because of his or her behavior or emotions? For this reason, education systems often group these conditions in a broad educational category with its own definition. Under the US Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), reauthorized in 2004 as the Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act, a student qualifies for services under the category of emotional disturbance (Emotional disturbance) if he or she exhibits one or more of the following five characteristics over a long period of time and to a marked degree, adversely affecting educational performance (Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act, 2004; U.S. Department of Education, 2006, 34 CFR §300.8(c)(4)):

  1. An inability to learn that cannot be explained by intellectual, sensory or health factors.
  2. An inability to build or maintain satisfactory relationships with peers and teachers.
  3. Inappropriate types of behavior or feelings under normal circumstances.
  4. A general pervasive mood of unhappiness or depression.
  5. A tendency to develop physical symptoms or fears associated with personal or school problems.

The definition then adds two sentences that weigh heavily on the decision: that the category includes schizophrenia, and that it does not apply to students who are “socially maladjusted” (Socially maladjusted) unless it is determined that they have an emotional disturbance under the characteristics above, according to the US Code of Federal Regulations (34 CFR §300.8(c)(4)(ii)).

Note the three shared conditions: a long period of time, a marked degree, and an adverse effect on learning. A student who has gone through two difficult weeks after the death of a grandfather is not placed in this category, although he or she may need support. Note also that the educational category does not coincide with clinical diagnosis: a student may be diagnosed with anxiety without its effect on learning reaching the level that qualifies for the category, and a student may qualify for the category on the strength of clear, documented characteristics before any particular clinical diagnosis has been completed.

For the specialist: the debate over excluding “social maladjustment”

US regulations do not define the term “social maladjustment,” so states and school districts have interpreted it in different ways. Some equate it with conduct disorder or delinquency and see in it “willful” behavior, in which the student knows the rules and chooses to break them, as opposed to an “involuntary” disorder that merits services. This distinction has been widely criticized in the behavioral assessment literature (Merrell, 2008), for reasons that include the following:

  • No measurable criterion: no standardized instrument separates “maladjustment” from “emotional disturbance,” so the decision comes to rest on the team's judgment.
  • Actual overlap: many students with externalizing problems also have anxiety, depression, the effects of trauma or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, so separating the two kinds within a single student is often artificial.
  • The impact of exclusion: the students most at risk of dropping out and of school discipline may be denied services, even though their need for structured support is plainer.
  • Risk of bias: in the absence of a criterion, the label “maladjusted” may be shaped more by the student's background and reputation than by the evidence (see Chapter 14).

Defenders of the exclusion argue, on the other hand, that the category was created for students whose emotional disturbance impedes their learning, and that folding every behavioral infraction into it would empty it of meaning. The practical position this book adopts is that the exclusion should be applied only after a comprehensive assessment that looks explicitly for the five characteristics, and that the evidence on which it rests should be stated in the report.

Saudi regulations, for their part, treat “behavioral and emotional disorders” as a distinct category of special education, alongside “attention deficit and hyperactivity disorders” (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). Section 10.11 sets out what we found in these texts and what we did not.

10.7The decision pathway

Universal behavior screening

Schools usually wait for a student to catch the teacher's eye, which means that a student who disturbs no one may never be seen at all. For this reason, schools that operate a multi-tiered system of supports are turning to universal behavior screening (Universal behavior screening): the teacher rates every student in the class, referred or not, with a brief tool two or three times a year, as is done for reading. These systems are useful only under two conditions. First, they must be preceded by whatever notification of families school procedures require, and their results must be kept confidential. Second, the school must have something to offer the students the screening identifies, such as support groups for social skills or anxiety management, and follow-up with the school counselor. Screening that identifies students and then leaves them without support defeats its own purpose. The screening result determines who needs a closer look; the decision rests on the assessment that follows. At the time of writing, we know of no published standardized Arabic version of any of these three systems.

For the teacher: the limits of your role

You are a key source of information about the school setting, and no assessment is complete without your ratings. But do not tell a family that their son “has hyperactivity” or that their daughter “is depressed,” and do not suggest medication. Diagnosis and treatment fall within the authority of the licensed health professional, and the decision on eligibility for services within that of the school's assessment team. Describe what you see, provide support in the classroom, and refer according to procedures.

For the specialist: Why do informants disagree?

A well-known meta-analysis pooled the results of dozens of studies on informant agreement about children’s behavioral and emotional problems. The mean correlation was about 0.60 between informants who see the child in the same setting, such as two parents; about 0.28 between informants who see the child in different settings, such as a parent and a teacher; and about 0.22 between children’s self-ratings and the ratings of others (Achenbach et al., 1987). A later, extensive review concluded that this discrepancy is information in its own right, and that averaging it away squanders that information: it tells us where the behavior appears and with whom, and it reveals problems that only the child can see (De Los Reyes et al., 2015). The practical rule is this: for externalizing problems, teachers and parents are strong sources; for internalizing problems, especially in adolescents, the student’s self-report is essential, because anxiety and sadness may go unnoticed by anyone else.

SDQStrengths and Difficulties QuestionnaireBroadband screening

The idea

A brief tool completed by someone who knows the student. Within minutes it screens for the most prominent behavioral and emotional problems while also measuring a positive domain, which makes it suitable for screening in both schools and clinics (Goodman, 1997).

What it looks like

A single page of 25 statements, divided equally among five subscales: emotional problems, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behavior. For each statement, the respondent chooses one of three options: “Not true,” “Somewhat true,” or “Certainly true.” There are parent and teacher forms, a self-report form from age 11, and an optional supplement asking about the impact of the difficulties on the student’s life.

A scene from administration

At the start of the semester, a sixth-grade teacher completes the questionnaire for a student referred by a colleague who teaches her another subject. It takes five minutes, and she notices that she would never have paid attention to the statements about fears and physical complaints had she not been asked about them. The parent form is sent to the mother with a letter explaining its purpose, and the student completes the self-report form with the school counselor. The counselor then gathers the three forms and compares the subscales across them.

How are the results interpreted?

The subscales are read before the total score, because the same total can result from externalizing problems in one student and internalizing problems in another. The forms are then compared: elevated emotional problems on the student’s own form alone, with low scores on the teacher and parent forms, may mean that what the student is going through is hidden from those around them, rather than that the student is exaggerating. A high prosocial behavior score is a reminder that the student has strengths alongside the difficulties, and it helps in building a support plan on those strengths.

Limitations

It is a screening tool, so a high band signals the need for a more detailed assessment. Five statements per scale are not enough to distinguish specific disorders, such as anxiety from depression. The bands are derived from British norms, so they should be used with caution with Arab students unless local norms are available.

VanderbiltVanderbilt ADHD Diagnostic Rating ScalesRating scale

The idea

Translating the DSM criteria into a form completed by parents and teachers, so that the physician or specialist counts symptoms from two sources rather than relying on a general impression, while also screening for the most prominent co-occurring disorders (Wolraich et al., 2003).

What it looks like

Two forms: one for parents and one for teachers. The first section lists the eighteen symptoms of the disorder in wording close to the DSM, each rated on a four-point scale from “Never” to “Very often.” Screening items follow for oppositional defiance, conduct disorder (on the parent form), and anxiety and depression. The final section contains performance items covering reading, mathematics, writing, relationships and following directions, each rated on a five-point scale from “Excellent” to “Problematic.”

A scene from administration

The pediatric clinic hands Umm Faisal the parent form, along with the teacher form in a sealed envelope to deliver to the school. The teacher completes her form drawing on her written notes from the past two weeks as well as her memory. When both forms come back, the physician counts the items rated “Often” or “Very often” on each, then looks at the performance items, and then at the screening items for co-occurring disorders (see Figure 10.8).

Limitations

Counting symptoms does not rule out other explanations; an anxious or sleep-deprived student may meet the count. Because the scale does not compare the student with age norms, the question of whether symptoms are “inconsistent with developmental level” is left to the specialist’s judgment, which matters for the youngest students in a class. The screening items for co-occurring disorders are few, so they serve only as a flag.

The program records omission errors, that is, failing to press when the target appears, which are associated with inattention; commission errors, that is, pressing when a nontarget appears, which are associated with impulsivity; and the variability of reaction time. Limitations: Why are they not enough on their own?

These tests appear objective because they are computerized, but systematic reviews have found that they do not discriminate accurately enough between children with and without the disorder, and that they do not reflect the child’s behavior in the classroom (Hall et al., 2016). A child may perform well during a quiet quarter of an hour with an examiner yet struggle through a full school day. For this reason, they are used as a supplement to rating scales and observation.

Co-occurring condition

The conditions in Table 10.14 are not always alternatives; many of them co-occur in the same student. Oppositional defiance frequently accompanies ADHD, anxiety and depression often occur together in adolescents, and published reviews report that a substantial proportion of students with learning disabilities also have ADHD, with estimates varying according to samples and criteria (DuPaul et al., 2013). Knowing that two disorders can co-occur is only a first step; each combination then calls for an assessment procedure that keeps one condition from masking or inflating the other:

  • With learning disabilities: Achievement is measured with standardized tools in every ADHD assessment, and the team checks whether inattention appears across all subjects or only in the area of weakness. If treatment for attention begins, academic skills are measured again afterward.
  • With oppositional defiance: Ask about symptoms in situations that involve no demands and no authority, such as free play or watching a program; inattention that appears there cannot be explained by defiance.
  • With anxiety and depression: Establish when each began, and ask the student directly using a measure of internalizing problems, because poor concentration is a shared symptom that will be counted twice unless care is taken.
  • With autism spectrum disorder: Read the attention and impulsivity items on rating scales with caution, because some may be elevated for reasons related to autism itself, such as not responding to one’s name or absorption in a special interest; rely on observation across varied situations (see Chapter 9).
  • With giftedness: Compare behavior in an ordinary lesson with a lesson that offers genuine challenge, gather ratings from two settings, and ask about developmental history before school (see Chapter 13).

Precise co-occurrence rates vary widely across studies depending on the source of the sample (clinical or community), the instrument and the criteria. For that reason we do not present them here as definitive figures, and we offer instead a practical rule: whenever one of these disorders is diagnosed, ask about the others.

Conclusion

The central argument we have sought to set out in this chapter is that judgment in these categories goes beyond the mere presence of a behavior: it turns on the number of symptoms, when they first appeared, the range of settings in which they show themselves and their effect on functioning, and on the absence of a more likely explanation. It follows that assessment begins with sensory checks and universal screening, rests on rating scales from multiple informants, compares the student with the peers closest in age, treats the student’s own report as essential for internalizing problems, and keeps computerized tests in a merely supplementary role. It also follows that the clinical diagnosis, made by a licensed health professional, must be kept distinct from the eligibility decision, made by the assessment team.

Some of the chapter’s questions, however, remain contested or unsettled. The exclusion of social maladjustment alone from the emotional disturbance category in US law is a matter of debate; the ICD-11 does not require a set number of symptoms as the DSM does; and Saudi regulations treat emotional disturbance as a separate category without published detailed criteria, leaving it to the team to state the criteria on which it based its judgment.

Chapter summary

  • Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is diagnosed on the basis of at least six symptoms in one or both lists, several of which appeared before age 12; several symptoms are present in two settings, they interfere with functioning, and no other disorder better explains them. The diagnosis specifies the presentation and the severity, and the ICD-11 does not require a set number of symptoms.
  • A learning disability is not among the exclusionary criteria, and autism has not been an exclusion since the fifth edition of the DSM.
  • The youngest students in a class are more likely to be misdiagnosed, according to studies from more than one country, so the student should be compared with the peers closest in age. The quiet presentation and internalizing problems are often overlooked, particularly in girls and adolescents.
  • Oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, anxiety, depression and posttraumatic stress disorder each have distinct criteria, and in children and adolescents they take particular forms, such as irritable rather than sad mood in depression.
  • The emotional disturbance category in US law requires a long period of time, a marked degree and an educational impact; it includes schizophrenia and excludes social maladjustment alone, an exclusion that remains a matter of debate. Saudi regulations treat the category as separate, without published detailed criteria.
  • Assessment begins with sensory checks and universal screening and rests on rating scales from multiple informants; the student’s own report is essential for internalizing problems. Computerized tests serve only as a supplement.
  • Clinical diagnosis falls within the remit of a licensed health professional, and the eligibility decision within that of the assessment team.

Glossary of terms

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)
A neurodevelopmental disorder marked by a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity-impulsivity, or both.
Executive functions
Capacities that direct behavior toward a goal, such as planning, working memory and self-control.
Presentation
The current picture of symptoms: predominantly inattentive presentation, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive presentation, or combined presentation.
In partial remission
A specifier used when full criteria were previously met but have not been fully met over the past six months, while impairment in functioning persists.
Relative age effect
The greater likelihood that the youngest students in a class will be diagnosed because they are compared with older classmates.
Externalizing problems
Problems directed toward others, such as aggression, defiance and rule-breaking.
Internalizing problems
Problems directed inward, such as anxiety, sadness, withdrawal and somatic complaints.
Oppositional defiant disorder
A persistent pattern of angry or irritable mood, argumentativeness, defiance and vindictiveness.
Conduct disorder
A repetitive and persistent pattern of violating the rights of others or major age-appropriate social norms or rules.
Limited prosocial emotions
A specifier for conduct disorder describing lack of remorse, lack of empathy, unconcern about performance and shallow affect.
Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder
Severe, recurrent temper outbursts with persistently irritable mood between them, beginning before age 10.
Emotional disturbance
An educational category in US law for students whose emotional or behavioral characteristics impede their learning over a long period of time and to a marked degree.
Social maladjustment
A term left undefined in US regulations; students so described are excluded from the emotional disturbance category unless they are also found to have an emotional disturbance.
Universal behavior screening
Periodically rating every student in a class with a brief instrument to find those who need support or evaluation, including students who have not been referred.
Informant discrepancy
Differences among parents’, teachers’ and students’ ratings of the same problems, read as information about context rather than measurement error.
Continuous performance test
A computerized task that measures sustained attention and impulsivity through responses to a sequence of stimuli.
Functional behavioral assessmentFunctional Behavioral Assessment (FBA)
Gathering information on what precedes and follows a behavior in order to determine the function it serves for the student.

T-score, sensitivity and specificity, and cut-off score are terms defined in Chapter 2, and partial-interval recording is defined in Chapter 4.

Review questions

  1. A fifth grader’s teacher rated seven inattention symptoms and two hyperactivity symptoms, and his mother rated three inattention symptoms, along with marked difficulty completing homework. Is Criterion C met? What is the likely presentation? And what information is needed to determine severity? (Objective 1)
  2. Identify two differences between the DSM and the ICD-11 covered in this chapter, and explain how each would affect a report you receive about a student. (Objective 1)
  3. In light of the first-grade admission rule in Saudi Arabia, how large might the age gap be between the youngest and the oldest student in a single class? Propose a procedure in your school that would reduce misdiagnosis arising from this. (Objective 2)
  4. An eighth-grade girl’s grades have slipped; she forgets her homework and seems distracted. What questions would you ask before considering attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder? And when does a late appearance of difficulties not rule out the diagnosis? (Objectives 2 and 6)
  5. Classify the following as externalizing or internalizing problems: refusing instructions, stomachaches on school-day mornings, frequent fighting, silence at school while speaking at home, and running away from school. Then explain the difference between a student who has severe outbursts three times a week with irritable mood between them and a student who argues with and defies his teachers without severe outbursts, and why the two diagnoses are not given together. (Objective 3)
  6. A secondary school student skips school and deliberately breaks rules with his friends, and shows no clear emotional symptoms. How does the definition of emotional disturbance in US law treat his case? What is your critique of this exclusion? And what do Saudi regulations say? (Objective 4)
  7. A student performed within normal limits on a computerized continuous performance test, while his family’s and teachers’ ratings are very high. How would you interpret this? (Objective 5)
  8. A mother rated her daughter’s internalizing problems in the normal range on the Child Behavior Checklist, while the student rated herself in the clinical range on the self-report. How would you interpret this? Why would you not average the two scores? And which instrument would you add next? (Objective 5)
  9. Why do hearing and vision checks and screening come at the second step of the decision pathway, before any diagnostic instrument? Name three explanations ruled out at the third step, with a question or instrument for each. (Objective 6)
  10. Both Haya and Lamia show inattention and falling grades. Why did the first case end in a referral for anxiety assessment, and the second in eligibility under the emotional disturbance category? Identify the evidence that made the difference. (Objective 7)

Learning activities

Every activity carried out with students is conducted with the consent of the family and the school administration, without any data that could identify the student, and under the supervision of a specialist; its results are not used in any decision concerning the student.

  1. Comparative observation: Observe a student referred for hyperactivity, together with two students of the same sex who are closest to him in age, for ten minutes using partial-interval recording, and compare the percentages. Desk-based alternative: Design the observation form, write operational definitions for two behaviors, and try it out on a published video of a classroom. (Objectives 2 and 6)
  2. Classroom screening: With the school administration’s permission, and after informing families according to its procedures, have the teacher form of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire completed for an entire class; then identify the students with elevated emotional problems who were not among those you had been watching. Pass anything that calls for follow-up to the school counselor through the official channel, without informing any family of a result or discussing names. Desk-based alternative: Write ten brief hypothetical student profiles, rate them with the questionnaire, and compare whom the screening “caught” with whom you would have referred on impression alone. (Objective 5)
  3. A map of explanations: List ten different reasons a student might not pay attention in class, and for each one specify a question or instrument that would reveal it and where it falls in the decision pathway. (Objective 6)
  4. Comparing systems: Apply the columns of Table 10.15 to the cases of Lamia and Walid, and write for each: What is the decision under each system? What evidence does it rest on? And what does the Saudi text leave unresolved? (Objective 4)
