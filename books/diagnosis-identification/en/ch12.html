Giftedness

Where the inquiry into disability categories turns to what impedes learning, the inquiry into giftedness turns to what lies beyond what the classroom offers. And because giftedness does not force itself on our attention as disability does, the greatest danger in identifying it is overlooking a gifted student whom no one noticed, a more serious error than misidentifying one.

Scene from the teachers’ room

The fourth-grade teachers were asked to nominate students for the gifted program. Each teacher nominated her highest-scoring students: the organized ones who raise their hands and hand in their homework on time. None of them nominated Jana, because she “daydreams a lot, and her spelling grades are average,” even though her teacher the previous year had thought she might be gifted.

But the science teacher remembered that two months earlier, in an open-ended project, Jana had designed an experiment to test which types of soil hold water the longest, and had recorded her results in a table that nobody had asked her for.

Is Jana gifted? Are the nominated students gifted? Perhaps, and perhaps not: high grades and good conduct are not giftedness, and daydreaming and average grades do not rule it out. In this chapter, we set out how to answer that question with evidence rather than impressions.

Introduction

Identifying gifted learners differs from diagnosing disability both in the direction of the question and in the nature of the costliest error. The inquiry into disability categories is directed at what impedes learning; the inquiry into giftedness is directed at what exceeds what the classroom provides. Because giftedness does not catch a teacher’s attention the way disability does, the greater risk is that a gifted student will be missed, whether because he or she was never given an opportunity to show ability or because the student did not fit the picture of the organized, high-scoring pupil, as happened to Jana in the opening scene. Who is found and who is missed depends on the definition of giftedness adopted, on the instruments and their ceilings, and on the admission rules.

We open the chapter by defining giftedness and its models (Section 12.1), then present the principles of identification and the construction of local norms (Section 12.2), followed by nomination, the opportunity to display, and nomination and observation tools (Section 12.3). We then take up ability measures and their limits, the ceiling effect, and above-level testing (Section 12.4), followed by creativity, achievement, and performance (Section 12.5), instrument profiles (Section 12.6), and identification in the Saudi context and the decision pathway (Section 12.7). We apply all of this to three cases (Section 12.8) and close with what resembles giftedness and what conceals it (Section 12.9).

Identification, not diagnosis: what sets the concept apart in giftedness

In the field of gifted education, the term identification (Identification) denotes a comprehensive educational process whose purpose is to determine which students need enrichment or acceleration services beyond what the regular classroom offers. Giftedness is not a disorder whose symptoms are sought out; it is high potential or performance for which we seek the service that will develop it. The process therefore starts from the program’s definition of giftedness and from the services it provides: the instrument is chosen according to the type of service, multiple criteria are used instead of a single score, and the student is compared with local norms that take into account the opportunities he or she has had (NAGC, 2019b; Pfeiffer, 2015). Its outcome is a decision to place the student in a program, open to review, unlike a permanent classification that follows the student, because giftedness develops and emerges at different times (Lohman & Korb, 2006). Its most common error is omission: missing the student who was never nominated in the first place, which happens more often than placing a student who does not need the program (Card & Giuliano, 2016).

With disabilities, by contrast, identification is bound up with determining eligibility for special education services: the questions are whether the student has a disability in one of the specified categories and whether it affects his or her learning to a degree that calls for those services. In many categories, such as autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, this runs alongside a clinical diagnosis made against the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), issued by a qualified professional and sometimes the basis for treatment outside school. The question in disability is “What impedes this student’s learning, and does he or she qualify for services?”; in giftedness it is “What does this student need that the classroom does not provide?” For this reason, we prefer the term “identification” for giftedness and use “diagnosis” when we are speaking of a disorder, as when distinguishing giftedness from conditions that resemble it in Section 12.9.

12.1What is giftedness?

Definitions of giftedness abound, and no single agreed definition unites them. This multiplicity has practical consequences, because the definition a program adopts determines whom it looks for and with which tools. A program that defines giftedness as a high IQ will administer an intelligence test; one that defines it as creativity will look for creative products; and one that defines it as excellence in a particular domain will measure that domain. The most prominent conceptions follow.

Giftedness as high general intellectual ability

The scientific study of the gifted began with Lewis Terman, who launched his longitudinal study of gifted children in the 1920s and defined the gifted child by a very high score on an intelligence test: an IQ of roughly 135–140 or above on the Stanford–Binet for most of his sample, that is, approximately the top 1% (Terman, 1925). General intellectual ability remains part of most definitions, but relying on it alone has been criticized for overlooking giftedness in specific domains, creativity, and motivation.

Renzulli’s Three-Ring Conception

In his Three-Ring Conception, Joseph Renzulli proposed that gifted behavior emerges when three traits come together: above-average (though not necessarily exceptional) ability, task commitment, and creativity (Renzulli, 1978). What matters in this model is that it speaks of gifted behavior that appears in certain situations, rather than a fixed trait that a student either has or lacks.

Renzulli distinguishes between two kinds of giftedness. The first is schoolhouse giftedness (Schoolhouse giftedness), proficiency in learning lessons and taking tests, which ability and achievement tests detect well. The second is creative-productive giftedness (Creative-productive giftedness), the capacity to produce original ideas and products for a real audience, which tests alone do not reveal (Renzulli, 1986). This distinction bears directly on identification. In the Schoolwide Enrichment Model associated with the theory, a talent pool (Talent pool) of roughly 15–20% of students is formed, instead of selecting a small number by a cutoff score, by combining ability and achievement scores with teacher ratings. Students who display gifted behavior during enrichment activities may then move to a higher level of service, even if they were not in the pool to begin with (Renzulli & Reis, 2014). This is what the phrase “above-average, though not necessarily exceptional, ability” means.

Gagné’s model: from gifts to talents

In his Differentiated Model of Giftedness and Talent (Differentiated Model of Giftedness and Talent, DMGT), François Gagné distinguishes between two words often used as though they meant the same thing: gifts (Gifts), high natural abilities not yet refined, and talents (Talents), mastered skills in a particular field that have developed from those gifts through learning and practice. Gagné later revised his model in a second version (DMGT 2.0), changing three things that bear directly on identification (Gagné, 2009, 2013).

First, gifts now span six domains: intellectual, creative, social, perceptual, muscular, and motor control. Intellectual ability is thus one domain among six, and a student may be gifted in a domain that an intelligence test does not measure at all. Second, the developmental process became a separate component with three dimensions: the activities the student gains access to, along with their content and format; the investment he or she makes in time, effort, and money; and progress, with its stages, pace, and turning points. The process is surrounded by intrapersonal catalysts, such as physical and mental traits and goal management (awareness, motivation, and volition), and by environmental catalysts, such as the milieu, influential individuals, and available services including enrichment and acceleration. Third, chance reaches beyond environmental factors to affect every component: the gifts themselves through heredity, the family and place into which a child is born, and the opportunities and people the child happens to encounter.

The second version adds something missing from most definitions: a numerical threshold. For Gagné, the gifted are those who fall within the top 10% of their peers in a domain of gifts or a field of talent. Those above the threshold are then divided into five levels, each ten times rarer than the one before.

The model has a further implication for identification: a student in an environment with limited opportunities may have high potential that has not yet turned into outstanding performance. If we look only for demonstrated performance, we will miss this student. Identification therefore needs instruments that reveal potential alongside achievement.

Giftedness as talent development

Recent theories increasingly view giftedness as a developmental process that extends across the lifespan. Its clearest expression is the talent development megamodel (Talent development megamodel) proposed by Rena Subotnik and her colleagues (Subotnik et al., 2011) and elaborated in a later review (Worrell et al., 2019). The model rests on five ideas, each with implications for identification:

  • Giftedness is a trajectory within a specific domain. The trajectory begins with potential, then moves to competency, then expertise, and finally eminence or professional creativity. Identification looks for something different at each stage: potential at the outset, then demonstrated achievement as the student advances.
  • Each domain has its own timing. The starting point, peak, and end of the trajectory vary from one domain to another. Mathematics, music, and sports begin early, and outstanding students in them can be identified in elementary school. Domains such as psychology, law, and philosophy begin later, and aptitude for them emerges only after a student has studied them. Both the age of identification and the type of instrument, in other words, depend on the domain.
  • Ability is necessary and malleable. General ability and domain-specific abilities are a prerequisite, yet they grow and are shaped by schooling and opportunity.
  • Psychosocial skills are decisive. These include persistence, tolerance of criticism, stress management, confidence, and the ability to learn from mentors. Their importance grows as the student advances along the trajectory, until at the higher stages they are what separates people of comparable ability.
  • Opportunities must be offered, and they must be taken. Schools and families must provide opportunities, and the student must seize them. The aim of the model is for the talented individual to reach, in adulthood, eminent achievement or a creative contribution to his or her field; what counts is what the person attains as an adult, more than a “gifted” label carried in childhood.

This is consistent with the definition of the National Association for Gifted Children (National Association for Gifted Children, NAGC), which describes gifted individuals as those who perform, or have the capability to perform, at higher levels compared with others of the same age, experience, and environment in one or more domains (NAGC, 2019a). Note two phrases in this definition: “have the capability to perform,” meaning potential alongside achievement, and “environment,” meaning that the comparison takes account of the opportunities available.

Pfeiffer’s tripartite model

Steven Pfeiffer proposed a model closer to assessment practice than to theory building, one that views giftedness through three lenses, each with its own tools (Pfeiffer, 2015, 2018). The first lens, high intelligence, is revealed by individual and group intelligence tests and suits programs that offer general academic challenge. The second lens, outstanding accomplishment, is revealed by achievement tests, products, and performance in the field, and suits subject acceleration or domain-specific programs. The third lens, potential to excel, concerns the student who has not yet had the opportunities; it is revealed by rating scales, observation over time, and dynamic assessment, and it suits a program that provides opportunities and then revisits the decision. The model’s value lies in reminding the team that a student may be gifted through one lens and invisible through the others, and that the third lens is the least stable of the three, so that its decision should be treated as provisional and subject to review.

Common misconceptions
  • “A gifted student excels in every subject.” Many gifted students are outstanding in one area and average in others, and some have a difficulty or disability alongside their giftedness; these are twice-exceptional learners (Chapter 13 discusses them in detail).
  • “A gifted student will find the way alone.” Gifts do not turn into talents without opportunities and appropriate instruction, and a gifted student who finds no challenge may lose motivation.
  • “A gifted student is well behaved and well liked by teachers.” Some gifted students challenge authority, ask awkward questions, and grow bored with repetition, so they come across as disruptive or indifferent.

How are local norms constructed?

The idea behind local norms is simple: we ask where the student stands among those who have had similar opportunities to learn, rather than among a national sample that includes far wealthier schools. Peters and colleagues found that computing norms at the school level increases the representation of underrepresented groups in gifted programs (Peters et al., 2019). Applying them, however, requires decisions made in advance and announced openly, which the following box summarizes.

Six questions before using local norms
  1. Who is the reference group? Students in the same grade who share the same opportunities to learn: the school, or a group of similarly situated schools within the education directorate, such as its rural schools. Schools with very different opportunities should not be pooled into one group, as this reintroduces the very problem local norms were meant to solve.
  2. What is the minimum size? There is no agreed figure, but the upper ranks require large numbers. In a class of 20 students, the “top 5%” is a single student. A percentile rank is the percentage of the student’s peers in the reference group or norming sample who scored lower than the student; a percentile rank of 84 means the student outperformed 84% of them. In a class of this size, a percentile rank of 95 does not distinguish the first student from the second, and a change in one or two answers is enough to change the judgment. If the group numbers only in the tens, it is better to enlarge it or to combine years.
  3. School or directorate? School norms come closer to the question “Who needs more than his or her class provides?”, while directorate norms are more statistically stable. A common compromise is to compute norms for a group of similarly situated schools, or for the school with several years combined.
  4. Should data be pooled across years? Yes, when classes are small, provided that the instrument and administration conditions remain constant and that the report says so.
  5. What are their limits? The label does not transfer between schools: a student identified in one school might not be identified in another. The absolute level of identified students also varies from school to school, so services must start from the student’s actual level as the evidence shows it. Moreover, local norms reveal able students in less advantaged settings, but they do not address the cause of the gap.
  6. How do they relate to excellence gaps? Excellence gaps (Excellence gaps) are differences between groups in the proportions reaching advanced levels of performance (Plucker & Peters, 2016). Local norms are a first step toward closing them, because they reveal who merits services; closing the gaps themselves requires services that make up for the opportunities that were missing.

12.3Nomination and the opportunity to display

Teacher nomination remains the most widespread route into identification. It has its uses, since the teacher sees the student over a long period and in many situations. But it has well-known limits: teachers tend to nominate high-scoring, well-behaved students who fit the image of the “good student,” and to overlook those who are quiet, those who differ linguistically or culturally, and those whose ability is masked by a difficulty. Nomination may even be influenced by the teacher as well as by the student: a large U.S. study found that the likelihood of a student being identified as gifted varied with the background of the student’s teacher, even when students’ test scores were equal (Grissom & Redding, 2016). In Saudi Arabia, Alfaiz examined the accuracy of parent and teacher nomination in identifying gifted students (Alfaiz, 2026), a question on which local practitioners will need to follow the evidence.

The opportunity to display

In his research on the visibility of gifted students for nomination, the author of this book proposes a framework he calls the opportunity to display (Opportunity to display), which locates a reason for the shortcomings of nomination deeper than teacher bias: a teacher cannot see what the classroom gives the student no opportunity to show. A classroom built on memorization and short answers does not reveal a student’s ability to solve an open-ended problem, pose an original question, or build a project. Many nomination checklists ask about behaviors, such as curiosity, unusual questions, and original thinking, that appear only in activities that allow for them. When such activities are absent, what might be called the teacher’s inference load increases: the teacher is forced to infer giftedness from indirect signs such as grades and good conduct, and so falls back on nominating high achievers.

The framework is close to well-established ideas in the literature; what it adds is to make opportunity a first step in the identification pathway, where it had been a circumstance mentioned when interpreting results. An instrument such as the Teacher’s Observation of Potential in Students links observation to providing rich learning activities over several weeks (Coleman et al., 2010). Local norms compare the student with those who have had similar opportunities (Peters et al., 2019). And Pfeiffer’s “potential to excel” lens concerns students who have not yet had the opportunities (Pfeiffer, 2015). What is new here is the question that comes before all of them: did the classroom offer anything to observe in the first place?

What this means in practice

Before we ask teachers to nominate, we need to give students opportunities to show what they can do: open-ended activities, problems without a single solution, and independent projects, even if only for a few weeks. Observation and nomination come afterward. In Jana’s scene at the start of the chapter, no one noticed her ability until the open-ended science project gave her an opportunity to display it.

For the teacher: indicators worth noticing

These are situations to watch for and record; when you see them, note the date and context, without waiting for grades:

  • Grasps a new idea after a brief presentation and grows restless when it is repeated.
  • Asks questions that go beyond the lesson, or connects two subjects no one else has connected.
  • Reaches a solution by a route other than the one taught, and can explain that route.
  • Holds a deep, sustained interest in a topic outside the curriculum and knows more about it than would be expected at his or her age.
  • Uses vocabulary or reasoning beyond his or her years, or understands complex humor before peers do.
  • Produces outside school what does not show up in it: a project, a program, drawings, or writing.
  • Shows clear improvement in performance and behavior when given a harder task.

If you notice these indicators in a student whose grades are average or whose behavior is disruptive, that is all the more reason to record them.

12.4Ability measures and their ceilings

Group and individual tests

In universal screening, group ability tests are typically administered to an entire class, on paper or online, such as the Cognitive Abilities Test (CogAT), which measures verbal, quantitative, and nonverbal reasoning, and the Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test, Third Edition (NNAT3), which relies on figural matrices and is therefore little affected by language. Students who exceed the screening cutoff, the score above which a student is referred for more in-depth assessment, are then evaluated with an individual intelligence test such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children or the Stanford–Binet (Chapter 3 presents these instruments in detail, and the Cognitive Abilities Test profile appears in Section 12.6).

Limits of nonverbal tests

Nonverbal tests are often presented as the answer to fairness for linguistically diverse students and those from less advantaged settings. There is some truth in this, but the literature points to three limits (Lohman, 2005). First, nonverbal reasoning predicts academic achievement less well than verbal and quantitative reasoning do, and academic programs need students who will succeed in their subject matter. Second, such tests sometimes narrow group differences, though their effect on this is weaker than commonly assumed. Third, relying on them alone may exclude students who genuinely excel in verbal or quantitative reasoning, since their performance on matrices and figures is not necessarily exceptional. The alternative this literature proposes is to keep the nonverbal test within a battery that includes verbal and quantitative reasoning, and then to compare the student with those who have had similar opportunities, that is, against local norms.

The General Ability Index and the Cognitive Proficiency Index

On the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fifth Edition (WISC-V), the Full Scale IQ (Full Scale IQ, FSIQ) is influenced by the Working Memory and Processing Speed indexes (Chapter 3 defines these indexes). Many gifted students, particularly twice-exceptional learners, perform noticeably lower on these two indexes than on reasoning and comprehension.

Two composite scores are therefore derived from the scale to separate the two sides. The first is the General Ability Index (General Ability Index, GAI), built from five subtests in verbal comprehension, visual-spatial processing, and fluid reasoning: Similarities, Vocabulary, Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, and Figure Weights. The second is the Cognitive Proficiency Index (Cognitive Proficiency Index, CPI), built from four subtests in working memory and processing speed: Digit Span, Picture Span, Coding, and Symbol Search. The former describes what the student can reason through; the latter describes how efficiently he or she processes information quickly and accurately (Kaufman et al., 2016).

The difference between the two indexes is read like any difference between two scores: Is it statistically significant, that is, too large to be attributed to measurement error? And how rare is it in the norming sample? (See Chapter 2.) A student with a General Ability Index of 132 and a Cognitive Proficiency Index of 104 shows a 28-point gap. Such a difference merits interpretation if it proves to be both significant and rare, as established by statistical computation rather than by the visual impression of a jagged profile. The National Association for Gifted Children recommends using the General Ability Index in identifying gifted students and not requiring the Full Scale IQ when a student’s profile is uneven (NAGC, 2018).

When a student reaches the ceiling of several subtests, answering the last items or most of them, the Full Scale IQ and the General Ability Index can no longer capture the student’s ability, because the score cannot go beyond the highest value in the norm tables. For this reason, the publishers of some scales, including the Wechsler, have issued extended norms (Extended norms) that extend scores beyond their usual limit for students who reach the ceiling (see Chapter 2). These help distinguish among the upper levels of giftedness, but they rest on very small numbers at the top of the distribution, so they should be read with caution, and the publisher’s conditions for their use should be consulted.

The ceiling effect and above-level testing

In assessing intellectual disability, a problem known as the floor effect (Floor effect) arises: the test lacks sufficiently easy items, so it cannot distinguish among very low levels of performance. Giftedness presents the opposite problem: the ceiling effect (Ceiling effect). An achievement test designed for a given grade lacks sufficiently difficult items, so a gifted student answers almost everything correctly and obtains a percentile rank of 99, just like a classmate who has merely mastered the grade-level curriculum. We then have no way of knowing where either student’s ability actually lies.

The solution developed in talent search programs (Talent Search) is above-level testing (Above-level testing): an outstanding student takes a test designed for students several years older. Students who were tied at the ceiling spread out, revealing those who need genuine acceleration. A long-term investigation, the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth (Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, SMPY), followed thousands of students identified in this way before the age of 13 and found that differences among them on above-level tests predicted large differences in their accomplishments decades later (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006).

12.5Creativity, achievement, and performance

Measuring creative thinking

Among the best-known instruments for measuring creative thinking are the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking, TTCT), whose profile appears in Section 12.6. They come in a verbal form and a figural form. In the figural form, the student is given incomplete or repeated shapes and asked to complete them as meaningful drawings and to give each drawing a title. The responses are then scored on several dimensions, among them fluency (Fluency), the number of ideas; flexibility (Flexibility), the variety of their categories; originality (Originality), their rarity relative to other people’s responses; and elaboration (Elaboration), how much the student adds to the basic idea (Kim, 2006).

Creativity tests have important limitations. They measure divergent thinking (Divergent thinking), the production of many and varied ideas, which is only one facet of creativity. Performance on them is sensitive to the instructions and to the student’s mood on the day of testing, and their reliability is lower than that of intelligence tests. Their predictive validity, that is, their capacity to predict creative achievement in adulthood, remains contested: some longitudinal studies have found a relationship between their scores and creative achievement decades later, while critics consider the relationship modest and inconsistent across studies (Kim, 2006). For this reason they serve as a supplementary source, alongside assessment of the student’s actual creative products over time.

For evaluating the product itself, a well-known method is the Consensual Assessment Technique (Consensual Assessment Technique), proposed by Teresa Amabile (Amabile, 1982). Its premise is that the creativity of a product is defined by the judgment of those who know the field: the product, whether a story, a drawing, or a design, is presented to several judges with expertise in the field, each of whom rates its creativity independently of the others, and their agreement is then examined. If they agree to an acceptable degree, the mean of their ratings is taken as a measure of the product’s creativity. This method comes closest to what “performance before a panel” means in the arts, writing, and design, provided that the judges are experts in the field and that they rate independently before any discussion.

Achievement and performance

Very high achievement in a domain signals a talent already realized in it. It is measured with achievement tests, preferably supplemented by above-level testing to overcome the ceiling effect, as discussed earlier. Performance, for its part, is measured through authentic performance tasks, that is, tasks resembling what specialists in the field actually do, and through portfolios that collect samples of the student’s work over time. In some domains, such as the arts, sports, and leadership, performance before an expert panel is the primary source of evidence, because paper-and-pencil tests do not capture it.

12.6Instrument profiles

The following profiles present four instruments that represent the main types of evidence used in identifying gifted learners: a group ability test for screening, a teacher rating scale, a test of creative thinking, and the above-level testing procedure. The profile of the Mawhiba test appears in Section 12.7, together with the regulatory context in which it is used. None of the profiles reproduces items from any published test.

CogATCognitive Abilities Test, Form 7Group ability test

The idea

Students learn at school through three symbol systems: words, numbers, and shapes and pictures. This test was therefore designed to measure reasoning within each system separately rather than to yield a single IQ. The aim is to identify the student’s ability profile so that suitable instruction or programming can be chosen (Lohman, 2011).

What it looks like

Three batteries, each with three subtests. Verbal battery: verbal or picture analogies, sentence completion, and classification. Quantitative battery: number analogies, number puzzles, and number series. Nonverbal battery: figure matrices, paper folding, and figure classification. At the early levels, from kindergarten to second grade, items are presented as pictures and directions are read aloud, so the student does not need to read. The test is administered on paper or online to a whole class.

A scene from administration

At a private international school that teaches in English, the administration decides to conduct universal screening of its third graders. Teachers administer the three batteries over two consecutive mornings. The profile of one student, whose first language is Arabic, reads as follows: Quantitative 131, Nonverbal 128, Verbal 112. The school summarizes the profile with a code such as “9B (V-)”: the number is the median stanine, the letter describes the extent of variation across the batteries, and the parenthesis identifies the relatively low battery. The school psychologist asks: is the lower verbal score a weakness in verbal reasoning, or an effect of learning in a second language? She decides to compare him with peers who are also learning in a second language before reaching any judgment.

How are the results interpreted?

Interpretation begins with pattern before level: are the three batteries close together or divergent? Scores are then chosen to fit the service. A mathematics acceleration program looks first at the quantitative and nonverbal scores, and a language program looks at the verbal score. Note that the standard age score has a standard deviation of 16, so a score of 130 on it is not equivalent to 130 on a Wechsler scale.

Its limits

Its items and norms are in American English, so it is unsuitable for students learning in Arabic unless adapted and normed. A group test is also less precise for the individual than an individual test; it is a screening tool that paves the way for more precise assessment. Relying on the nonverbal battery alone reintroduces the limitations of nonverbal tests discussed in Section 12.4. Among the best-known nonverbal alternatives is the Naglieri Nonverbal Ability Test, Third Edition (NNAT3), a 48-item matrices test completed in about 30 minutes that yields an index with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 (Naglieri, 2016). We likewise know of no published, normed Arabic version of it.

GRSGifted Rating ScalesRating scale

The idea

A teacher sees a student in many situations, yet one teacher’s impression is not easily compared with another’s. This scale was designed to convert teacher observation into a standard score that compares the student with same-age peers across six domains of giftedness that extend beyond intellectual ability (Pfeiffer & Jarosewich, 2003).

What it looks like

The school form contains 72 items distributed across six scales of 12 items each: intellectual ability, academic ability, creativity, artistic talent, leadership, and motivation. The teacher rates each item on a nine-point scale divided into three ranges: below average (1–3), average (4–6), and above average (7–9), relative to students of the same age. A second form covers preschool and kindergarten.

A scene from administration

A fifth-grade teacher completes the school form for a student she has taught for two semesters. She pauses at an item on leadership and recalls that the girl has never led a group, because the teacher has always assigned the roles herself. Rather than choosing a number from the middle range, she notes in the margin that she has had no opportunity to observe this behavior.

How are the results interpreted?

The six scales are read as an integrated profile before any total. A high score in artistic talent alone points toward a performance assessment in art rather than a general academic program. The score is also compared with what another source says: if intellectual ability is high on the rating but low on the test, the question becomes what the teacher saw that the test did not, or vice versa.

Its limits

It reflects the teacher’s impression and what the classroom allowed the teacher to see, and so it carries the limitations of nomination discussed in Section 12.3. A student’s scores on the six scales may also cluster closely, which suggests a global impression more than discrimination among domains, a phenomenon known as the halo effect. It does not cover the middle and secondary school years. The Scales for Rating the Behavioral Characteristics of Superior Students, by contrast, are flexible and cover more domains, but they lack national norms and therefore require local norms built by the program (Renzulli et al., 2010).

TTCTTorrance Tests of Creative ThinkingPerformance test

The idea

If creativity is one component of giftedness, we need a task that shows how a student generates ideas when a question has no single answer. That is what this test was designed to do: open-ended tasks in which ideas, their variety, and their rarity are counted, regardless of whether they are correct (Kim, 2006).

What it looks like

The test has two forms, each with two equivalent versions, A and B. The figural form consists of three activities of ten minutes each: picture construction from a given shape, completion of incomplete figures, and transformation of repeated shapes into different drawings, with a title for each drawing. The verbal form comprises written activities such as asking questions about a picture, guessing causes and consequences, improving a product, and proposing unusual uses for a familiar object. Under the current scoring method for the figural form, fluency, originality, and elaboration are scored, along with abstractness of titles and resistance to premature closure, that is, keeping a figure open long enough before closing it with a familiar solution. Flexibility is no longer scored on the figural form, although it remains a useful concept for reading responses, as in Figure 12.4.

A scene from administration

The specialist administers the figural form to a small group of students. He reads the directions exactly as they appear in the manual, because a single extra word of encouragement can raise the number of ideas. He notices one student who finished the first activity in four minutes and then sat staring at the ceiling. He records this in his notes: a low fluency score may reflect that day’s motivation more than the student’s ability.

Some items in the verbal form depend on cultural experiences and therefore require adaptation. Such rarity shifts over time and with changes in what children are exposed to: an idea that was rare thirty years ago may be commonplace today.

Above-levelAbove-level testingAssessment procedure

The idea

We saw in Section 12.4 that a grade-level test stops at its ceiling, where a student who has mastered the grade’s curriculum becomes indistinguishable from one who is years ahead. The idea is to raise the ceiling by giving the student a test designed for older students. The procedure was developed by talent search programs, the best known of which is the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, which administered a college admission test to twelve-year-olds (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006).

What it looks like

Two steps. First, a grade-level test; students who score at the 95th percentile or higher advance to the second step, the usual requirement in talent search programs. Second, a standardized test designed for a grade two to four years higher, in the subject in which acceleration is being considered. The result is then interpreted against the norms of the higher grade and by comparing the student with same-age peers who took the same test.

A scene from administration

After Salma’s result came in (Figure 12.3), the counselor did not settle for calling her “very advanced.” He asked for an analysis of her answers on the ninth-grade test to find out what she did not yet know. A learning plan was then drawn up covering only those topics, followed by an end-of-course test in an advanced course to confirm her mastery before she moved on to a higher course. This is a well-known model in talent search programs called Diagnostic Testing → Prescriptive Instruction (Diagnostic Testing → Prescriptive Instruction, DT→PI), which rests on the principle that students should not be taught what they have already shown they know (Assouline et al., 2015).

Its limits

It measures a specific subject and so cannot serve as evidence of general giftedness. It may also be affected by content the student has not yet been taught, so low performance should be read as lack of exposure before it is taken as lack of ability. It must also lead to an actual educational decision; otherwise it becomes a number that changes nothing. The literature on acceleration summarizes extensive evidence of its academic benefit when it is grounded in assessment of this kind (Assouline et al., 2015).

Three observations arise from these documents. First, the older regulations treated the criteria as alternatives, any one of which suffices, whereas the gifted classes guide made them cumulative, requiring two criteria, one of which is ability. An “or” rule widens the door and increases errors of the “identifying a student who is not gifted” kind, while an “and” rule narrows it and increases errors of the “missing a gifted student” kind (Chapter 6 presents these combination rules). Second, the documents use three IQ cutoffs, 115, 120, and 125, without stating which level of giftedness each is meant to capture, and without any provision directing that the standard error of measurement or the confidence interval be taken into account. A student with a score of 118 and a confidence interval of 112–123 does not differ statistically from a student who scores 120. Third, in the documents we reviewed, we found no provision on adapting identification instruments for students with disabilities, nor any provision permitting or prohibiting simultaneous enrollment in a gifted program and special education services, even though a recent Saudi study estimated that about 5.9% of a sample of 6,875 students in four private schools in Riyadh may be twice-exceptional (Almulhim, Aljughaiman, Alnaim, et al., 2026).

A question for reflection: A student scored 126 on the Wechsler scale but did not sit the Mawhiba test because her family did not know when registration opened. Under which of the three documents would she be admitted, and under which would she not? And what does that say about the effect of the national instrument on access?

How are the results interpreted?

Because the official scale is unpublished, the practitioner reads the result from two angles: Did the student exceed the threshold that qualifies him or her for a particular program? And where does the student rank among applicants from the same grade? Note that the comparison here is only with those who sat the test. If the four domain scores are available, the pattern among them matters more for service decisions than the total score alone.

Its limits

This is a reading of the test in light of the principles in Section 12.2, and fairness requires stating what it achieves before what it does not. It achieves a uniform instrument across regions, measurement of more than one domain of reasoning, an authentically Arabic construction, a repeated opportunity at each level, and fee waivers for subsidized students. In its published form, however, it does not achieve four things. First, optional registration is not universal screening: students whose families do not know about the test, or do not consider it important, never reach it, which is precisely what studies of universal screening have warned against (Card & Giuliano, 2016). Second, admission to programs rests on a single cutoff score, whereas the principles call for multiple sources. Third, the norms are national, so a student from a small village is compared with a student from a large city. Fourth, its technical evidence and its accommodations for students with disabilities are unpublished, so practitioners cannot judge the precision of a score near the cutoff. The Ministry’s programs fill part of these gaps: the gifted classes guide allows unfilled places to be completed using multiple school-based criteria, among them personal traits and creative output. But this completion depends on places remaining unfilled, and the national test remains the main gateway.

What evidence could change the decision? If her performance in the advanced science course falls short of what the above-level test result suggests, the team would revisit the amount of acceleration and the starting point. If the daydreaming persists at home and at school after she has found challenge, with effects on her organization and performance, an assessment for possible twice-exceptionality would be added to the giftedness decision.

This is what aligning identification with services means: the question is not “Is she gifted?” but “What service does she need?”

What evidence could change the decision? If a later reassessment of her ability yielded a General Ability Index whose entire confidence interval lay above the program threshold, or if independent judges rated her creative writing products highly, the team would reconsider placing her in the general gifted program or in a specialized language program.

Conclusion

The central argument of this chapter is that the definition of giftedness determines the tools of identification, and that every definition and every admission rule determines, at the same time, who may be missed. It follows that teacher nomination should not be relied on alone, since it is limited by teachers’ biases and by the opportunities to display that the classroom affords; that universal screening and local norms should widen access, with due attention to their conditions; that a nonverbal test should form part of a broader battery; and that above-level testing should be used when the ceiling limits what we can read of a student’s ability. Creativity tests serve as a complement, alongside the evaluation of real products.

Some questions in the chapter nonetheless remain contested, and the evidence on others has yet to be published. The predictive validity of creativity tests is disputed. The Mawhiba test is a national instrument developed in Arabic, but registration is optional, and its score scale and technical evidence are unpublished. Saudi documents use different IQ thresholds (115, 120, and 125) with different combination rules. Distinguishing giftedness from a disorder remains a judgment that no single question can settle: the disappearance of a behavior under challenge is a supporting clue that needs others alongside it. And identification extends beyond the question “Is the student gifted?” to the service that suits the domain and level of the student’s giftedness.

12.10Summary, key terms and activities

Chapter summary

  • The definition of giftedness determines the tools of identification; contemporary definitions encompass both potential and achievement and take the environment into account. Each of the four models has those it may miss.
  • Gagné distinguishes natural gifts from developed talents and sets a threshold at the top 10%, with five levels that help in reading admission rules.
  • Teacher nomination is limited by teachers’ biases and by the opportunities the classroom affords for giftedness to be displayed. Universal screening and local norms widen access; local norms carry conditions on the size of the reference group and limits on how far the label transfers.
  • A nonverbal test is part of a broader battery. The General Ability Index is useful with uneven profiles, and its difference from the Cognitive Proficiency Index is read in terms of significance and rarity. Above-level testing solves the ceiling problem and sets the starting point for acceleration.
  • Creativity tests measure divergent thinking; their predictive validity is disputed, and they are used as a complement alongside the evaluation of real products.
  • The Mawhiba test is a national instrument developed in Arabic, but registration is optional and its score scale and technical evidence are unpublished; Saudi documents use different IQ thresholds (115, 120, and 125) with different combination rules.
  • In distinguishing giftedness from a disorder, the disappearance of a behavior under challenge is a supporting question that needs further evidence.
  • The most important question after “Is the student gifted?” is “What service suits the domain and level of the student’s giftedness?”

Glossary of terms

Gift
High natural ability not yet refined through learning and practice.
Talent
A mastered skill in a specific field, developed from gifts through learning and practice.
Opportunity to display
The situations a learning environment offers the student in which to display his or her abilities.
Universal screening
Assessing all students at a given grade level rather than relying on nomination.
Ceiling effect
A test’s inability to discriminate among high-performing students because it contains too few difficult items.
Above-level testing
Administering a test designed for older students to an able student in order to gauge how far ahead he or she is.
General Ability IndexGeneral Ability Index, GAI
A composite score measuring reasoning and comprehension without working memory or processing speed.
Cognitive Proficiency Index, CPI
A composite score drawn from working memory and processing speed subtests, compared with the General Ability Index to describe an uneven profile.
Talent pool
A broad group of students, about 15–20%, offered enrichment services in Renzulli’s model, with flexible entry for those who display gifted behavior.
Excellence gaps
Differences between groups in the proportions of students who reach advanced levels of performance.
Consensual Assessment Technique
Evaluating a product’s creativity through independent ratings by judges familiar with the field, then examining their agreement.
DT→PI
A model used in talent search programs that pinpoints what a student does not yet know of an advanced course, teaches only that material, and then tests the student at the end of the course.
Divergent thinking
The capacity to generate many varied and original ideas for a single problem.
Three-Ring Conception
Renzulli’s conception of gifted behavior as the intersection of above-average ability, task commitment, and creativity.
Local norms
Comparing a student’s performance with that of peers in the same school or area rather than with the national norming sample alone.
Creative-productive giftedness
The capacity to produce original ideas and products for a real audience; Renzulli contrasts it with schoolhouse giftedness, that is, skill at learning lessons and taking tests.
Acceleration
Presenting content to a student at a younger age or a faster pace than usual, such as studying a subject with a higher grade.
Twice-exceptional
Gifted students who also have a disability or disorder.
Gifted underachiever
A student whose ability is very high and whose achievement falls clearly below it.

Learning activities

Any activity in which an activity or instrument is administered to students, or in which a real student’s data are used, is carried out with the consent of the family and the school administration, without any information that could identify the student, and under the supervision of a qualified specialist. Its results are not used in any decision concerning the student and are reported only through official channels.

  1. A week of opportunities to display: Design five open-ended activities for a class you teach or are able to observe, and before administering them, write a list of the students you would have nominated for a gifted program. Then administer the activities under the conditions above, record, using codes rather than names, the students who showed indicators you had not expected, and compare the two lists. If no class is available to you, apply the steps to Jana’s scene and Thamer’s case. Product: five written activities, a comparison table, and a reflective paragraph. Time: one week for administration and about two hours for writing.
  2. A program’s definition: Choose a gifted program in your area, such as gifted classes or one of Mawhiba’s programs, and extract from its published documents its definition of giftedness, its tools, its thresholds, and its combination rule. Then compare these with Tables 12.3 and 12.9, identify whom the program may miss, and propose one feasible modification. Product: a table and a paragraph. Time: about two hours.
  3. A creative activity: Design an activity in the style of Figure 12.4, using an incomplete geometric shape other than a semicircle. Administer it to ten of your classmates, or to students under the conditions above, and score the responses for fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration. Then ask two colleagues to rate the creativity of the same responses independently using the Consensual Assessment Technique, and compare the three sets of ratings. Product: the activity, a table of ratings, and a paragraph on interrater agreement. Time: about two hours.
