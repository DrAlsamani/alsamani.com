Three messages to the counseling office

The second-grade teacher: “Khalid daydreams a lot, and he only follows instructions if I repeat them. He often asks the boy next to him what I said. The odd thing is that in some lessons he is fully present, and in others it’s as if he’s in another world. Should we refer him for an ADHD evaluation?”

The first-grade teacher: “Rahaf holds the book very close, tires quickly when reading, and confuses letters that look alike.”

The kindergarten teacher: “Abdulrahman is six, and he says ‘galam’ (گلم) instead of ‘qalam’ (قلم, pen) and ‘tamaka’ (تمكة) instead of ‘samaka’ (سمكة, fish). Does he have an articulation disorder?”

The three messages raise questions that this chapter sets out to answer. Could what looks like inattention be a hearing loss that face-to-face conversation with the child fails to reveal? How do we assess vision in a way that serves learning? And how do we tell a child’s dialect apart from a disorder in his speech? We then move on to harder questions that the messages did not raise: How do we assess the ability of a deaf student who communicates in sign language? And how can a judgment be reached about a child who cannot see, cannot speak and cannot hold a pencil?

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

  1. Read an audiogram, determining the degree and type of loss by comparing air conduction with bone conduction, and infer which speech sounds the child is missing.
  2. Classify results for distance visual acuity and visual field according to the international classification and the Saudi regulations, and specify what functional vision assessment and learning media assessment add to the medical report.
  3. Identify, for each communication disorder, its distinguishing feature, the specialist who assesses it and an appropriate assessment procedure.
  4. Apply the criteria for developmental language disorder to a child’s data, and distinguish it from dialectal difference and second-language learning.
  5. Select instruments for assessing the ability of a student who is deaf, blind or has a motor impairment, and determine in a given scenario whether an accommodation invalidates norm-based interpretation of the score.
  6. Plan a functional assessment for a child with multiple disabilities or deafblindness for whom standardized instruments are unsuitable.
  7. Judge whether the evidence in a new case is sufficient to warrant a judgment, and justify that judgment through the steps of the decision pathway, including ruling out sensory impairment before assigning any other category.

Introduction

In sensory disabilities and communication disorders, equipment and medical specialists carry a large share of the diagnosis. Yet the importance of these categories extends well beyond their own borders, because weak hearing, vision or language can mimic any other category. A student who follows instructions only when they are repeated, as in one of the messages at the start of the chapter, may be referred for an attention evaluation when the root of the problem lies in his hearing. The educational question remains the team’s to answer: what does the medical number mean for the student’s learning? And how can the student be assessed in a way that reaches past the disability to the ability behind it, especially when disabilities converge and standardized instruments are, more often than not, unusable?

We open the chapter by explaining why these categories matter for all the others (Section 11.1), then present hearing impairment (Section 11.2) and visual impairment (Section 11.3), from measurement to functional assessment. Next we address communication disorders and the assessment of need for augmentative and alternative communication (Section 11.4), developmental language disorder and how it presents in Arabic (Section 11.5), and the difference between difference and disorder (Section 11.6), followed by multiple disabilities, deafblindness, physical and health impairments, and brain injury (Section 11.7). We then turn to warning signs and decision pathways (Section 11.8), instrument profiles (Section 11.9), four cases (Section 11.10), and differential diagnosis and co-occurrence (Section 11.11), and we close with the Saudi and Arab context (Section 11.12).

11.1Why do these categories matter for every category?

Hearing and vision screening appears in the decision pathway of almost every category in special education. The reason is that a hearing or vision impairment can resemble any other category: it may look like inattention, as we will see in Khalid’s case in this chapter, or like a learning disability, or autism, or even intellectual disability if the student is assessed with instruments that do not suit his senses. Sensory disabilities are therefore the gateway of exclusion in every other diagnosis: ruling out hearing and vision impairment is a step that precedes any diagnostic instrument, and we do not judge a child’s ability until we are sure that he hears and sees what we present to him.

This chapter brings together three groups of categories joined by a single feature: the disability lies in the very channels of reception and expression on which assessment instruments depend. Hearing and visual impairments affect what reaches the child; communication disorders affect what comes out of the child; and multiple and physical disabilities affect both, as well as the ability to point, grasp and write. In all of these cases, the first question in assessment becomes: does the instrument measure what it was designed to measure in this child, or does it measure the disability?

Common misconceptions
  • “The child passed newborn hearing screening, so his hearing is fine.” Hearing loss may emerge after birth, may be acquired, after meningitis for example, or may be a fluctuating conductive loss that comes and goes with ear infections. A screening result is a snapshot of a single day.
  • “A hearing aid restores normal hearing.” A hearing aid amplifies sound but does not fully restore its clarity, and its benefit is weaker in noise and at a distance.
  • “Glasses solve vision problems.” Glasses correct refractive errors; they do not repair retinal disease, cerebral visual impairment or a restricted visual field.
  • “Sign language is simplified gesture.” Sign languages are complete natural languages with their own vocabulary and grammar, and they differ from one country to another.
  • “A child who does not speak does not understand.” The absence of speech may be motor or sensory in origin, and many of these children understand far more than they are able to express.

11.2Hearing impairment

The audiogram

The audiologist measures the softest sound the child can hear at different frequencies, from low-pitched to high-pitched sounds, in each ear separately, and plots the results on a chart called an audiogram (Audiogram). The horizontal axis represents frequency in hertz, from low on the left to high on the right. The vertical axis represents sound intensity in decibels, with louder sounds toward the bottom. The lower the marks fall, the louder a sound the child needs in order to hear, and thus the more severe the hearing loss.

Hearing is measured in two ways. In air conduction (Air conduction), sound is delivered through headphones and travels through the outer and middle ear to the inner ear. In bone conduction (Bone conduction), a small vibrator is placed behind the ear, and sound reaches the inner ear through the bones of the skull, bypassing the outer and middle ear. Comparing the two is what reveals where the problem lies: if air conduction is reduced while bone conduction is intact, the problem lies in the pathway of sound to the inner ear; if both are reduced, the problem lies in the inner ear or the nerve, as in the left ear in Figure 11.1.

The shaded area labeled “Speech banana” is the range in which the sounds of ordinary speech fall. Any speech sound that lies above the child’s marks, that is, softer than them, goes unheard. This explains why some children hear “that someone is talking” without understanding the words: they hear the loud, low-pitched sounds such as vowels, but miss many of the soft, high-pitched sounds such as /s/, /f/ and /θ/.

The audiologist also determines the type of loss by comparing the two kinds of conduction:

  • Conductive (Conductive): bone conduction is normal and air conduction is reduced, with a gap between them. It originates in the outer or middle ear. Among its most common causes in children is fluid collecting behind the eardrum with middle ear infection, and it is often temporary or fluctuating: it comes with a bout of infection and eases afterward, and it is treated medically.
  • Sensorineural (Sensorineural): both air and bone conduction are reduced by roughly the same amount, as in the left ear in Figure 11.1. It originates in the inner ear or the auditory nerve and is usually permanent.
  • Mixed (Mixed): both are reduced, with a gap between them, so the loss combines the two types.

Presentations missing from the standard audiogram

Three presentations of hearing loss can easily escape a team that asks only whether the child can hear or not.

  • Unilateral hearing loss (Unilateral hearing loss): the child hears well face to face in a quiet room and so passes many everyday observations. But he struggles in noise, has difficulty telling where a sound is coming from, and misses speech coming from the side of the weaker ear. Even so, the claim that “one ear is enough” is mistaken: these children are at greater risk than their peers for difficulties in language and achievement, and they need monitoring and classroom accommodations, as we will see in Khalid’s case (11.10).
  • Auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder (Auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder): the inner ear works, so otoacoustic emissions appear normal, but the signal is not transmitted to the brain in an orderly way, and the auditory brainstem response is absent or abnormal. The distinguishing educational sign is that the child’s understanding of speech is far worse than the teacher would expect from the audiogram, and that hearing may fluctuate from day to day. Because some screening programs rely on otoacoustic emissions alone, these children may pass the screening.
  • Fluctuating hearing loss (Fluctuating hearing loss): its best-known form is the conductive loss that accompanies recurrent middle ear infections. It is among the conditions that most closely resemble inattention, because the child is “present” one week and “absent” the next, and because a single test on a good day may come back normal.
For the specialist: The debate over central auditory processing disorder

The team is sometimes referred a child whose audiogram is normal but who is said to have central auditory processing disorder (Central auditory processing disorder): difficulty understanding speech in noise, distinguishing similar sounds or following long instructions. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association defines it as a deficit in the central nervous system’s processing of auditory information that is not attributable to higher-order language or cognitive deficits, while acknowledging that it may co-occur with other disorders (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association [ASHA], 2005). The concept, however, is widely contested. Most of its tests depend on language, attention and memory, so their results are hard to separate from weaknesses in those abilities; there is no agreed reference test; and its symptoms overlap with those of ADHD and developmental language disorder. The practical conclusion for the team is this: the explanation should be considered only after peripheral hearing has been fully assessed and language and attention have been evaluated, and it should then be treated as a description of a pattern of difficulty that merits classroom accommodations, added to whatever other diagnoses are established.

Screening and diagnosis

  • Newborn screening: quick tests that require no response from the infant, such as measuring otoacoustic emissions from the inner ear (Otoacoustic emissions, OAE) or the auditory brainstem response (Auditory brainstem response, ABR). The Joint Committee on Infant Hearing (Joint Committee on Infant Hearing, JCIH) recommends the 1-3-6 principle: screening by the end of the first month, diagnosis by the end of the third month and intervention by the end of the sixth month. Its most recent position statement adds a more ambitious target for systems that have already achieved this: 1-2-3, that is, diagnosis by the end of the second month and intervention by the end of the third (JCIH, 2019). A well-known study found that children whose hearing loss was identified and whose intervention began before six months of age had clearly better language than those identified later (Yoshinaga-Itano et al., 1998). Section 11.12 describes the national newborn hearing screening program in the Kingdom.
  • School screening: the student is presented with tones at 20 dB at 1000, 2000 and 4000 Hz in each ear. If the student fails to hear any of them, the screening is repeated, and if the tone is missed again, the student is referred to an audiologist (ASHA, 1997).
  • Diagnosis: this is the audiologist’s task, using pure-tone audiometry by air and bone conduction, speech audiometry for detection and discrimination, middle ear pressure measurement (Tympanometry) and other procedures. The first profile in Section 11.9 presents these procedures.

What educational assessment adds

An audiogram yields a number, but it does not tell us how the child hears in a classroom with thirty students and an air conditioner. The educational team therefore completes the picture by assessing functional hearing (Functional hearing), that is, how the child actually uses his hearing in class; by assessing language in the mode through which the child communicates, whether spoken Arabic or sign language; and by determining the most suitable mode of instruction. The child’s cognitive ability is measured with nonverbal instruments, or instructions are given in the communication mode he has mastered, rather than through verbal tests delivered in a language he cannot hear well (see the box in 11.9).

The perspective of the Deaf community and sign language

Many deaf people see themselves as members of a linguistic and cultural community with its own language, not as individuals who lack hearing. Whether or not the team adopts this perspective, it has three consequences for assessment. First, Saudi Sign Language is a complete language, so a child who is fluent in it has an intact first language, and his language development should be judged in that language before his spoken or written Arabic. Second, assessment should be conducted in the child’s stronger language, by an examiner fluent in it or with the help of a qualified interpreter, with an explicit statement in the report that interpretation alters the standardized conditions of administration. Third, the greatest danger to a deaf child is language deprivation, which is more harmful than deafness itself: reaching school age without a fluent language, whether spoken or signed, because no one gave the child access to a language he could perceive in full. Such a child may look on tests as if he has an intellectual disability, when what he actually has is deprivation of language.

At the time of writing, we know of no published standardized instrument for assessing Saudi Sign Language. Its assessment therefore relies on nonstandardized sources: a sample of the child’s narrative in sign language, evaluated by a teacher or a deaf adult fluent in it; comparison with what is known about language development in general; and questions to the family about the language in which the child was raised and when his exposure to it began.

11.3Visual impairment

Visual acuity

Visual acuity (Visual acuity) is the sharpness of vision. It is measured with a chart of letters or symbols of decreasing size and expressed as a fraction. A value of 6/6 means that the person sees at six meters what a person with normal vision sees at six meters. A value of 6/60 means that the person must come as close as six meters to see what a person with normal vision sees from sixty meters. In systems that use feet instead of meters, 6/6 is written as 20/20. With young children and those who do not know letters, charts with simple symbols are used, such as LEA symbols, a circle, a square, an apple and a house, or four-letter charts (HOTV) on which the child points to the matching letter on a card in front of him without having to name it.

This is distance acuity. Near acuity (Near acuity) is measured at reading distance and is not always equal to distance acuity: some children see the board but tire when reading a book, and others the reverse. Nor does acuity reveal contrast sensitivity (Contrast sensitivity), the ability to distinguish an object from its background when their colors are similar, such as gray print on white paper or a stair step the same color as the floor. A student’s acuity may be acceptable on a black-on-white chart, yet he stumbles over a faded photocopied worksheet.

Visual field

The visual field (Visual field) is the area a person can see without moving the eyes. Visual acuity may be good at the center of vision while the field is restricted, so the student sees what is directly in front of him clearly, as if looking through a tube, but misses what lies to either side. Such a student may read the small letters on the chart yet bump into classmates in the hallway and fail to see the whole board.

Functional vision assessment, learning media assessment, and orientation and mobility

The ophthalmologist or optometrist provides acuity, field and the medical diagnosis. The educational assessments are carried out by the teacher of students with visual impairments and other specialists:

  • Functional vision assessment (Functional vision assessment): shows how the student actually uses vision in the classroom, the hallways and the playground: what print size the student reads comfortably, from what distance the board is visible, what lighting and contrast suit the student, and how long the student can sustain visual work before tiring.
  • Learning media assessment (Learning media assessment): determines the most suitable medium for the student to learn reading and writing: regular print with magnification devices, large print, braille, a combination of media, or auditory media. The decision rests on observing how the student explores the environment, primarily through vision or through touch, and on the student’s efficiency and speed in reading with each medium (Koenig & Holbrook, 1995).
  • Orientation and mobility assessment (Orientation and mobility): conducted by an orientation and mobility specialist, it shows how the student determines where he is and moves about safely and independently: in hallways and on stairs, in dim lighting and when crossing the street, and whether he needs training with the white cane.

Assessment of a student with a visual impairment extends beyond the general curriculum. Specialists in educating this population have formulated what is known as the expanded core curriculum (Expanded core curriculum): skills that sighted children usually learn through incidental observation but that a blind or low-vision student needs to be taught explicitly. It comprises nine areas: compensatory and access skills such as braille, orientation and mobility, social interaction, independent living, recreation and leisure, career education, assistive technology, sensory efficiency, and self-determination. Each area requires an assessment of the student’s present level, because a plan that overlooks them teaches the student the curriculum while leaving him unable to access it.

Cerebral visual impairment

Some children have healthy eyes, but the brain does not process what the eye sees in a typical way. This is called cerebral or cortical visual impairment (Cerebral/Cortical visual impairment, CVI), and it is one of the common causes of visual impairment among children with multiple disabilities. It appears in characteristic patterns, such as a preference for a particular color, difficulty seeing objects in a visually cluttered environment and delayed visual response. Detecting it requires a specialized functional assessment that complements the conventional eye examination (Roman-Lantzy, 2018). We will meet it in Salman’s case (11.7).

11.4Communication disorders

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), classifies communication disorders into several types (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022). Under its implementing regulations, the U.S. Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) adds voice disorders to the category of “speech or language impairment”; these do not appear in the diagnostic manual because they are not neurodevelopmental disorders (U.S. Department of Education, 2006, 34 CFR §300.8(c)(11)). The pathologist also uses picture-based articulation tests in which the child names pictures covering the sounds of the language in their various positions in the word: initial, medial and final. A language sample (Language sample analysis) drawn from the child’s natural speech is analyzed, yielding measures such as mean length of utterance. For stuttering, the percentage of stuttered syllables in a speech sample is calculated, and a standardized instrument may be used to rate severity, such as the Stuttering Severity Instrument, Fourth Edition (SSI-4) (Riley, 2009). For voice disorders, voice therapy begins only after an ear, nose and throat physician has examined the larynx, because persistent hoarseness may have an organic cause that requires medical treatment.

Comparison with ICD-11

The International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11), was adopted in 2019 and came into effect on 1 January 2022. It places speech and language disorders among the neurodevelopmental disorders under the heading “developmental speech or language disorders,” distinguishing developmental speech sound disorder, developmental speech fluency disorder and developmental language disorder (Developmental language disorder). The most important difference from the American manual is that pragmatic impairment in ICD-11 is a subtype of developmental language disorder, “with impairment of mainly pragmatic language,” alongside other subtypes, including one that affects both receptive and expressive language and one that affects mainly expressive language. The classification states explicitly that the language in question may be spoken or signed (World Health Organization, 2024). Hearing and visual impairments, by contrast, are classified in ICD-11 as health conditions in other chapters, outside the neurodevelopmental disorders.

Arabic is not a translation of English

Arabic differs from English in its sounds, the order in which they are acquired and its grammatical structures. An articulation or language test translated from English does not measure Arabic sounds that do not exist in English, nor does it reflect their order of acquisition. Researchers have studied the order of acquisition of Arabic consonants among Jordanian children, first at preschool age and then at older ages (Amayreh & Dyson, 1998; Amayreh, 2003). Such studies, along with instruments built on Arabic, should inform judgments about a child’s articulation, bearing in mind that the child’s dialect may differ from that of the study sample.

Assessing the need for augmentative and alternative communication

For some children, speech is not enough to communicate, either because they do not speak or because their speech is unintelligible to anyone outside the family: the child with cerebral palsy affecting the speech muscles, the nonspeaking autistic child, the child with multiple disabilities. For these children, augmentative and alternative communication (Augmentative and alternative communication, AAC) is assessed; it includes signs, pictures, symbol boards and speech-generating devices. This assessment does not require the child first to prove “readiness” by reaching a particular cognitive or linguistic level; every child whose speech does not meet his communicative needs is a candidate. The assessment answers practical questions: How can the child make a selection, by touch, by eye gaze, or with a switch pressed with the head? What can the child see, and what size of symbol can he discriminate? Does he understand that a picture stands for an object? With whom does he communicate, and what does he want to talk about? One or more systems are then tried in real situations and judged by what the child achieves with them. The evidence indicates that alternative communication often supports the development of speech, contrary to the common fear that it hinders it.

11.5Developmental language disorder

Children whose language was delayed for no apparent reason used to be described in many ways: “language delay,” “developmental aphasia” and “specific language impairment” (Specific language impairment). The last of these usually required the child’s language to be clearly below his nonverbal intelligence. An international panel of specialists then came together in a consensus study known as CATALISE and settled on a unified term: developmental language disorder (Developmental language disorder, DLD) (Bishop et al., 2017). The international classification (ICD-11) has adopted the same term.

What has changed?

  • Language disorder or developmental language disorder? “Language disorder” is used for any child with a language difficulty that is likely to persist beyond early childhood and that affects communication or learning. If the difficulty is associated with a known condition, such as sensorineural hearing loss, autism, intellectual disability or a genetic syndrome, the term is “language disorder associated with….” The term “developmental language disorder” is reserved for children whose difficulty is not associated with any of these conditions (Bishop et al., 2017).
  • A discrepancy with nonverbal intelligence is not required. A child whose nonverbal intelligence is in the low average range, above the level of intellectual disability, can be diagnosed with developmental language disorder. A British population study found that about 7% of children at school entry have the disorder, and that children with low nonverbal intelligence did not differ from others in the severity or impact of their language difficulties (Norbury et al., 2016). Requiring a discrepancy had denied children services without justification.
  • Co-occurrence does not rule out the diagnosis. The presence of ADHD, dyslexia or developmental coordination disorder does not preclude a diagnosis of developmental language disorder.

The dilemma of diglossia

The Arab child speaks a dialect at home and encounters Modern Standard Arabic at school and in books, and the examiner’s or the test’s dialect may differ from the child’s own. When the child is measured in Standard Arabic or in another dialect, his performance appears lower than it actually is; this is diglossia (Diglossia). Matters become more complicated still with a child raised in a language other than Arabic. Assessment therefore rests on three principles: language is measured first in the child’s own dialect; if the child is bilingual, his other language is examined as well, because a true disorder appears in both languages; and tasks less influenced by prior language experience are used, such as dynamic assessment (see 11.6 and Chapter 5) and nonword repetition.

Its role in differential diagnosis

Developmental language disorder lies at the heart of differential diagnosis for several categories. With intellectual disability, the deficit is general, encompassing nonverbal ability and adaptive behavior, whereas in developmental language disorder nonverbal ability and adaptive behavior outside the language domain are clearly stronger. With weak reading comprehension, many students who read fluently but do not understand have a weakness in oral language itself, so listening comprehension is measured before a reading difficulty is concluded. With autism spectrum disorder, the two may resemble each other in weak language and social difficulties, but the social difficulties in developmental language disorder stem from the language weakness, are not accompanied by restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, and leave nonverbal communication and the desire to interact intact. With hearing loss, the loss is ruled out first, including fluctuating loss that a single test may miss.

11.6Difference versus disorder

Among the most important things a teacher should know is that a child who speaks in his own dialect does not have a disorder. Children learn to talk from their families and pronounce words as the people around them do. A child who says “گلم” (galam, “pen”) in a community where everyone pronounces the qaf as a g is speaking entirely correctly by the standard of his dialect. A disorder, by contrast, is measured as a departure from the pattern of the child’s own dialect. Among the patterns that warrant evaluation is the replacement of fricatives (Fricatives), sounds produced with a continuous stream of turbulent air, such as s, sh and z, with stops (Stops), sounds in which the airflow is blocked and then released in a single burst, such as t and d. This substitution is called stopping (Stopping).

The same rule applies to a student learning Arabic as a second language. His errors in sentence structure may simply be a natural stage in learning a new language. To tell the two apart, we ask about his first language: does he speak it with fluency appropriate for his age? A genuine language disorder appears in both languages, whereas difficulty learning a second language appears in the new language alone. Here dynamic assessment, presented in Chapter 5, is useful: the child is taught a new language skill during the assessment session itself, and the examiner observes how quickly he learns it. A child who learns quickly once taught is most likely showing limited experience with the language, whereas one who learns very slowly despite instruction warrants a more in-depth evaluation.

11.7Multiple disabilities, deafblindness, physical and health impairments, and brain injury

In these categories, standardized instruments recede to the margins, because most of them assume a child who can see, hear, point, hold a pencil and sit still for a full hour. Functional assessment, repeated observation in natural settings and curriculum-based measurement become the primary sources of evidence. Assessing the child with deafblindness

A child with deafblindness is seldom both totally deaf and totally blind; most of these children have residual hearing, residual vision, or both. Assessment therefore begins with a joint functional assessment of hearing and vision: what the child sees, at what distance and in what lighting; what he hears and under what conditions; and how he uses the two senses together. This assessment is carried out over several days in familiar settings, because the child’s responses are affected by fatigue, medication and location.

Next comes the communication assessment, the centerpiece of the entire evaluation. Many of these children are at the pre-symbolic communication stage: they communicate through body movements, changes in facial expression, crying, and taking an adult’s hand and guiding it, before words, signs or pictures have developed. The assessment asks: does the child act intentionally to influence others? Does he use real objects as symbols, such as holding a spoon to ask for food? Does he understand tactile communication (Tactile communication), such as signs made with his hand resting on the signer’s hand so that he can feel them, or consistent touch cues on his body that announce what is about to happen? Among the descriptive tools used here is the Communication Matrix, which describes seven levels of communication, from unintentional behavior to language, without yielding a standard score. Family members and others who know the child well take part in this assessment, because they are best able to read his subtle signals.

Orthopedic impairment: when does an accommodation invalidate the norms?

A child with cerebral palsy may understand the question and know the answer, yet be unable to point to it quickly, arrange the pieces or draw the figure. If a standardized test is administered to him as it stands, it measures his hands, not his mind. The response mode is therefore adapted: pointing with eye gaze instead of a finger, selecting an option with a switch, or saying the answer for the examiner to record, along with comfortable, supportive seating that stabilizes the body.

For the specialist: accommodations that preserve the norms and accommodations that invalidate them

Norms assume that the student was given the same task under the same conditions as the norming sample. The decisive question, then, is whether the accommodation changes what the test measures or only how the student accesses it. If the test measures reasoning, changing the way the student indicates an answer usually leaves the construct intact, and the score can be interpreted against the norms, with a note in the report. But if timing is removed from a test of speed, if drawing is replaced with selecting an option on a test of visual-motor integration, or if the items of a reading test are read aloud to the student, the construct itself has changed and the standard score becomes meaningless. In that case the result is written as a description of what the student did under the accommodated conditions and is not converted into a standard score or a percentile rank. (The percentile rank is the percentage of the student’s peers in the norming sample who scored below him, so a rank of 84 means that he outperformed 84% of them; it is precisely this comparison that has lost its basis here.) The same holds for converting a test into braille or large print: an enlarged item may become easier or harder, and braille reading is slower than print reading, so item timing and difficulty change. Every accommodation is named in the report, along with the examiner’s judgment of its effect on the norms.

Health impairments: attention and attendance

Students with chronic illnesses are often referred for “poor attention” or “low achievement.” The first question here is whether what we are seeing is an effect of the illness or its treatment, or a separate difficulty. Absence seizures appear as repeated lapses lasting a few seconds that a teacher may mistake for daydreaming, and some antiepileptic medications can affect alertness and memory. Low blood sugar in a student with diabetes weakens concentration at particular times of day. Frequent absences for treatment or hospitalization, as may occur with sickle cell anemia, leave gaps in instruction, not in ability. Assessment therefore takes three things into account: it is scheduled at a time when the student is in his best health; the physician is asked about the expected effect of medication; and the student’s achievement is compared with the instruction he has actually received, alongside comparison with his class, with measurement repeated once his condition stabilizes.

Acquired brain injury and distinguishing it from intellectual disability

A student who was learning like his peers and then, after a car accident or a fall, saw his performance decline differs from a student with intellectual disability, even if their scores are similar. The difference is established through three lines of evidence. The first is history: the injured student has a record of performance before the injury, in grades, reports and work samples, that serves as a baseline for comparison. The second is the pattern of abilities: injury usually leaves an uneven profile, such as slow processing and weak new learning and attention, while knowledge acquired before the injury remains intact, whereas the deficit in intellectual disability is more general. The third is the trajectory of change: the injured student’s performance may improve rapidly in the first months, so assessment is repeated at intervals before any lasting judgment is made. The criterion of onset during the developmental period is not decisive on its own, because a child injured in childhood is injured during the developmental period; for this reason the diagnostic manual permits a diagnosis of intellectual disability in such cases if its criteria are met, together with a diagnosis of a neurocognitive disorder (APA, 2022). The educational difference here is that this student’s needs change quickly, so his plan is reviewed more often than those of other students.

11.9Instrument profiles

The three profiles present core procedures in these categories: audiometry, functional vision assessment together with learning media assessment, and a comprehensive language test. The first two are professional procedures without published norms, so some fields in their tables differ from those in the test profiles. Where we were unable to verify a figure in the technical manual itself, we have written “in the technical manual.” This book does not publish the items of any test; the examples were prepared by the author.

PTA · OAE · ABRAudiometry: pure tones, tympanometry and objective measuresHearing impairment

The idea

Pure-tone audiometry (Pure-tone audiometry, PTA) determines the softest sound the child can hear at each frequency, by air and bone conduction, and thus yields the degree and type of loss. Tympanometry shows whether the eardrum moves normally, and so indicates fluid behind it. Otoacoustic emissions and the auditory brainstem response, for their part, are objective measures that require no response from the child; they are used with newborns and with children who cannot respond.

What it looks like

A sound-treated booth, an audiometer, headphones, and a bone-conduction vibrator. The response method changes with age: an older infant is rewarded with a lighted toy when he turns toward the sound, a preschooler drops a block into a basket each time he hears a sound, and an older child raises his hand or presses a button. In tympanometry, a small probe is placed at the entrance of the ear canal for a few seconds.

A scene from administration

The audiologist tells a six-year-old: “Every time you hear the beep, even if it’s very soft, put the bead in the box.” She begins with a clearly audible tone at 1000 Hz, then lowers it by 10 dB each time he responds and raises it by 5 dB each time he does not, until she reaches the softest sound he responds to on most trials. She notices that the child has begun dropping the bead whenever she looks at him, responding to her glance rather than to the sound, so she varies the timing of the presentations to keep him from guessing.

How are the results interpreted?

The air-conduction marks are read to determine the degree, then compared with bone conduction to determine the type: a gap between the two with normal bone conduction indicates conductive loss, whereas the two converging at a reduced level indicates sensorineural loss. The tympanogram is then read: a flat tympanogram with conductive loss suggests fluid behind the eardrum. Finally, the thresholds are compared with the speech banana to see what the child is missing, and the word recognition score is compared with what the audiogram predicts; if it is much worse, auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder is considered.

Its limits

It measures what the child hears in a quiet room; how well he understands in a noisy classroom is another matter. It gives a picture of a single day and therefore does not detect fluctuation unless testing is repeated. Behavioral testing depends on the child’s cooperation and attention, so it may be inaccurate with young children or children with multiple disabilities, in which case objective measures are decisive.

FVA · LMAFunctional vision assessment and learning media assessmentVisual impairment

The idea

The medical report answers the question: what is wrong with the eye? These two assessments answer two other questions: how does the student use his vision in his daily life, and by what means does he read and write? They require no norms, because the student is compared with the demands of his environment rather than with his peers (Koenig & Holbrook, 1995).

What it looks like

A collection of tools and situations rather than a single test booklet: near and distance acuity charts, including LEA Symbols and HOTV charts for young children; contrast sensitivity charts; texts in graded print sizes; light sources; and observation checklists for the classroom, hallway and playground. In learning media assessment, reading rate and accuracy are recorded in regular print, in large print, and in braille for students who are learning it, and the way the student explores new objects is observed.

A scene from administration

A teacher gives a third-grade student three cards bearing the same text in three print sizes, asks her to read each for one minute, and records the words read correctly. She then repeats this with lighting from the side. Next, she accompanies the student at recess and notes where she stops, where she touches the wall, and whether she recognizes her friends from a distance or only after they speak.

The aim is to find the medium that gives the student the best combination of accuracy, speed and comfort over sustained work. A student who reads large print at an acceptable rate without tiring has large print as her medium. Its limits

There are no norms against which to compare the student, so its quality depends on the quality of observation. It is affected by fatigue, lighting and the day of assessment. How the specialist read the evidence: Studies of the acquisition of Arabic consonants indicate that mastery of some fricatives comes late in Arabic and may extend beyond school entry (Amayreh & Dyson, 1998; Amayreh, 2003). Had Abdulrahman’s error involved a single late-mastered sound, watchful waiting and follow-up would have been the better course. What tipped the judgment was three things taken together: the substitution covers an entire class of sounds, it reduces his intelligibility to unfamiliar listeners at an age when he is expected to be understood, and his family’s dialect does not explain it.

Decision: Speech sound disorder, manifested in the replacement of fricatives with plosives. Speech therapy sessions are recommended. His pronunciation of /q/ is not treated; he will learn Standard Arabic at school as his classmates do.

What evidence could change the decision? If samples of his speech in other settings showed that the substitution is limited to one or two late-mastered sounds, or that unfamiliar listeners understand most of what he says, monitoring would be preferable to treatment.

Case 4: Mashael, age 14

Deaf, with no additional learning disability

The story: Mashael is in the second year of middle school. She has been deaf since birth, with a profound bilateral sensorineural loss, and wears two hearing aids of limited benefit. Her parents are deaf, so she grew up with Saudi Sign Language from birth. This year she moved to a new school, and her Arabic teacher noticed that her reading and writing were at roughly a fourth-grade level. The teacher asked: does she have a learning disability, or low intellectual ability?

How the team read the evidence: Had Mashael been assessed with a verbal intelligence test in Arabic, her score would have come out low, and the team would have concluded that she has an intellectual disability. Had her language development been judged by her Arabic writing alone, they would have concluded that she has a language disorder. Yet her first language is intact and rich, her nonverbal ability is average, and her weakness is confined to her second language and improves when the text is explained to her in her first. This is the pattern of a second-language learner, not of a learning disability.

Decision: Mashael remains in the hearing impairment category, with no added diagnosis of learning disability or intellectual disability. Explicit instruction in Arabic reading and writing that builds on sign language is recommended, along with test instructions delivered in sign language and an interpreter or a teacher fluent in it during lessons. Should she show difficulty in sign language itself, or in arithmetic despite full access to instruction, that would be grounds for reconsidering a co-occurring learning disability.

The lesson of the case: A deaf student’s language development is measured in the student’s stronger language. Delay in written Arabic alone proves neither a learning disability nor any other disability, because it is to be expected of anyone who learns Arabic as a second language without hearing it.

What evidence could change the decision? If her Arabic reading made no meaningful progress after a sufficient period of explicit instruction building on sign language, or if a second signed narrative sample revealed a weakness absent from the first, the team would reconsider a co-occurring learning disability.

Co-occurring condition

Many causes of deafness and blindness, such as extremely preterm birth, certain infections before and after birth, and genetic syndromes, also affect the brain, so other disabilities occur alongside the sensory disability in the same child. Surveys report that a considerable proportion of deaf and hard-of-hearing children have an additional disability, and that visual impairment is common among children with cerebral palsy and multiple disabilities, although estimates vary with samples and definitions. Developmental language disorder, for its part, frequently co-occurs with dyslexia, ADHD and developmental coordination disorder. Each combination carries its own guidance for assessment:

  • Sensory disability with suspected intellectual disability: A judgment of intellectual disability requires both a tool suited to the functioning channel and adaptive behavior that supports it; where this is not possible, the judgment is deferred, as in Salman’s case.
  • Hearing impairment with a suspected learning disability: Evidence is sought from outside the second language, such as difficulty in sign language itself, or in arithmetic despite full access to instruction, as in Mashael’s case.
  • Sensory disability with suspected autism: The sensory loss is addressed first, with a hearing aid, glasses or accommodations, and the social symptoms are then reconsidered. Any autism measure used with a deaf or blind child is interpreted with great caution, because its items assume intact hearing and vision.
  • Developmental language disorder with reading difficulty: Listening comprehension and decoding are each measured separately; if both are weak, the two problems co-occur.
  • Developmental language disorder with inattention: Instructions for attention measures are given in simple language, and the team observes whether the student’s attention also falters on nonlinguistic tasks; if it falters only on language tasks, language is the more likely explanation.
  • Stuttering with social anxiety, especially in adolescents: the students themselves are asked about the situations they avoid and how they feel in them, because avoidance may disrupt their lives more than the stuttering itself.

Two points emerge from the table. First, the Saudi regulations classify a student as educationally blind within wider limits than the international classification: a student with an acuity of 4/60 has “severe low vision” under the international classification but is “blind” under the regulations. This makes educational sense, since students in this range usually need the media used by blind learners. Second, the hearing criterion is measured with hearing aids, so it captures the disability that remains after intervention, whereas the audiologist measures the underlying loss unaided. The team must therefore be sure which of the two measurements a report refers to. The Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities affirms the right to “modern measurement and diagnostic tools” and to early intervention programs and early detection of disability (Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, 1445 AH [Arabic], Arts. 8 and 9).

Newborn hearing screening in the Kingdom: The National Newborn Hearing Screening Program has operated since 2016. In 2021, 199,034 newborns were screened, a coverage rate of 92.6%, and 1.87% did not pass and were referred (Alothman et al., 2024). A systematic review of thirteen Saudi studies identified six challenges facing the program, among them low awareness, loss of some children to follow-up after referral, and weak data-entry and tracking systems (Alanazi & Nicholson, 2025). For the school team, this means that a child’s having been screened at birth does not always mean that diagnostic follow-up took place if the child did not pass, so the question should be asked explicitly. At the time of writing, we found no published official Ministry of Health page on the program that we could consult, and no published text establishing a national hearing and vision screening program at school entry or its protocol.

A question for reflection: Khalid has a permanent, moderate-to-severe sensorineural loss in one ear, and his other ear is normal. Does he meet the “hard of hearing” criterion in the Saudi regulations? If not, where will he obtain the accommodations he needs? And should unilateral loss be named explicitly in the regulations, as the World Health Organization’s classification does?

Conclusion

The central argument we have sought to develop in this chapter is that the number produced by a device or a medical examination is where educational judgment begins, not where it ends. The audiogram and vision measurements establish the degree and type of loss, but what this means for the student’s learning calls for functional assessment and learning media assessment, and a deaf student’s language development is measured in the student’s stronger language. It follows that hearing and vision screening should precede any diagnostic tool; that the team should watch for forms that are easily missed, such as fluctuating and unilateral loss and auditory neuropathy; that neither dialect nor second-language learning should be treated as a disorder; and that the report should name the classification it has adopted for degrees of hearing and vision.

The hardest judgments we have discussed remain those in multiple disabilities and deafblindness, where standardized tools usually fall away. Judgment then rests on repeated functional and communicative assessment in natural settings, and no degree of intellectual disability is assigned when it cannot be validly measured. The line between an accommodation that preserves the norms and one that invalidates them remains a matter of judgment in each case: what changes the mode of access usually preserves them, and what changes the construct invalidates them, in which case the result is reported descriptively.

11.13Summary, key terms and activities

Chapter summary

  • Hearing and vision screening is the gateway of exclusion in every diagnosis and precedes any diagnostic tool, because impairment in either can resemble any other category.
  • The audiogram shows the softest sound a child hears at each frequency, and comparing air and bone conduction determines the type of loss. Fluctuating and unilateral loss and auditory neuropathy are forms the team can easily miss.
  • Classifications of degrees of hearing and vision differ across sources, and between the international classification and the Saudi regulations, so the report names the classification it has adopted.
  • Vision is measured through distance and near acuity, visual field and contrast sensitivity, complemented by functional vision assessment, learning media assessment, orientation and mobility, and the areas of the expanded core curriculum.
  • Communication disorders are of five types, including voice disorders, and are assessed by the speech-language pathologist after hearing has been tested. Developmental language disorder does not require a discrepancy with nonverbal intelligence.
  • Dialect is not a disorder, second-language learning is not a language disorder, and a deaf student’s language development is measured in the student’s stronger language.
  • In multiple disabilities and deafblindness, standardized tools usually fall away, so judgment rests on repeated functional and communicative assessment in natural settings, and no degree of intellectual disability is assigned when it cannot be validly measured.
  • An accommodation that changes the mode of access usually preserves the norms; one that changes the construct invalidates them, and the result is then reported descriptively.

Glossary of terms

Audiogram
A chart showing the softest sound a person hears at different frequencies in each ear.
Decibel
The unit for measuring sound intensity; the higher the value, the louder the sound.
Bone conduction
Transmission of sound to the inner ear through the bones of the skull, bypassing the outer and middle ear.
Conductive hearing loss
Loss arising in the outer or middle ear, shown by a gap between air and bone conduction; it may be temporary or fluctuating.
Sensorineural hearing loss
Loss arising in the inner ear or the auditory nerve, usually permanent.
Auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder
A disorder in which the inner ear functions but the signal is not transmitted to the brain in an orderly way, so speech understanding is poorer than the audiogram would predict.
Tympanometry
A measurement of eardrum movement; a flat tracing usually indicates fluid behind the eardrum.
Saudi Sign Language
A complete natural language used by deaf people in the Kingdom, with its own vocabulary and grammar.
Language deprivation
A child’s reaching an advanced age without a fluent language, spoken or signed, because full access to a language was never provided.
Visual acuity
Clarity of sight, expressed as a fraction such as 6/6 and measured at distance and near.
Contrast sensitivity
The ability to distinguish an object from its background when their colors are close.
Visual field
The area a person can see without moving the eyes.
Functional vision assessment
An assessment of how a student actually uses vision in learning situations and daily life.
Learning media assessment
An assessment that determines the most suitable medium for a student with low vision or blindness to learn reading and writing.
Expanded core curriculum
Skills that a student with visual impairment needs to be taught explicitly, in nine areas, including braille, orientation and mobility, and independent living.
Cerebral visual impairment
Impairment in the brain’s processing of what the eye sees, with the eye itself intact or nearly so.
Developmental language disorder
A persistent language difficulty with functional impact, not associated with a known condition such as hearing loss, autism or intellectual disability, and not requiring a discrepancy with nonverbal intelligence.
Speech sound disorder
Persistent difficulty producing speech sounds that is not appropriate for age and interferes with the intelligibility of the child’s speech.
Stopping
Replacing a fricative sound, such as s, with a plosive sound, such as t.
Voice disorders
A departure in the quality, pitch, loudness or resonance of the voice from what suits the speaker’s age and sex.
Augmentative and alternative communication
Means that supplement or replace speech, such as signs, pictures and speech-generating devices.
Tactile communication
Communication that relies on touch, such as signs the child feels with a hand placed over the speaker’s hand.
Pre-symbolic communication
A stage at which the child communicates through body movements, facial expressions and actions, before words, signs and pictures.
Dialectal difference
Pronunciation that matches the child’s dialect and community; it is not considered a disorder even when it differs from Standard Arabic.

Dynamic assessment, accommodation, cut-off score and norming sample are terms defined in Chapters 2, 3 and 5.
