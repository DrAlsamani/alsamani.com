Twice-exceptional learners

A student who is gifted and has a disability at the same time: the phrase may sound contradictory to anyone who sees giftedness and disability as opposite poles. Yet reality shows them coexisting in a single student, each concealing the other, so that the student falls between two systems, neither of which sees him fully as he is.

Scene from a meeting that never took place

Imagine two meetings at the same intermediate school in the same week. In the first, the gifted program team reviews the list of students whose performance has slipped and proposes withdrawing Yasser from the program: “He didn’t hand in his research project report, and his written test scores aren’t what you’d expect from a student in the program.” In the second, the learning disabilities team discusses the list of struggling students, and Yasser’s name never comes up: “He reads well, his Arabic grades are average, and he doesn’t need services.”

The two teams never met. Had they done so, they would have seen a student who solves math problems taught in secondary school and explains the idea behind his project orally with a fluency that astonishes his teachers, then spends a full hour writing a single paragraph about it, riddled with errors.

Yasser is fourteen, in the third year of intermediate school (ninth grade), and has been in the gifted program since fourth grade. His teacher in the program noticed that the gap between what he says and what he writes widens year after year: he avoids writing reports, asks to present his project orally, and his papers are full of spelling errors no one would expect from a student at his grade level. In this chapter we show why his difficulty was nearly overlooked and how to build an assessment that captures both sides at once, and then we return to his full case in Section 13.8.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

  1. Define twice-exceptional learners using the two reference definitions, and identify the difference between them.
  2. Classify a student in a given description into one of the three groups, and explain how each of his two sides masks the other.
  3. Identify what each of the usual identification models misses, and compare the arguments for normative comparison and intra-individual comparison.
  4. Design an assessment protocol that opens both the giftedness and the disability tracks together for a given student, and justify each of its components.
  5. Interpret an uneven cognitive profile using the difference between the General Ability Index and the Cognitive Proficiency Index, and explain why no judgment can rest on it alone.
  6. Determine, in a given case, which of three possibilities is most likely—giftedness only, giftedness with a disorder, or a disorder only—and state the deciding evidence.
  7. Judge whether the evidence in a new case suffices for a determination, and justify that judgment.

Introduction

In twice-exceptional learners, giftedness and disability coexist, each concealing the other, so that their performance often appears average and catches the attention of neither the gifted team nor the special education team. Their cases therefore fall into the zone where the usual models break down: a student may be proposed for withdrawal from the gifted program because the disability has lowered his performance, and excluded from learning disabilities services because his giftedness has lifted his achievement to the average range—just as the opening scene portrays through two meetings that never converged. Judgment in such cases requires applying the criteria for each side separately, reading the uneven profile systematically, and knowing when a discrepancy counts as evidence and when it does not.

We open the chapter by defining twice-exceptional learners and locating them within the book’s chapters (Section 13.1), then present masking and the three groups (Section 13.2), the reasons the usual models fail these students (Section 13.3), and the debate over identification criteria (Section 13.4). We then take up the assessment protocol, the reading of an uneven cognitive profile, and the adaptation of identification tools for learners with sensory and physical disabilities (Section 13.5), followed by the decision pathway that opens both tracks together (Section 13.6) and the three possibilities in differential diagnosis (Section 13.7). We apply all of this to four cases (Section 13.8) and close with the Saudi and Arab context (Section 13.9).

13.1Who are twice-exceptional learners?

Twice-exceptional (Twice-exceptional, 2e) learners are students who show potential for high performance or creative productivity in one or more domains and who, at the same time, have one or more disabilities under recognized eligibility criteria (Reis et al., 2014). The literature stresses that the combination of the two exceptionalities produces a unique situation that is more than the sum of the two conditions: giftedness changes how the disability presents, the disability changes how the giftedness presents, and the student needs instruction that attends to both sides at once (Baldwin et al., 2015). Two definitions are widely cited in the literature, and they serve different functions:

Two reference definitions
The operational definition (Reis et al., 2014)

It specifies who counts as twice-exceptional: a student who shows potential for high achievement or creative productivity in one or more domains and who has one or more disabilities according to recognized eligibility criteria, such as learning disabilities, speech and language disorders, emotional and behavioral disorders, physical disabilities, autism spectrum disorder, and health impairments such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. By tying the category to existing criteria, the definition is easy to apply in schools.

The community of practice consensus definition (2014; in Baldwin et al., 2015)

It describes how the two exceptionalities interact and what the student needs: exceptional ability may dominate the disability, may conceal it, or may itself be concealed by it. These students need specialized identification methods that take the interaction between the two sides into account; dual differentiated instruction that develops their talents while meeting their learning needs; concurrent services that include the social and emotional domain; and teachers prepared to work with them.

The first, then, is a definition for eligibility, answering the question “Is this student one of them?”; the second is a definition for practice, answering the question “What does that mean for how we assess and teach him?” The assessment team needs both: the first in order to decide, and the second in order to know where to look and why the usual tools fall short.

Their number is not precisely known, because definitions and identification methods vary widely across studies and because many of these students are never identified at all (Foley Nicpon et al., 2011). Among recent studies, one examined the cognitive and achievement characteristics of students from a U.S. national sample who were identified as potentially twice-exceptional (Maddocks, 2020). In the Kingdom, an exploratory study of 6,875 students in grades 2 through 12 at four private schools in Riyadh estimated that about 5.9% of them may be twice-exceptional: 23.6% showed a discrepancy of more than 1.5 standard deviations between their performance in two subjects, and 24.5% of these then appeared possibly gifted (Almulhim et al., 2026). This is a preliminary estimate based on indicators of discrepancy in achievement, and it would need individual diagnostic assessment to confirm. It does not estimate the prevalence of the group in Saudi schools, but it does signal that the group may be less rare than is commonly assumed.

Recent literature favors planning for these students that begins with their talents and interests rather than their deficits, an approach known as the strength-based, talent-focused approach (Baum et al., 2014). The examples in this chapter include Yasser, gifted in mathematics with a writing difficulty; Joud, linguistically gifted with autism spectrum disorder; and Faris, gifted in mathematics with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Set against them is Muhannad, a gifted student some of whose characteristics resemble a disorder.

13.2Masking and the three groups

How does each side mask the other?

One way to picture it is as two forces pulling the student’s performance in opposite directions: giftedness lifts it, and the disability drags it down. What the teacher sees in the grades is the net result of this tug-of-war, and it may well be an average performance that draws no one’s attention.

The three groups

Because of this masking, twice-exceptional learners appear in schools in three groups (Brody & Mills, 1997; Baum & Owen, 2004):

The claim that the third group is the largest is a common hypothesis in the literature (Baum & Owen, 2004) that still awaits proof, since by their very nature we have no prevalence data on students who go unidentified. Yasser, in the scene that opens this chapter, belongs to the first group: he is in the gifted program, and his writing difficulty is invisible to anyone who hears him speak—so much so that he was nearly withdrawn from the program over the reports he never handed in. Had he never been nominated for the program in the first place, he would have belonged to the third group.

13.3Why do the usual models fail them?

To identify learning disabilities and giftedness, schools rely on familiar models, among them response to intervention, which treats a student’s failure to improve with instruction of graduated intensity as an indicator of difficulty; the criteria for specific learning disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR), which require the skill to be clearly below what is expected for the student’s age (APA, 2022); and the admission criteria of gifted programs. When we examine these models, we find that each of them may miss the twice-exceptional student (Chapter 6 discusses identification models in detail). Researchers warned early on that relying on the response to intervention model alone may hide these students, because its screening looks for those who fall below their peers (Crepeau-Hobson & Bianco, 2011):

13.4The debate over identification criteria

The most important scholarly debate in this area turns on a single question: what do we compare the student’s performance against?

One study compared different methods of identifying gifted students with learning disabilities within a single sample and found that the methods differed considerably in how many students they identified and in which students, with limited agreement among them (Maddocks, 2018). In other words, who counts as twice-exceptional depends in part on the criterion we choose.

A balanced practical position

The gap between ability and achievement is not sufficient on its own, since everyone has uneven abilities. Nor is normatively low performance sufficient on its own, because it misses these students. The position toward which good practice leans is to base judgment on the gap together with converging evidence: a history of early difficulty in the same skill; processing indicators that explain it, such as weak phonological awareness in reading or weak orthographic knowledge in writing; a genuine functional impact, such as double effort, avoidance, or exhaustion; the persistence of the difficulty despite appropriate instruction targeting it; and a skill that actually lies in the low-average range or near the threshold, not merely below the student’s other abilities.

13.5Assessment: the protocol and the uneven profile

Reading an uneven cognitive profile

Many twice-exceptional learners show a recurring pattern on intelligence scales such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fifth Edition (WISC-V): very high reasoning and verbal comprehension, alongside clearly lower working memory and processing speed. These two indexes enter into the calculation of the Full Scale IQ and pull it down. For this reason, identifying giftedness in these students relies on the General Ability Index (General Ability Index, GAI), which is calculated without these two indexes (NAGC, 2018). Chapter 12 (Section 12.4) explained this index, the ceiling effect, and above-level testing in detail.

What this chapter adds is that the index has a counterpart that completes it: the Cognitive Proficiency Index (Cognitive Proficiency Index, CPI), calculated from the working memory and processing speed subtests—that is, from the efficiency with which the student processes information, independent of his reasoning ability. Comparing the two indexes is the systematic way to describe an uneven profile, rather than looking at the graph and judging by eye (Kaufman et al., 2016). Two questions are asked, in order:

  1. Is the difference statistically significant? That is, is it larger than measurement error alone would produce? This is settled by consulting the tables in the manual.
  2. Is it rare? Many statistically significant differences are common in the norm sample, and their presence signals nothing unusual. We therefore look at the proportion of the norm sample who showed a difference of this size, that is, the base rate.

When a student reaches the ceiling on several subtests, ordinary scores do not reveal the full extent of his ability. Extended norms have been published for the WISC-V Full Scale IQ and General Ability Index that allow scores above the ceiling of the standard norms; the publisher should be consulted on the conditions for their use. But the uneven profile as a whole, however wide its gap, is not diagnostic: it appears in many gifted students with no disability whatsoever, and it is not sufficient on its own for any judgment.

Principles of assessment

  1. Two lenses in one assessment: The assessment involves both someone who knows giftedness and someone who knows disability, or else an assessor who brings both kinds of expertise. Two questions are asked about the student together: What are his abilities? And what is holding him back?
  2. Start with strength: What the student excels at is measured with high-ceiling tools, such as above-level testing, performance tasks, and authentic products, rather than grade-level tools on which he reaches the highest possible score and which therefore cannot reveal how far his ability extends (see Chapter 12, Section 12.4).
  3. Measure skill by time and effort: Many twice-exceptional learners arrive at the correct answer eventually. Performance is therefore measured once under time limits and once untimed, and the assessor observes the effort the student expends and the compensatory strategies he relies on.
  4. Developmental and educational history: When did the difficulty first appear? How did the student compensate for it? Did his performance drop when demands rose at a particular stage, such as the move to intermediate school, when writing and self-regulation demands multiply? What instruction has he received in the area of his difficulty, and how did he respond to it?
  5. Guard against overinterpretation: No judgment rests on a gap between indexes alone, nor on analyzing each subtest in isolation. The Full Scale IQ and the General Ability Index are more reliable than any single index, and the difference between two indexes is less reliable than either of them.
  6. Emotional aspects: Twice-exceptional learners are vulnerable to frustration and a poor self-concept, because they know they are capable yet cannot understand why they keep stumbling. This side is therefore assessed and taken into account in the plan.

Assessment protocol profile

No single tool reveals twice-exceptionality. What reveals it is a protocol: a set of tools planned in advance and selected according to the referral question, with each component answering a specific question. The profile below presents this protocol in the same format used for instruments in the chapters on specific categories.

The idea

Both tracks are opened together from the outset: one track searches for strength with tools unconstrained by a ceiling, and the other searches for difficulty with tools that expose what compensation hides. How are the results interpreted?

The results are read in three layers. First, are the criteria for giftedness met on an index that the difficulty does not depress? Second, are the criteria for the disorder met, with evidence from history, processing, functional impact, and persistence? Third, does the evidence from different sources converge, or does some of it contradict the rest, so that the team needs additional evidence before reaching a judgment?

Its limits

The protocol lengthens the assessment and requires a team that is not available in every school. The large number of tools raises the likelihood that a low score will appear by chance, so no judgment should rest on a single score. Most of its tools are standardized on foreign samples, so their scores are read with caution where Arabic norms are unavailable.

Adapting gifted identification tools for learners with sensory and physical disabilities

A deaf or blind student, or a student with a physical disability, may be gifted, yet the usual identification tools measure the disability before they measure the ability. A written verbal reasoning test measures a deaf student’s command of written Arabic; a test of printed matrices is inaccessible to a blind student; and a timed test measures the hand speed of a student with a physical disability before it measures his thinking. The following should therefore be observed:

  • For deaf and hard-of-hearing students: Instructions are given in sign language or in whatever mode the student uses to communicate, and nonverbal ability measures are used, such as the Universal Nonverbal Intelligence Test, Second Edition (UNIT2; Bracken & McCallum, 2016). Nonverbal tests, however, are weaker predictors of academic achievement and may miss students who excel in verbal and quantitative reasoning (Lohman, 2005), so they are supplemented with the student’s products and with ratings from teachers who know him.
  • For blind and low-vision students: Oral verbal measures are relied on, and materials are presented in braille or large print. Any adaptation that changes how an item is presented may change what it measures, so the score is not compared with the original norms unless the adaptation is approved in the tool’s manual.
  • For students with physical disabilities: Alternative response modes are permitted, such as pointing, dictating to a scribe, or using a computer; tests that depend on motor speed are excluded from the judgment, and this is stated explicitly in the report.

The general rule is this: an adaptation that removes a barrier unrelated to the ability being measured improves the validity of the result, whereas an adaptation that changes the measured ability itself invalidates comparison with the norms. In either case, the adaptation is documented in the report. As for the Mawhiba Multiple Cognitive Abilities Test, administered within the National Program for Gifted Identification (Ministry of Education, 1437 AH [Arabic]), we found no description, in the published sources we consulted, of the accommodations available in it for students with disabilities. It is therefore advisable for the family or the school to ask the administering body about them before registration, and for the performance of a student with a disability on the test to be read in light of what he was given or denied.

For the teacher: signs worth noticing
  • A wide gap between what the student says orally and what he writes.
  • A gifted student who declines on moving to a stage with heavier writing and self-regulation demands, such as intermediate school.
  • A deep grasp of complex ideas alongside stumbling over simple skills such as spelling or math facts.
  • Erratic performance: excellent on one day or task and weak on others, for no apparent reason.
  • Severe fatigue or strong avoidance of particular tasks, alongside enthusiasm for others.
  • Frustration and remarks like “I’m stupid” from a student who strikes you as very bright.

Your role is to document what you see with examples, situations, and dates; to refer the student to both teams together; and to leave the judgment of “gifted only” or “has a difficulty only” to the integrated assessment.

13.6The decision pathway: opening both tracks together

In the chapters on specific categories, the decision pathway runs along a single line, from initial indicators to matching against the criteria of one category. With a student suspected of being twice-exceptional, however, the most common error is to open one track and close the other prematurely: the student is admitted to the gifted program and no one asks about his difficulty, or he is diagnosed with a disability and no one asks about his giftedness. For this reason, after the second step the pathway branches into two lenses that work together and then converge in a single reading of the profile before the decision.

The crucial point in the fifth step is that the team must not “go easy” on one side’s criteria because the other side’s are met. Giftedness does not prove a disorder, and a disorder does not rule out giftedness. What changes for the twice-exceptional student is the evidence accepted for each criterion, while the criterion itself stays fixed: the General Ability Index is used in place of the Full Scale IQ for the giftedness criterion, and for the difficulty criterion the skill is read under time limits and in terms of the effort expended.

13.7Differential diagnosis: three possibilities

When a student shows signs suggestive of both giftedness and a disorder, the team faces three possibilities, all of them plausible. It may be giftedness only, some of whose characteristics resemble a disorder when the student finds nothing suited to him (Webb et al., 2005). It may be giftedness with a disorder, which is twice-exceptionality. Or it may be a disorder only, with a relative strength in one part of the profile that does not rise to the level of giftedness. Error is possible in all three directions, and every error has its cost.

Caution: “What happens when he finds challenge?” is a helpful question, not a decisive one

Comparing a student’s behavior in an ordinary lesson with his behavior in a lesson that offers genuine challenge is a useful comparison, but it does not settle the matter by itself. The diagnostic manual states that signs of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder may be minimal or absent when the child is receiving frequent rewards for appropriate behavior, is under close supervision, is in a novel setting, or is engaged in especially interesting activities (APA, 2022). A student’s ability to concentrate for hours on an activity he loves therefore does not rule out the disorder. What tips the balance toward one possibility or the other is the convergence of evidence: ratings from two settings, developmental history, functional impact at school and at home, and what remains of the behavior after weeks of appropriate placement. These carry more weight than an hour in a club he loves.

13.8When is a judgment warranted? Four cases

The four cases that follow cover the three possibilities set out in the previous section. In three of them, the criteria for both sides are met through different kinds of evidence: a writing difficulty, autism, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. In one, Muhannad’s case (Case 2), the evidence is not yet sufficient for any judgment about a disorder.

Case 1: Yasser, age 14, ninth grade (third year of intermediate school)

Giftedness with a writing difficulty

The story: Yasser, whose scene opened this chapter, has been in the gifted program since fourth grade and trains with his school’s team for mathematics competitions. At the start of ninth grade, his program teacher referred him after he failed to submit the report for his research project, even though his oral presentation of the idea had astonished the judging panel. His family reported that he had been slow to learn letter formation and spelling in first and second grade, and that he “used to write words the way he said them”; they were told he would improve with time. The gap then widened in intermediate school, as reports and essay answers multiplied, and his older brother began writing down what Yasser dictated to him.

How did the team read the evidence? Yasser reads both vowelized and unvowelized text accurately, and this was the first thing that set his case apart from dyslexia. His writing, however, breaks down at two points where difficulty is common in Arabic: orthographic knowledge, such as the rules of the hamza and tāʾ marbūṭa and distinguishing letters that differ only in their dots; and the distance between his spoken dialect and written Standard Arabic. Comparing copying with dictation, free composition and oral presentation further showed that his hand is not where the problem lies (Chapter 8, in Sections 8.3 and 8.4, set out the characteristics of Arabic reading and writing and the logic of this comparison).

Decision: The evidence that the balanced position calls for had come together: a large discrepancy, an early history, an orthographic processing indicator, functional impact, persistence despite intervention, and a skill below the threshold. The team therefore concluded that Yasser is twice-exceptional: gifted in mathematics, with a specific learning disorder with impairment in written expression, affecting spelling accuracy and the clarity and organization of written expression, at a mild level. He remains in the gifted program and receives explicit instruction in spelling grounded in root and pattern, and in planning a text before writing it, with accommodations: word processing for long assignments, speech-to-text for project drafts, extra time, and assessment of content in subjects other than Arabic independently of spelling. His profile is also discussed with him openly, so that he understands that his struggles with writing do not cancel out his ability.

On the term “dysgraphia”

The term dysgraphia is used in the literature in two senses: a difficulty with handwriting and the physical act of writing, and a difficulty with spelling and written expression. The diagnostic manual speaks instead of a specific learning disorder “with impairment in written expression,” and does not count poor handwriting alone as part of it (APA, 2022). Yasser’s difficulty is of the second kind, which is why the solution was not to improve his handwriting.

What evidence could change the decision? If, after a semester of explicit instruction, his free writing samples showed his spelling errors falling to the level of his peers without accommodations, the description of the disorder and its severity would be revisited. And if a full evaluation by an occupational therapist revealed a motor impairment that the screening had missed, the possibility of developmental coordination disorder would come back under consideration.

Case 2: Muhannad, age 9, fourth grade

Giftedness that resembles an attention disorder: the evidence is not yet sufficient

The story: Muhannad was referred for evaluation on suspicion of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. In class he does not finish his exercises, interrupts the teacher with off-topic questions and doodles in the margins of his notebook. His teacher’s ratings on an attention rating scale are elevated. Before reaching any judgment, and after confirming that his hearing and vision were normal, the team gathered the following evidence over six weeks.

How did the team read the evidence? It would be inaccurate to say that the symptoms “appear in only one setting.” Criterion C in the diagnostic manual requires only that several symptoms be present in two or more settings, without requiring the full threshold in each, and three symptoms at home may satisfy it (APA, 2022). What prevents a judgment at this point is two other things. The first is the absence of clear functional impact outside the classroom, whereas Criterion D requires that the symptoms interfere with, or reduce the quality of, functioning. The second is a strong alternative explanation that the manual itself mentions under differential diagnosis: individuals of high ability may appear inattentive in an environment that does not challenge them. This explanation is strengthened by the onset’s link to placement and by the absence of any symptoms before school. Yet the decline in the behavior in the challenging lesson and at the chess club is not enough to rule out the disorder, since this also happens when the disorder is present.

Decision: The evidence is not yet sufficient either to diagnose attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or to rule it out definitively. Muhannad is therefore identified as gifted and accelerated in mathematics (Acceleration), that is, moved through its curriculum at a pace that suits his level. The systematic observation and the teacher and family rating scales are repeated after 8 to 12 weeks of appropriate placement. If the symptoms decline in class and there is still no functional impact at home, the “giftedness only” possibility gains weight. If the symptoms persist in two settings with functional impact, the evaluation of the disorder is completed as a possible case of twice-exceptionality.

The lesson

Not every gifted student who struggles is twice-exceptional. Some characteristics of giftedness, such as boredom with repetition, constant questioning and absorption in ideas, may resemble a disorder when the student finds nothing suited to him (Webb et al., 2005). The question “What happens when he finds the right level of challenge?” is a supporting question, answered through actual placement and follow-up over several weeks, and it remains one piece of corroborating evidence to be weighed alongside others.

What evidence could change the decision? If his off-task behavior in class remained high after 8 to 12 weeks of acceleration, or if functional impact emerged at home where none had existed before, such as late homework or lost belongings, the balance would tip toward completing the evaluation of the disorder. And if kindergarten records turned up notes on attention or activity level that had not reached the team, the argument for an onset tied to placement would be weakened.

Case 3: Joud, age 15, tenth grade (first year of secondary school)

Verbal giftedness with autism, hidden for years

The story: Joud was identified as gifted in elementary school for her verbal talent; today she writes metrical poetry and has won a literary competition at the regional level. Her teachers describe her as “polite, quiet, outstanding.” Her name did not reach the assessment team through the gifted program but through the school counselor: she had been repeatedly absent on Sunday mornings with headaches, had begun spending recess alone in the learning resource center, and then broke down in tears after her only friend moved to another group. The counselor assumed she was dealing with social anxiety or depression. Her mother said that Joud “shuts down” after school, closing her bedroom door for hours without speaking to anyone. Joud herself told the counselor: “I study my classmates the way I study texts: I memorize what they say, pick out what fits the situation, and practice my facial expressions in the mirror.”

How did the team read the evidence? The differential question was whether this was social anxiety alone. A person with social anxiety has social skills that fear holds back, and interacts naturally with familiar people. Joud’s difficulty with social reciprocity, by contrast, has been evident since before age three, including with her family, and it comes with an intense interest, sensory sensitivity and an aversion to change. The anxiety here co-occurs with autism; it is not a better explanation for it. Her identification was delayed until adolescence because her verbal talent and her powers of observation allowed her to camouflage her traits, and because her intense interest, in poetry and prosody, is one that school praises and that does not seem unusual.

Decision: Joud was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder, without intellectual impairment and without language impairment, at support level 1, the lightest of the three support levels in the diagnostic manual, with co-occurring anxiety for which she was referred for evaluation and treatment. She is twice-exceptional. The team designed services that bring both sides together, an approach known as dual differentiation: advanced enrichment in literary writing with a specialist mentor who follows her work; a quiet space available at recess; explicit teaching of what is expected in social situations, rather than leaving her to work it out alone through exhausting effort; and a conversation with the family about the after-school “shutting down” as the price she pays for camouflaging throughout her school day.

Camouflaging among gifted autistic girls

Some autistic individuals, and girls in particular, learn to camouflage their traits (Camouflaging): they watch others, imitate how they speak and their facial expressions, and memorize ready-made phrases for different situations (Hull et al., 2017; see also Chapter 9, Section 9.4). Verbal giftedness makes this camouflaging more polished and longer-lasting, so that a student can reach secondary school without anyone asking about autism, while her anxiety and exhaustion are seen as the whole problem. For this reason, in assessing this group the team gives particular weight to early developmental history, to discrepancies between home and school ratings, and to what the student herself says about the effort she spends to appear “normal.” A low score in a structured observation session is not reassuring: the session is short and structured, precisely the kind of situation in which the student camouflages most skillfully.

What evidence could change the decision? If early records or an interview with another relative showed that the indicators before age three were not as her mother described them, or if her social reciprocity improved markedly after treatment for anxiety, social anxiety would return as the leading explanation, and the autism diagnosis would need to be revisited.

Decision: The team concluded that the evidence favors attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, predominantly inattentive presentation, and that Faris is twice-exceptional. He was referred to a physician to complete the medical evaluation and to discuss treatment options with the family, since the Special Education Organizational Guide requires, for eligibility in this category, that the student’s need for intervention be established “according to an approved report” (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). Faris remains in the gifted program, with support for executive functions: breaking each project into short stages, each with its own deadline and review; a checklist against which he verifies the steps of his solution before submitting; external organizational tools such as a calendar and reminders; and teaching him to monitor his own attention.

The lesson: What distinguished Faris from Muhannad?

Both are gifted in mathematics, and both had teachers who rated their inattention symptoms as elevated. But Faris’s symptoms remained far above his peers’ even in a session he enjoys and that challenges him; they appeared in three settings, predated school, and affected his life at home. Muhannad’s behavior, by contrast, dropped markedly with challenge, with no functional impact at home and no symptoms before school. What made the difference was the convergence of evidence from different sources.

What evidence could change the decision? If the medical evaluation revealed another cause for the symptoms, such as a sleep disorder the family had not noticed or a health condition affecting attention, the conclusion would be revisited. And if ratings from other teachers who know him well in different subjects came back clearly low, the requirement that symptoms appear in more than one setting would be called into question and would need to be examined anew.

13.9In the Saudi and Arab context

In the Saudi context: Can a student receive both gifted program services and learning disabilities services?

The short answer: We found no published regulatory text that explicitly permits a student to combine a gifted program with a special education service, nor any text that prohibits it. The Special Education Organizational Guide lists eleven disability categories and does not address the gifted (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). The gifted program guides we reviewed regulate nomination and admission and make no mention of students with disabilities (Ministry of Education, n.d., 1437 AH [Arabic]). In practice, then, the matter is left to the judgment of teams in schools and education directorates.

Even so, the texts contain provisions that open the door rather than close it. The Regulations for Special Education Institutes and Programs defined both “superior ability and giftedness” and “learning disabilities” in a single document, set separate admission requirements for each, and did not make giftedness a reason to exclude a learning disability. Moreover, the admission requirement for learning disabilities in the Regulations is “a clear discrepancy between the student’s level of ability and level of academic achievement” in areas that include writing. This is the logic of intra-individual comparison, which suits twice-exceptional learners better than comparison with peers, although the Regulations do not specify a figure for this discrepancy (Ministry of Education [Al-Ma'arif], 1422 AH [Arabic]). Gifted classes, for their part, require meeting two or more criteria, one of which is a cognitive abilities test with a score of no less than 120 (Ministry of Education, n.d. [Arabic]), and the guide does not specify whether this refers to the full-scale score or to an index that the difficulty does not depress.

Nor may the group be rare, as the preliminary estimate in Section 13.1 suggests (Almulhim et al., 2026). Saudi researchers have studied the experiences of teachers in the Hail region with these students (Alsamiri & Alsamani, 2023), teachers’ professional development needs for identifying them in inclusive schools (Alsamiri & Alsamani, 2026), and the barriers that elementary school teachers see to identifying and supporting them (Alsamani et al., 2026).

A question for reflection: If Yasser’s case came before your school tomorrow, who would request his evaluation: the gifted team or the learning disabilities team? And who has the authority to decide that he receives both services at once?

Conclusion

The central argument of this chapter is that twice-exceptional learners are identified through a multi-instrument protocol that opens the giftedness and disability tracks together and applies the criteria for each separately. It follows that response to intervention, normative criteria or the “and” rule cannot be expected to identify these students; that the difference between the General Ability Index and the Cognitive Proficiency Index should be read as a systematic description of an uneven profile, one that precedes any diagnosis and requires further evidence; and that early history and home–school discrepancies deserve particular weight, especially for gifted autistic girls, who may camouflage their traits for years. In every case, the team faces three possibilities: giftedness only, giftedness with a disorder, or a disorder only.

Debate continues over the basis for judgment: normative comparison or intra-individual comparison. The balanced position presented in this chapter combines the discrepancy with history, processing indicators, functional impact and persistence despite appropriate instruction, but it remains a matter of professional judgment that has not yet settled into a rule. Two further questions also remain open: the claim that students in whom neither side has been detected form the largest group is a common hypothesis that awaits proof, and no published Saudi regulatory text has been found that either permits or prohibits combining a gifted program with learning disabilities services.

13.10Summary, key terms and activities

Chapter summary

  • Twice-exceptional learners are gifted and also have a disability. The operational definition specifies who they are, while the consensus definition describes the interaction between the two sides and what these students need.
  • Mutual masking makes their performance look average, so they appear in three groups. The claim that students in whom neither side has been detected form the largest group is a common hypothesis that has yet to be proven.
  • The usual models, such as response to intervention, normative criteria and the “and” rule, may miss them.
  • The debate centers on normative versus intra-individual comparison; the balanced position combines the discrepancy with history, processing indicators, functional impact and persistence despite appropriate instruction.
  • Twice-exceptional learners are identified through a multi-instrument protocol that opens both tracks together and applies the criteria for each side separately.
  • The difference between the General Ability Index and the Cognitive Proficiency Index describes an uneven profile systematically, but it does not diagnose on its own.
  • The team faces three possibilities: giftedness only, giftedness with a disorder, or a disorder only. The question “What happens when the student finds the right challenge?” is a supporting one, to be weighed with other evidence, because symptoms of a disorder may be absent during a preferred activity.
  • Gifted autistic girls may camouflage their traits for years, so early history and home–school discrepancies are given particular weight.
  • We found no published Saudi regulatory text that either permits or prohibits combining a gifted program with learning disabilities services.

Glossary of terms

Twice-exceptionalTwice-exceptional (2e)
A student who has high performance potential in one or more domains and, at the same time, one or more disabilities.
Masking
Giftedness compensating for the disability and the disability depressing the giftedness, so that performance appears average.
Normative comparison
Comparing a student’s performance with that of same-age peers.
Intra-individual comparison
Comparing a student’s performance in one domain with the student’s abilities in other domains.
Compensatory strategies
Methods a student uses to overcome a difficulty, such as memorization or reliance on context, which may conceal it.
Dual differentiation
Instruction that provides challenge in areas of strength and support in areas of difficulty at the same time.
Cognitive Proficiency IndexCognitive Proficiency Index (CPI)
A Wechsler index calculated from the working memory and processing speed subtests and compared with the General Ability Index to describe an uneven profile.
Assessment protocol
A set of instruments and procedures planned in advance, each answering a specific question in the assessment.
Camouflaging
An autistic person’s concealment of their traits by observing and imitating others and memorizing ready-made phrases, at an exhausting cost in effort.
Dysgraphia
A term used for a difficulty with handwriting or a difficulty with spelling and written expression; its counterpart in the diagnostic manual is specific learning disorder with impairment in written expression.
