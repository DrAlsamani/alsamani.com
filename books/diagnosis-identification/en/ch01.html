Chapter 1

Concepts of diagnosis and identification

Screening, identification, diagnosis and eligibility are four terms often used as if they were synonyms, yet they are in fact four distinct steps, each with its own question, its own tools and its own responsible party. In this chapter we sketch the conceptual map on which all the chapters of the book are organized, and we place it within its regulatory framework in Saudi Arabia.

Scene from the teachers' lounge

At the start of the school year, Munira, a teacher, received the files of the students in her third-grade class. In Abdullah's file was a hospital report stating that he had been “diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder.” Sultan's file held the result of a reading screening the school had conducted the previous month, marked “needs follow-up.” Reem's file was empty, but last year's teacher had left Munira a note: “Keep an eye on Reem. I think she's gifted.”

Munira wonders: Is Abdullah entitled to special education services because he has a diagnosis? Does Sultan have a learning disability because the screening flagged him? And is one teacher's opinion enough to consider Reem gifted?

The answer to all three questions is the same: “Not necessarily.” Why this is so becomes clear only once we draw careful distinctions between steps that are frequently confused in practice. That is the work of this chapter, and we will return to the three students at its close.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

  1. Determine the purpose of assessment in a given situation and the type of tool suited to it.
  2. Distinguish among screening, identification, diagnosis and eligibility determination by the question each step asks and the kind of evidence it requires.
  3. Compare the three models of disability in terms of the diagnostic question toward which each one points.
  4. Map the special education categories in Saudi regulations onto the categories of US law and onto medical classification systems.
  5. Identify which principle of fair assessment has been violated in a given assessment situation.
  6. Build a RIOT/ICEL matrix for gathering evidence about a referred student, indicating the source of each piece of information in it.
  7. Plan a conversation with a family about a teacher's concern, following a structured sequence of steps.
  8. Detect common errors in a given assessment situation and propose ways to avoid them.
  9. Judge whether the available evidence is sufficient to move from one step of the assessment pathway to the next, and justify that judgment.

Introduction

Every school year, teachers and assessment and diagnosis teams make decisions that bear directly on the paths of particular students: who is referred for assessment, who meets the criteria for a given category, and who needs special education services or a gifted program. Many of the errors in these decisions stem less from the limitations of the tools than from confusing distinct steps that go by similar names. A screening result is treated as a diagnosis, a medical diagnosis is taken as an educational eligibility decision, and a single teacher's opinion is deemed sufficient to judge a student gifted, just as Munira finds in the opening scene. For this reason we open the book by pinning down concepts before turning to tools and categories, since it is the question each step asks that determines the kind of evidence required, who is responsible for it, and how much of it is enough to move on to the next step.

We begin the chapter with the purposes of assessment, starting from the premise that assessment is a means of making decisions (Section 1.1). We then distinguish among screening, identification, diagnosis and eligibility determination (Section 1.2), and among measurement, testing and assessment (Section 1.3). Next we present the models of disability and the question toward which each points (Section 1.4), followed by medical and educational classification systems and the regulatory framework in Saudi Arabia (Section 1.5). We then turn to the principles of fair assessment (Section 1.6), the RIOT/ICEL framework for organizing sources of evidence (Section 1.7), and the roles of the team and the family (Section 1.8), and we close with the general assessment pathway and its common errors (Section 1.9).

1.1Why do we assess? The purposes of assessment

Assessment is a means of making a decision. The first question an assessor asks, therefore, is “What decision do I need to make?” Only after that comes the question “Which test should I give?” A tool that is excellent for one decision may be useless for another. Specialists in educational measurement identify several purposes of assessment, each with its own question (Salvia et al., 2017):

Table 1.1: Purposes of assessment, the question each purpose answers, and an example of a tool for it.
PurposeQuestion it answersExample of a tool
ScreeningWho needs a closer look?A short screening checklist given to all kindergarten children
Diagnosis and classificationDoes the student have a disability, a disorder or giftedness? Of what kind?A comprehensive battery: intelligence, achievement, adaptive behavior and observation
Eligibility determination and placementIs the student entitled to special services? In what setting should they be provided?A team decision based on the diagnosis and its impact on learning
Instructional planningWhat should we teach? Where should we start?Criterion-referenced tests (which compare a student's performance with a defined level of mastery rather than with the performance of peers), and error analysis
Progress monitoringIs the student learning? Is the plan working?Brief, repeated measures such as curriculum-based measurement (Curriculum-Based Measurement, CBM)
Program evaluationIs the program meeting its goals for its students as a whole?Aggregated data on the program's students across years

In this book we focus on the first two purposes, with some attention to the third. The teacher, however, is present in all of them, because diagnostic information must ultimately feed into instructional planning; otherwise it loses its value.

1.2Screening, identification, diagnosis and eligibility

Picture these four steps as a funnel: at the top are all students, and at the bottom are those who receive special services. The funnel narrows at each layer, because each step asks a more precise question than the one before it and requires stronger evidence.

Screening

Screening is applied to an entire group, such as all first graders, using a short tool that does not require a specialist. Its aim is to ensure that no child who needs a closer look slips past us. Screening tools are therefore preferably high in sensitivity (Sensitivity), that is, able to capture the largest possible proportion of those who actually have the problem, even if they also flag some who do not, because the cost of missing a child is greater than the cost of examining a child who turns out not to need it.

Sensitivity alone, however, is not enough to judge a screening tool. The tool also needs acceptable specificity (Specificity), meaning the ability to rule out those who do not have the problem; otherwise it will swamp the team with unnecessary referrals. A school principal will care about a third question as well: if a student's result is negative, how likely is it that the student truly does not have the problem? This is the negative predictive value (Negative predictive value), the most important question to ask of a screening, because it measures how confident we can be that we have not missed anyone. Its magnitude, like that of the positive predictive value, depends jointly on the properties of the tool and on the prevalence of the problem in the group screened (Chapter 2 works through these concepts with numbers in Section 2.6).

Misconception: “The screening flagged him, so the problem is confirmed”

A positive screening result is an invitation to look more closely; diagnosis is a later stage that requires broader evidence. Screening tools are deliberately designed to catch more than they should, and when a problem is rare in a group, many of those flagged by the screening may in fact be free of it. The reverse holds as well: a negative result is no certificate of health. If the teacher's or the family's concern persists, a documented observation is reason enough to move on to the identification stage.

Identification and referral

After screening, or after a teacher's observation, the identification stage (Identification) begins: the stage of gathering initial evidence. At this stage the teacher and the school try to provide support within the classroom, whether by adjusting the method of instruction or by offering additional small-group teaching, while monitoring the student's response. If the difficulty persists despite this support, the student is referred for a comprehensive assessment. Referral is a significant decision, because it signals that the school has tried what it can and that this has not been enough.

In the field of giftedness, this stage is usually called nomination. Teachers and families nominate students in whom they see indicators of giftedness, and those students are then assessed. Relying on nomination alone, however, misses many gifted students, because nominators tend to notice those who display their excellence clearly in achievement and behavior, and overlook those whose giftedness is hidden behind a second language, a disability or a quiet disposition (Chapter 12 addresses nomination in detail).

The concept of identification: Why is it different in the field of giftedness?

In this book we restrict “identification” to the stage that precedes referral, a terminological choice that gives each step of the pathway a name of its own. In the literature, however, the term Identification usually denotes the entire process of determination, up to and including the decision, and in the Arabic literature it overlaps with “al-kashf” (detection), as in the name of the “National Program for Gifted Identification,” which encompasses both testing and classification. Yet this broad sense does not carry the same content in the two fields. In the field of giftedness, identification is the complete process of determining who needs gifted services, and it is an educational process; the term “diagnosis” is avoided here because giftedness is neither a disorder nor a deficit whose cause must be sought. The process starts from the program's definition and goals, draws on multiple criteria and on local norms that compare students with peers in their own setting, and links the tool to the type of service offered: a measure of mathematical reasoning suits an enrichment program in mathematics but is not, on its own, appropriate for a program in creative writing. Its outcome is a decision to place a student in a program or to provide a service, one that is tied to the student's educational need and open to revision whenever the evidence or the program changes (National Association for Gifted Children [NAGC], 2019).

In the field of disabilities, by contrast, “identification” usually means determining eligibility for special education services under the categories recognized by the education system, as in the US Individuals with Disabilities Education Act and its implementing regulations published in the Code of Federal Regulations (IDEA, 2004; 34 C.F.R. §300.8). For some categories, such as autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, this educational decision is paralleled or preceded by a clinical diagnosis under the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). The educational decision thus meets the medical one without either replacing the other, as Section 1.5 explains in detail.

The two pathways converge in twice-exceptional learners: the same student may need a clinical diagnosis of a disability and an educational identification of giftedness, and each pathway may obscure what the other reveals if the team pursues one without the other (Chapter 13 examines this overlap in detail). So when you encounter either term in another source, infer its meaning from the context and the field, and do not assume that it refers to the narrow stage we intend here.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis answers the question: Does the student meet the criteria for a particular category? It requires a comprehensive team assessment using multiple tools and multiple sources. A diagnosis may be medical, issued by a physician or a qualified psychologist according to a diagnostic manual, or educational, issued by a school team according to education regulations.

Eligibility determination

Here we arrive at the distinction that puzzled Munira. Diagnosis answers the question “What does the student have?”, whereas eligibility answers the question “Does the student need special services in order to learn?” A student may have a medical diagnosis yet learn successfully in the general classroom without needing special services, and so not be eligible for them. Conversely, a student may need services without yet having a medical diagnosis, in which case the student is assessed educationally.

The equation that governs eligibility

In most education systems, eligibility for special education services requires two conditions together: the student must meet the criteria for one of the disability categories, and the disability must affect the student's learning to the point that he or she needs specially designed instruction. If either condition is absent, the student is not eligible, even if the other is met. Saudi regulations phrase the second condition in their own words: an impairment “to the extent that it necessitates the provision of special education services” (Ministry of Education [Al-Ma'arif], 1422 AH [Arabic]).

1.4Models of disability

Before we diagnose a disability, we must ask: What is disability in the first place? Where does the problem lie? How we answer these two questions is a stance with consequences, for it determines what we measure and where we look for solutions. There are three main models:

The medical model
The problem lies in the individual

Disability is a defect in the individual's body or functions, to be diagnosed and treated or compensated for. The focus is on the impairment and its causes.

In diagnosis: looking for symptoms, matching them against criteria, and determining severity.
The social model
The problem lies in the environment

Disability arises from the barriers society places in front of those who differ: buildings a wheelchair cannot reach, curricula that ignore individual differences. The focus is on removing barriers (Oliver, 1990).

In diagnosis: asking about the barriers in the student's environment and what needs to change around the student.
The biopsychosocial model
The problem lies in the interaction between the two

Disability results from an interaction between the individual's health condition and the environmental and personal factors surrounding him or her (Engel, 1977). This model is the foundation of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health issued by the World Health Organization (World Health Organization [WHO], 2001).

In diagnosis: describing the impairment, what the student is able to do, and the environmental factors that hinder or help.

It is often said that modern diagnosis follows the third model, but this claim is a generalization that needs qualifying. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR), remains at its core a categorical classification belonging to the medical model: the criteria are either met or not met, even though the manual adds severity specifiers and levels of support that move it closer to graded description (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). The biopsychosocial model, by contrast, underlies the International Classification of Functioning specifically, a descriptive classification that addresses functioning, participation and environmental factors and leaves the diagnosis of conditions to the medical systems. There is also growing discussion in the literature of dimensional models that describe psychological difficulties along graded continua rather than in discrete categories, one of the best known being the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP).

Good practice comes closest to combining the two logics: the assessor judges whether criteria are met using a categorical logic when the system requires it, and then describes the impact of the condition and its relationship to the environment using a biopsychosocial logic. When we say that a student with autism spectrum disorder “requires substantial support,” we are describing the student's relationship to the environment as well as what the student has. The same student may need less support in a calm, structured classroom and more in a crowded, changeable one.

1.5Classification systems

Diagnosis rests on classification systems that define categories and their criteria. The most important thing for a teacher to know is that there are two kinds of systems, medical and educational, and that each serves a different purpose.

Clinical diagnosis relies on one of the first two manuals (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; WHO, 2019, 2024). The two converge for most developmental categories, though they differ in naming and detail, differences taken up in the category chapters of Part Three.

An example of an educational system: the US categories

The US Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, IDEA) was reauthorized in 2004 as the Individuals with Disabilities Education Improvement Act (IDEIA), though it is still known by its original acronym. The law specifies thirteen categories eligible for services: autism, deaf-blindness, deafness, emotional disturbance, hearing impairment, intellectual disability, multiple disabilities, orthopedic impairment, other health impairment, specific learning disability, speech or language impairment, traumatic brain injury (resulting from a blow or impact), and visual impairment including blindness. The law also permits a developmental delay category for children aged 3 to 9 (IDEA, 2004).

Note three important points about this list:

  1. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder, ADHD) is not a separate category. A student diagnosed with it usually qualifies for services under the category of “other health impairment.” In other words, medical diagnoses and educational categories do not always coincide. Saudi regulations depart from this arrangement, as we will see in the next section.
  2. Giftedness is absent. The law concerns disabilities, and gifted services in the United States are governed by separate legislation. This explains why a twice-exceptional student, that is, a gifted student who also has a disability or disorder, falls between two systems that are not coordinated with each other (Chapter 13 discusses this group in detail).
  3. The labels differ from those of the medical manual. “Emotional disturbance” is a broad educational category that encompasses conditions bearing different names in the medical manual.

Identification of gifted students

The identification of gifted students in Saudi Arabia rests on two complementary pathways. The first is the “National Program for Gifted Identification,” which targets students from third grade through the first year of secondary school at three levels. Students may sit the test once at each level, and the program administers the “Mawhiba Multiple Mental Abilities Test” upon registration by the family (Mawhiba, n.d. [Arabic]). The second consists of the Ministry of Education's gifted classes, which draw their students from those classified as gifted by the national program. If places remain unfilled, the classes complete their numbers using four criteria: a mental abilities test, personal traits, academic achievement and creative products. A student must satisfy two or more criteria, one of which must be the mental abilities test with a score of at least 120 (Ministry of Education, n.d. [Arabic]). For a recent overview of gifted education in Saudi Arabia, see Alfaiz and colleagues (Alfaiz et al., 2022).

In the Saudi context: What the comparison reveals
  • Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is a separate category in Saudi Arabia. This brings the educational category closer to the medical diagnosis than the US system does, but it also makes the “accredited report” a cornerstone of eligibility, which raises the stakes for the quality of the assessment on which that report was based.
  • The learning disabilities model in the text rests on discrepancy, exclusion and nonresponse to general instruction. The text specifies neither the size of the discrepancy nor how nonresponse is to be established. Recent studies indicate that practices resembling the response to intervention model exist in Saudi schools, but that they are largely informal and lack systematic progress monitoring, and that the absence of formal adoption of the model is among the barriers to its implementation (Almulla & Mahama, 2026; Altheiabi et al., 2025).
  • Gifted students with disabilities fall between two documents. The Special Education Organizational Guide does not address gifted students, and the gifted guides we reviewed do not address students with disabilities. We found no published regulatory text that either permits or prohibits combining a gifted program with learning disabilities services. A recent exploratory study estimated that about 5.9% of a sample of 6,875 students in four private schools in Riyadh may be twice-exceptional, and called for policies specific to them (Almulhim et al., 2026).

The documents cited in this section were consulted in September 2026, and we found no published edition of the Organizational and Procedural Guides more recent than their first edition; readers should consult whichever edition is in force at the time of reading. Question for reflection: If you were invited to join a committee updating the Organizational Guide, which of these three gaps would you address first, and why?

1.6Principles of fair assessment

An assessment that ends in a diagnosis changes the course of a student's life: the school the student attends, the curriculum the student learns, how others see the student, and how the student sees himself or herself. For this reason, legislation and professional standards have set out principles that protect students from error and injustice. Among the clearest formulations are those in the implementing regulations of US law governing evaluation procedures (34 C.F.R. §300.304). The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing treat fairness as a third foundation of testing alongside validity and reliability, devoting their third chapter to it (American Educational Research Association [AERA] et al., 2014). Table 1.6 sets these principles against the provisions of Saudi regulations.

The comparison reveals the strength of Saudi regulations on the principles of multiple tools and examiner qualifications, and their silence on the language and mode of assessment, a silence with real consequences for the assessment of deaf students and of students whose native language is not Arabic. The consent rule deserves particular attention. In the professional literature, informed consent is an affirmative act that the family performs after it understands, and silence does not count as consent under the US definition (34 C.F.R. §300.9). Treating a lack of response as consent may streamline procedures, but it does not ensure that the family understood what it agreed to. The team would therefore do well, even when the rule allows it to proceed, to seek explicit consent and to arrange a meeting in which it explains what will take place.

These principles do not take the place of the ethical codes of the professions in which assessors work, such as the principles of the US National Association of School Psychologists (National Association of School Psychologists, 2020). Technical standards set out how a test is built and used; ethical codes set out what the assessor owes the student and the family (Chapter 2 discusses the difference between the two in detail).

1.7Sources of evidence: the RIOT/ICEL framework

How can we be sure that the evidence we have gathered is sufficient and varied? School psychology offers a simple, practical framework for this purpose, known by its acronym (RIOT/ICEL; Christ, 2008). The acronym is formed from the initial letters of its words: the first part (RIOT) covers the methods of gathering information (Review, Interview, Observe, Test), and the second part (ICEL) covers the domains (Instruction, Curriculum, Environment, Learner). The framework is a two-dimensional matrix: four methods of gathering information, applied across four domains in which the problem may lie.

  • The four methods: review, interview, observation and testing.
  • The four domains: instruction, curriculum, environment and the learner.

The central idea of the framework is that the problem may lie outside the student: in the way he is taught, in a curriculum pitched above his level, or in a noisy environment. For the teacher

Most of the cells in this matrix are in your hands before they are in the specialist's. You are the one who knows how the student is taught, the level of the curriculum and the classroom environment, and you hold the student's daily work. When you attach this information to the referral, you give the team half the picture before it begins.

1.8The team and the family

No single specialist has all the knowledge needed to understand a student fully. Diagnosis is therefore the work of a team, which, depending on the case, may include the psychologist, the special education teacher, the classroom teacher, the speech-language pathologist, the physician, the occupational therapist, the audiologist, the social worker and the family. Teams differ in how they work together:

The assessment team in Saudi regulations

The Regulations assign assessment and diagnosis to a team made up of the relevant special education teacher, the behavioral training teacher (the psychologist), the child's guardian, and anyone whose participation the case requires (Article 81). Among the psychologist's duties they list “conducting assessment and diagnosis” using formal tools, such as standardized intelligence scales and adaptive behavior scales, and informal tools, such as interviews, observation and checklists (Articles 48 and 49) (Ministry of Education [Al-Ma'arif], 1422 AH [Arabic]). The Organizational Guide widens the team to include the school principal, the vice principal for student affairs, the special education teacher, the general education teacher, the student counselor or social worker, specialists according to the disability category, the physician or nurse where available, and the student's guardian “if possible” (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH-a [Arabic]). The phrase “if possible” is weaker than what the Regulations themselves establish when they make the guardian a member of the team, and weaker than what this book holds in the next section.

The family as a partner in assessment and decision-making

The family has known the student since birth, and in situations no one else sees. Its role in assessment goes well beyond answering questions:

  • The holder of consent: Assessment does not begin without the family's informed consent, and the family has the right to ask questions and to object.
  • A source of developmental history: When the child walked, when he began to talk, and what changed and when.
  • A witness to behavior in a different setting: What the family sees at home may differ from what the teacher sees, and the difference is itself information.
  • A partner in the decision and the plan: The family attends the results meeting, understands the findings and takes part in setting goals.

The Regulations grant the family explicit rights in this regard: the guardian's permission must be obtained for diagnosis, for preparing the program and for approving any modification; the family is to be invited to take part in diagnosis, program planning and evaluation; their child's condition is to be explained to them “in understandable language”; and the student is to be rediagnosed at the family's request if they believe he has not been placed in the appropriate program (Article 76). The Regulations also require the family's participation in preparing, implementing, evaluating and monitoring the individualized education program (Ministry of Education [Al-Ma'arif], 1422 AH [Arabic]).

For the teacher: When you talk to the family about your concern
  1. Begin with what the student does well; the family needs to know that you see their child as a whole person.
  2. Describe what you have noticed in terms of specific behaviors and examples, and avoid labels: say “He doesn't turn when I call his name,” not “I think he has autism.”
  3. Ask: “Do you notice anything similar at home?” Then listen.
  4. Explain the next step and the reason for it: “I suggest that a specialist team assess him, so that we can understand how to help him.”
  5. Do not promise an outcome, and do not suggest a diagnosis to the family; that is outside your role, and it may be wrong.

1.9The general pathway and its common errors

Detailed procedures vary across countries and categories, but most systems follow a broadly similar pathway:

  1. Observation and screeningThe teacher or the family notices indicators, or the student is flagged in screening results.Teacher, family, screening team
  2. Classroom support and documentationInstructional adjustments and additional support for a set period, with the response measured and documented.Teacher, supported by the school team
  3. Referral and consentIf the response is insufficient, a referral is written with the evidence attached, and the family's consent is requested.Teacher, school administration, family
  4. Comprehensive assessmentThe team gathers evidence from multiple sources, using tools suited to each area of suspected need.Multidisciplinary team
  5. Decision meetingThe team and the family discuss the results and determine whether the criteria are met and whether the student is eligible for services.Team, family
  6. Plan, monitoring and reevaluationThe individualized education program (Individualized Education Program, IEP) is developed, progress is monitored, and the student is reevaluated periodically or as needed.Special education teacher, classroom teacher, family

Some systems set binding timelines for these steps. In the US system, for example, the initial evaluation must be completed within sixty days of parental consent, unless the state sets a different timeline (IDEA, 2004). The purpose of such timelines is to ensure that a student does not wait for months without services or a decision. Table 1.8 sets each step against the provisions of the two systems.

Common errors in diagnosis

Table 1.9: Common errors in diagnosis, how they occur and how to avoid them.
ErrorHow it occursHow to avoid it
Diagnostic overshadowingNew symptoms are attributed to a disability the student is already known to have, so that another disorder is missed; for example, anxiety in a student with an intellectual disability is explained away as “part of his disability” (Reiss et al., 1982).Asking every time: Does the known disability really explain this symptom?
Confirmation biasThe assessor starts from a hypothesis, sees the evidence that supports it and overlooks the evidence against it.   • Abdullah: He has a medical diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, but his eligibility for services is determined by the impact of the disorder on his learning. He may need extensive services, or only simple classroom accommodations, that is, changes in how he learns or is tested that leave the level of the curriculum untouched. The medical report in his file is evidence the assessment team can draw on in reaching the eligibility decision.
  • Sultan: The screening flagged him, which means he needs a closer look; concluding that he has a learning disability would be premature. The next step is reading support and monitoring of his response, before any referral.
  • Reem: The teacher's opinion is a useful nomination and the starting point for identification. Conclusion

The argument we have developed in this chapter holds that assessment begins with the decision to be made and then selects its tool, and that each step in the pathway has its own question: screening sorts, identification gathers initial evidence and refers, diagnosis matches criteria, and eligibility determines the need for services. It follows that no step should be asked for what it cannot provide. A screening result does not establish a disability, a medical diagnosis does not automatically confer educational eligibility, and a teacher's opinion is the beginning of identification. It follows, too, that evidence should be gathered from multiple tools and sources, that instruction, curriculum and environment should be examined before a shortfall is attributed to the student, and that the family should be a partner in the decision as well as a source of information.

Some of what this chapter has presented, however, is a matter of terminology or of judgment. Using “identification” for the stage before referral is a terminological choice we have adopted for this book; the meaning of the term differs between the field of giftedness and the field of disabilities; medical and educational categories do not always coincide; and combining criterion-based judgment with a description of functioning in context calls for professional judgment that no ready-made rule can supply. Deciding whether the evidence is sufficient to move from one step to the next remains a professional judgment, one that must be reasoned and that weighs the numbers, the documents and all the other evidence.

1.10Summary, glossary and activities

Chapter summary

  • Assessment is a means to a decision, and its first question is: What decision do we want to make?
  • Screening sorts, identification gathers initial evidence and refers, diagnosis matches criteria, and eligibility determines the need for services. Using “identification” for the stage before referral is a terminological choice made for this book.
  • A screening tool is judged by its sensitivity and its specificity together, and its negative predictive value tells us how confident we can be that we have not missed anyone.
  • Medical diagnosis and educational eligibility are two different questions, and medical and educational categories do not always coincide.
  • The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual is categorical at its core, the biopsychosocial model underpins the International Classification of Functioning, and good practice combines criterion-based judgment with a description of functioning in context.
  • In Saudi Arabia, diagnosis is governed by the Regulations for Special Education Institutes and Programs (1422 AH) and the Special Education Organizational Guide (1436–1437 AH); the Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (1445 AH) establishes the right to diagnosis and early detection; and the identification of gifted students follows a separate pathway.
  • Fair assessment draws on multiple tools and sources, is conducted in the student's language, uses tools valid for their purpose, and rests on the family's informed consent.
  • The RIOT/ICEL framework is a reminder that the problem may lie in instruction, curriculum or the environment, as well as in the student.

Glossary of terms

Screening
A quick procedure applied to an entire group to determine who needs a more in-depth assessment.
Identification
In this book: the stage of gathering initial evidence and attempting support within the classroom, in preparation for a referral decision. In much of the literature, the term denotes the entire process of determination up to the decision; in the field of giftedness, it is a multi-criteria educational process for determining who needs gifted services, not a clinical diagnosis.
Identification; detection
A term that overlaps in the Arabic literature with “identification” and is generally used for the process of locating and identifying gifted students or students with disabilities, as in the “National Program for Gifted Identification” and the “early detection of disability.”
Referral
A formal request for a comprehensive assessment of the student, accompanied by the evidence and the family's consent.
Nomination
A proposal by a teacher, the family or the student himself that a student be assessed within a gifted identification program.
Diagnosis
A judgment that the criteria for a particular category apply to the student, based on a comprehensive assessment.
Eligibility
A decision on whether the student is entitled to special services, based on the diagnosis and its impact on his learning.
Sensitivity
The ability of a tool to capture those who actually have the problem; a property strongly required of screening tools.
Specificity
The ability of a tool to rule out those who do not have the problem.
Negative predictive value
The proportion of those with a negative result who are actually free of the problem; it is affected by the prevalence of the problem in the group.
Informed consent
The family's agreement after learning what will be done, why, and who will have access to the results.
Diagnostic overshadowing
Attributing new symptoms to a known disability, which leads to another condition being missed.
Confirmation bias
The assessor's tendency to see the evidence that supports a prior hypothesis and to overlook the evidence against it.
Cut-off score
The threshold that a scale or a system sets for making a decision.
Multidisciplinary team
A group of specialists from different fields who take part in assessing the student, each usually working alone.
Interdisciplinary team
A team in which each member assesses the student within his or her own field; members then share their findings and reach a joint decision and plan together.
Transdisciplinary team
A team that assesses the child together in a single session, with one member working directly with the child under the guidance of the others.
Biopsychosocial model
A model that views disability as the outcome of an interaction between the individual's health condition and environmental and personal factors.
Review, Interview, Observe, Test / Instruction, Curriculum, Environment, Learner
A matrix for gathering evidence through four methods (review, interview, observation and testing) across four domains (instruction, curriculum, environment and the learner).
