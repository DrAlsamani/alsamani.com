Chapter 6

Identification models

The tools are many and the evidence varied, yet one question remains: how is it all brought together into a single decision? Do we look for a gap between intelligence and achievement, wait to see what a student's response to sound instruction reveals, or search for a pattern in the student's abilities? And how are several scores combined to judge that a student is gifted? In this chapter we present the models that take up this question of decision, and then read the Saudi regulations in their light.

Scene from two team meetings

Two third graders both read very slowly and make many errors. At one school, the team said of the first: “His intelligence is average and his achievement is low. The gap is large, so he has a learning disability.” Of the second, they said: “His intelligence is slightly below average and the gap is small, so he does not have a learning disability. He is just a slow learner.”

At another school, the team said of both students: “We won’t make a judgment now. We’ll teach them reading intensively for eight weeks and measure their progress every week. Whoever still doesn’t improve will need a comprehensive evaluation.”

The students are the same, and so is the need, yet the decision changed with the model each school relies on. That is why understanding identification models is a practical necessity for every team.

Chapter objectives

  3. Compare the three versions of the patterns of strengths and weaknesses model in terms of what each requires, and summarize one piece of supporting evidence and one piece of critical evidence.
  4. Apply the criteria of the hybrid model to a given case, and match them against the DSM criteria for specific learning disorder.
  5. Apply the three combination rules to the scores of given students, and determine whom each rule excludes.
  6. Analyze the Saudi regulatory texts on learning disabilities and giftedness using the concepts of this chapter, and identify whom they may miss.
  7. Judge whether the evidence is sufficient to identify a learning disability in a new case, justify the judgment, and name the missing evidence.

Introduction

No single test can detect a learning disability or giftedness; both are judgments built on multiple sources of evidence. A team therefore needs a model that specifies which evidence is gathered, how it is combined, and where the dividing line falls. Choosing a model is a decision with direct consequences for students. As the opening scene shows, the same student may be considered to have a learning disability under one model and not under another, and in giftedness the rule for combining scores changes who is identified as gifted and who is excluded. Understanding the logic and limits of each model is a prerequisite for reading the regulations within which a team works, and for recognizing what those regulations leave to its professional judgment.

We open the chapter by explaining why a decision model is needed (Section 6.1), then present the discrepancy model and the criticisms leveled at it (Section 6.2), followed by response to intervention, the dual-discrepancy criterion and when nonresponse can be determined (Section 6.3). We then take up the patterns of strengths and weaknesses model in its three versions and the debate surrounding it (Section 6.4), the hybrid models with a worked case (Section 6.5), and multiple-criteria models in giftedness, combination rules, universal screening and local norms (Section 6.6). We close by comparing the models and reading the Saudi regulations in their light (Section 6.7).

6.1Why do we need a model?

Some categories have relatively clear criteria: hearing impairment is measured with audiometric equipment, and autism spectrum disorder has detailed criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Two categories in particular, however, need a model that specifies how we reach a decision: learning disabilities, because no single test detects them, and giftedness, because its very definition differs from one system to another. A model answers three questions: What evidence do we gather? How do we combine it into a decision? And where do we set the line?

In this chapter, “learning disabilities” names the educational category that qualifies for services in school systems, and “specific learning disorder” (Specific learning disorder) is used when the discussion concerns the DSM criteria. The two concepts are close, each has its own criteria, and we set them side by side in Section 6.5.

6.2The discrepancy model

For decades, the discrepancy model (Discrepancy model) was the most widely used approach to identifying learning disabilities. Its idea is simple: a student with a learning disability is intellectually capable, but his achievement in a particular area falls well below what his ability would predict. In other words, there is a discrepancy between ability and achievement (Ability-achievement discrepancy). It is usually calculated as the difference between the standard score on an intelligence test and the standard score on an achievement test, and the difference must reach a set threshold, such as one and a half standard deviations, that is, 22.5 points on a scale with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15.

Misconception: “He doesn’t have a learning disability; he’s just a slow learner”

The first team’s remark about the second student in the chapter’s opening scene is common in our schools. It sounds like a diagnosis, but it is in fact a popular description with no basis in any classification. “Slow learning” is not a category in the DSM or the International Classification of Diseases, nor among the categories of U.S. law, nor among those adopted by the Special Education Organizational Guide in the Kingdom (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). Its closest counterpart is “borderline intellectual functioning” (Borderline intellectual functioning). Earlier editions of the DSM defined it by a numerical range; the current edition lists it under “other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention,” with no numerical cutoff, and does not regard it as a disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2022).

The practical danger is that this remark closes the file. The student’s intelligence is above the threshold for intellectual disability, so he does not qualify for those services, and the gap between his intelligence and his achievement is small, so under discrepancy logic he does not qualify for learning disability services either. He is left without support, even though his reading weakness is real and he may respond to intensive intervention as well as anyone. The more productive question is “Does he learn when we teach him intensively, and what does he need?” Asking it serves him better than searching for a label for his condition, and it is the question the models in the rest of this chapter set out to answer.

Why has the discrepancy model been criticized?

  • It does not distinguish who needs different treatment: A large meta-analysis, that is, a study that pools and analyzes the results of many studies, found that poor readers with a discrepancy and poor readers without one are similar in reading-related skills and need similar instruction (Stuebing et al., 2002). The discrepancy, in other words, does not separate two genuinely different groups.
  • A wait-to-fail model: In the early grades, reading demands are simple, so the gap does not grow wide enough until third or fourth grade. The student waits years, struggling all the while, before qualifying for services, even though early intervention is far more beneficial.
  • An unstable decision: Both scores carry measurement error, and the two errors combine in the difference between them. If the reliability of the intelligence test is 0.95 and that of the reading test is 0.90, the standard error of the difference is about 15 × √(2 − 0.95 − 0.90) ≈ 5.8 points, and the first student’s observed difference of 27 is consistent, with 95% confidence, with a true difference of roughly 16 to 38. That range straddles the 22.5 cutoff, so we cannot say with certainty whether the student is above or below it. This is why a student may be classified this year and not next year without anything about him having changed (see the calculation of the standard error of the difference in Chapter 2, 2.5.1).
  • It depends on the tests used: A different intelligence test or a different achievement test may change the entire decision.
  • It ignores regression toward the mean: Intelligence and achievement are correlated, but imperfectly, so for a student with an IQ of 130 we expect achievement closer to the mean than 130. The simple difference overlooks this, as the following box shows.
For the specialist: Regression-based discrepancy

Rather than comparing achievement with the intelligence score itself, we compare it with the expected achievement of a student with that level of intelligence. The expected score is calculated with a regression equation. If both tests have a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, and the correlation coefficient between them is r:

Expected achievement = 100 + r × (IQ − 100)We then compare actual achievement with expected achievement and judge the difference against the standard error of estimate: 15 × √(1 − r²)

The simple difference thus over-identifies students with high intelligence, because it expects their achievement to equal their intelligence, and under-identifies students with slightly low intelligence, because it expects lower achievement from them than regression does. This is one face of regression toward the mean (see Chapter 2, 2.5.2). The result bears directly on twice-exceptional learners: a gifted student with average achievement may appear “discrepant” under the simple difference but not under regression. Correcting the formula, however, does not solve the model’s other problems; the meta-analysis cited above found no meaningful difference in reading-related skills between discrepant and nondiscrepant poor readers (Stuebing et al., 2002).

For these reasons, the U.S. Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), as amended in 2004, and its implementing regulations provide that states may not require schools to use a discrepancy, must permit the use of a process based on response to intervention, and may permit other research-based procedures (U.S. Department of Education, 2006, 34 CFR §300.307).

6.3Response to intervention

The response to intervention model (Response to intervention, RTI) turns the question around. Instead of asking “Is there a gap between the student’s ability and achievement?”, it asks “Does the student learn when we provide sound, evidence-based instruction?” If the student does not learn despite good instruction from which peers have learned, that is evidence of a difficulty requiring more than ordinary teaching (Fuchs & Fuchs, 2006). The model is usually implemented in three tiers:

The broader framework that encompasses academic and behavioral domains together is called a multi-tiered system of supports (Multi-tiered system of supports, MTSS). The idea is the same: support that intensifies with need, and decisions based on repeated data. The best-known source of such data is curriculum-based measurement, presented in Chapter 4: brief, equivalent probes administered every week or two, such as the number of words a student reads correctly in one minute, with results graphed to show whether the student is moving toward the goal.

The dual-discrepancy criterion

How do we know that a student “has not responded”? One of the best-known criteria is the dual discrepancy (Dual discrepancy), which Lynn Fuchs proposed as a basis for response decisions (Fuchs, 2003): the student is clearly below peers in the level of performance and also below them in the rate of growth despite intervention. A low-performing student who grows at the same rate as peers, or faster, is responding to intervention and simply needs time. A low-performing student who grows slowly, however, is falling further behind peers and needs more.

When do we say a student has not responded?

“Nonresponse” is a judgment built on a criterion and a threshold set in advance, not on a general impression. Table 6.2 presents the best-known criteria in the literature, with examples of thresholds that have been used in research and practice and the limitations of each. The thresholds here are illustrative and vary from study to study. Some are expressed as percentile ranks, that is, the percentage of peers in the comparison sample (the norm sample or the students in the grade) who scored below the student; a rank of 25 means that the student outperformed 25% of them.

The deeper problem is that these criteria do not identify the same students. When different criteria were applied to the same sample of poor readers, agreement among them on who “had not responded” was generally weak (Barth et al., 2008). The decision thus depends as much on the criterion the team chose as on the student. Added to this is what Ardoin and colleagues found in their review of curriculum-based measurement decision rules: most of these rules rest more on expert opinion than on research, slopes calculated over short periods are unstable, and they shift with variation in the difficulty of passages that are supposed to be equivalent (Ardoin et al., 2013). Three practical conclusions follow: the team should set its criterion and threshold before collecting data, not after; it should collect data over a sufficient period using passages that are truly equivalent; and it should treat the slope as it would any score, that is, as a range of plausible values.

Response to intervention does not delay evaluation

The model can be misunderstood and turned into a pretext for waiting: “We won’t evaluate him until he has completed Tiers 2 and 3.” The Office of Special Education Programs in the U.S. Department of Education has cautioned that an RTI process may not be used to delay or deny a comprehensive evaluation when the family requests one or the school suspects a disability (U.S. Department of Education, Office of Special Education Programs, 2011). The data gathered across the tiers of support enter the evaluation as evidence; they do not replace it. This holds even more clearly for a student who shows signs of intellectual or sensory disability or a developmental disorder; such a student should not be made to wait until nonresponse has been “proven.”

For the teacher: What should you document during the tiers of support?

The value of your data for a later decision depends on how well they are documented. For each student, record the program or method used; the number of sessions planned and actually delivered, and the length of each; the student’s absences; the result of each measurement, with its date, on the same passage or an equivalent one; and any change in method, with its date. Ask a colleague or supervisor to observe at least two sessions using a checklist of the program’s steps; this is treatment fidelity (Treatment fidelity). If the student does not progress, the team’s first question will be: was the intervention delivered as planned? If the answer is no, nonresponse says more about the intervention than about the student.

Criticism of the model also comes from the opposite direction. Reynolds and Shaywitz argued that response to intervention has not yet proven its validity as a diagnostic tool, that relying on it alone turns “wait to fail” into “watch the student fail,” that it leaves the question of cause unanswered, and that it may overlook the high-ability student who struggles relative to his own ability rather than relative to his peers (Reynolds & Shaywitz, 2009). This criticism does not cancel the model’s value for early intervention, but it reminds us that nonresponse is one piece of evidence, to be combined with others in a comprehensive evaluation.

6.4Patterns of strengths and weaknesses

The patterns of strengths and weaknesses model (Patterns of strengths and weaknesses, PSW) seeks to restore to diagnosis a question that response to intervention leaves aside: why is the student struggling? It holds that a learning disability is linked to a weakness in a specific cognitive process, such as phonological processing, the ability to perceive, remember and manipulate speech sounds, or working memory, while other abilities remain intact. It therefore looks for a pattern with three corners (Flanagan et al., 2018):

Under this model, a student is classified as having a learning disability in reading, for example, if his general abilities are good, he has a weakness in phonological processing, and his decoding achievement is low, because decoding depends on phonological processing. If, on the other hand, he has a weakness in processing speed and a weakness in reading comprehension, the theoretical link between the two is weaker and the pattern less convincing.

The debate over the model

Proponents argue that the model is consistent with the original concept of learning disability as a disorder in basic psychological processes, and that it offers an explanation of the problem that may guide intervention. Studies that have examined it, however, have found important problems. In a simulation study of the three versions, they were good at ruling out students without a disability, that is, highly specific, but weak at identifying students who actually had one; many of those they identified did not carry the “true” pattern on which the simulation was built, and they identified only small proportions of students (Stuebing et al., 2012). An applied study found that the model’s methods often disagree about who has a disability and who does not, and that their identification rates are low and inconsistent (Miciak et al., 2014). Nor has it yet been shown that tailoring intervention to a cognitive profile improves outcomes more than direct academic intervention. Part of these problems stems from what we saw in the discrepancy model: the pattern rests on differences between scores, and difference scores are less reliable than the scores themselves.

The model’s developers respond that these studies treat it as a mechanical algorithm, whereas, as proposed, it is a framework for organizing evidence within a comprehensive evaluation, one that draws data from multiple sources and methods, including response-to-intervention data, and that is judged through clinical reasoning rather than numerical comparisons alone (Flanagan et al., 2018). They hold that understanding the cognitive weakness helps in choosing accommodations and explaining nonresponse, even if it has not yet been shown to improve the design of the intervention itself. The model therefore remains the subject of genuine scientific debate to this day.

6.5Hybrid models

After two decades of debate, many researchers lean toward a hybrid model (Hybrid model) that combines the best of the earlier models and drops what has proven weak. One of its best-known versions is the model proposed by Fletcher and colleagues (Fletcher et al., 2019), which rests on three criteria that must all be met:

Inadequate responseThe student has not improved sufficiently despite good, intensive instruction, with documented progress-monitoring data.
Low achievementPerformance in a specific academic area is clearly low on a standardized achievement measure.
ExclusionThe weakness is not explained by intellectual or sensory disability, emotional disturbance, lack of opportunity to learn, or limited proficiency in the language of instruction.

Note that this model requires neither an intelligence test to calculate a discrepancy nor an analysis of cognitive processes. It may, however, use an intelligence test for another purpose: to rule out intellectual disability and to gain a fuller understanding of the student (Chapter 8 examines the application of this model to learning disabilities in detail).

The hybrid model is very close to the DSM criteria for specific learning disorder, which makes it a bridge between medical diagnosis and educational eligibility. The DSM requires that the difficulties persist for at least six months despite interventions targeting them (Criterion A), which corresponds to nonresponse. It requires that academic skills be substantially and quantifiably below those expected for the student’s age, as confirmed by standardized achievement measures and a comprehensive clinical assessment (Criterion B), and for this it suggests a threshold of about 1.5 standard deviations below the mean, with leniency down to one standard deviation when other evidence supports it. It requires that the difficulties begin during the school years (Criterion C). And it requires that they not be better explained by intellectual disability, uncorrected visual or auditory acuity, another mental or neurological disorder, psychosocial adversity, limited proficiency in the language of instruction, or inadequate instruction (Criterion D) (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Note that the DSM compares achievement with what is expected for age; it does not require a discrepancy from intelligence.

The lesson

Had the team relied on the simple difference with a cutoff of 1.5 standard deviations, Nayef would not have been identified, because the difference between his intelligence (97) and his reading (76) is 21 points, below 22.5. Under regression, however, his expected achievement is about 98, and his reading falls about 22 points below it, exceeding the cutoff of 18. The judgment about the same student changed with the formula, even though his need did not. This is precisely what the hybrid model avoids: it bases the decision on what no one disputes, namely clearly low achievement, documented nonresponse, and the exclusion of other explanations.

What evidence could change the decision? If a retest of word reading with another standardized instrument came out close to average, or if his slope accelerated clearly, approaching his grade’s slope, when measured with passages of established equivalence, the team would need to revisit the identification. And if a weakness in listening comprehension or in hearing emerged on re-examination, another explanation for his difficulty would have to be considered.

6.6Multiple-criteria models in giftedness

In most systems it is no longer acceptable to identify a gifted student by a single intelligence score. In contemporary theories, giftedness encompasses intellectual ability, creativity, motivation and achievement in a particular domain, and it interacts with the environment and with opportunity (Gagné, 2009; Renzulli, 1978). Identifying gifted students therefore draws on multiple sources of evidence: an ability test, an achievement test, a teacher rating scale, a portfolio and perhaps a creativity test.

Gathering multiple sources of evidence, however, raises a question that is often overlooked: how do we combine them into a decision? An important study showed that the method of combination, known as the combination rule (Combination rule), by itself changes how many students are identified as gifted, and which students they are, even when the instruments and scores are identical (McBee et al., 2014). The scores are the same, yet the number identified ranges from one student to six. Three observations in the table deserve attention. First, Student E has an ability score of 140 and an achievement score of 95, a pattern that may point to twice-exceptionality or to limited opportunities to learn; this student is identified only under the “or” rule, because the averaging rule lets the weakness consume the strength. Second, Students C and D are very close: the difference between 121 and 118 is too small to be trusted, since the standard error of a test with a reliability of 0.95 is about 3.4 points. Even so, one of them is identified under two rules and the other under none. Third, each rule errs in its own way. The “or” rule gives measurement error on every criterion a chance to lift a student above the cutoff, increasing false identifications, while the “and” rule gives it a chance to push a student below the cutoff on any criterion, increasing false exclusions (McBee et al., 2014). Choosing a rule, technical as it may appear, is therefore a decision about which of the two errors a program is willing to accept, and it should follow from the program's definition of giftedness and the services it provides.

Point matrices

Some programs use a matrix that assigns points for each criterion and then adds them up. The method looks fair and comprehensive, but it merges scores that differ in nature and reliability into a single number, and the meaning of each is lost. A student may accumulate high points from criteria of weak reliability, while another who is truly exceptional on one strong criterion is excluded. It is better to specify the combination rule explicitly, based on the program's definition of giftedness.

Universal screening and local norms

One of the greatest sources of inequity in gifted identification is reliance on nomination alone instead of universal screening (Universal screening), which is applied to all students. A student whom the teacher does not nominate, because the student is quiet, speaks a different home language, or has a difficulty that masks ability, never enters the assessment at all. A study in a large U.S. school district found that adding universal screening of all second graders markedly increased the representation of students from low-income and minority families in gifted programs (Card & Giuliano, 2016).

Likewise, comparing a student with national norms can exclude an entire school's students in a less advantaged setting, despite their clear superiority over their peers there. Researchers therefore propose using local norms (Local norms), which identify the highest-performing students in each school relative to their schoolmates, because these are the students who need services beyond what their classroom offers (Peters et al., 2019). (Chapter 12 addresses this in detail.)

In the Saudi context: Which model do the regulations adopt?

Learning disabilities. The Regulations for Special Education Institutes and Programs set three conditions for admitting a student to learning disabilities programs. The first is that “the student shows a clear discrepancy between the level of his abilities and the level of his academic achievement” in one area, such as reading, writing, counting, or mathematical reasoning, “or has a disorder in one of the psychological processes, such as memory, attention, thinking, and perception.” The second is that the difficulties do not result from mental impairment, a behavioral disorder, or sensory causes. The third is “that it be established that regular educational services are unsuitable or of limited effectiveness” (Ministry of Education [Al-Ma'arif], 1422 AH [Arabic], Article 7). The Special Education Organizational Guide restates these conditions in nearly identical wording and makes no mention of response to intervention (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). The model the texts prescribe is thus discrepancy, exclusion, and the ineffectiveness of regular instruction, with no numerical size for the discrepancy and no specified method for establishing ineffectiveness.

A critical reading with the chapter's tools. In learning disabilities, the absence of a numerical size for the discrepancy is a double-edged sword. It allows two teams to reach two different judgments about the same student, as in the chapter's opening scene, but it also frees the team from an arbitrary cutoff and allows it to estimate the discrepancy through regression and to take the standard error of the difference into account. The phrase “or has a disorder in one of the psychological processes” opens a door close to the logic of patterns of strengths and weaknesses, yet it does not require consistency between the cognitive weakness and the academic weakness, so the critique of that model applies to it with even greater force. The condition that regular services be shown to be ineffective is, in essence, the first criterion of the hybrid model. Two recent studies, however, indicate that practices resembling response to intervention exist in Saudi schools but are mostly informal and lack systematic progress monitoring, and that the absence of formal adoption of the model is among the obstacles to its implementation (Almulla & Mahama, 2026; Altheiabi et al., 2025). A team can therefore read the text in a hybrid way without departing from it, as in Nayef's case: it documents ineffectiveness with monitoring data and a criterion set in advance (Table 6.2), establishes low achievement with a standardized measure and its confidence interval, and satisfies the exclusion requirement.

In giftedness, the Guide to Gifted Classes makes the cognitive abilities score a mandatory condition at 120. This may exclude the twice-exceptional student whose difficulty lowers the ability score, as well as the student whose score reflects language more than ability, and it gives a student who scores 118 a different fate from one who scores 120, although the difference between them lies within measurement error. The different cutoffs in the Regulations for the Wechsler (125) and the Stanford-Binet (127) are consistent with the different standard deviations of the two scales in their editions at the time, so both cutoffs fall at roughly the same distance from the mean. This is a sound application of the idea that a score can be understood only in relation to its scale. The national program, for its part, administers a standardized instrument to large numbers of students, but it depends on family registration, so a student's access to assessment hinges on the family's awareness and means; this is precisely the problem universal screening addresses. We found no officially published score scale for the test, and a recent peer-reviewed study reports an operational cut-off score of 1540 (Almulhim et al., 2026). Nor did we find a published regulatory text specifying how the four criteria are to be weighed when the scores diverge, or a text that permits or prohibits combining a gifted program with learning disabilities services.

A question for reflection: If the rule of the Guide to Gifted Classes were applied to Student E in Table 6.8, and the student passed the personal characteristics criterion, would the student be admitted? And if the ability score were 118 rather than 140, what would change? What would you propose as a rule that does justice to both students without opening the door to everyone who comes close to the cutoff?

Conclusion

We conclude in this chapter that every identification model accepts some kind of error in exchange for what it offers. The discrepancy model is simple, but it delays help and fluctuates with measurement error. Response to intervention offers help early and rules out poor instruction as an explanation, but it does not explain why a student fails to respond. Patterns of strengths and weaknesses are high in specificity and low in sensitivity, and hybrid models combine nonresponse, low achievement, and exclusion. In giftedness, the combination rule determines who is identified and who is left out. It follows that a team should recognize the kind of error accepted by the model it works with, should not let the label “slow learner” become a verdict that closes the question of what the student needs, and should not allow response to intervention to become a reason for delaying assessment.

Some questions remain unsettled by research, such as the value of patterns of strengths and weaknesses for guiding intervention and the weak agreement among nonresponse criteria. Also open in the Saudi context is the fact that, for learning disabilities, the regulations prescribe discrepancy, exclusion, and the ineffectiveness of regular instruction without a numerical cutoff, leaving the cutoff to the team's discretion, and that, for giftedness, they use different combination rules, the most consequential being the cognitive abilities requirement of 120 for gifted classes.

6.8Summary, key terms, and activities

Chapter summary

  • Learning disabilities and giftedness require a model that specifies the evidence, how it is combined, and where the cutoff lies, because no single test reveals either one.
  • The discrepancy model is simple, but it does not distinguish groups that genuinely differ, it delays help, and it fluctuates with measurement error, because the standard error of the difference is larger than the error of each score. The simple-difference method over-identifies students with high ability and under-identifies those with slightly low ability; regression corrects this without solving the model's other problems.
  • “Slow learner” is not a diagnostic category, and settling for it denies the student services without answering what the student needs.
  • Response to intervention offers help early and rules out poor instruction as an explanation, but it does not explain why a student fails to respond; its criteria agree poorly, and it must not delay assessment.
  • Patterns of strengths and weaknesses comprise three models that seek a cognitive explanation; they are high in specificity and low in sensitivity, and their value for intervention has yet to be demonstrated.
  • Hybrid models combine nonresponse, low achievement, and exclusion, and they come close to the DSM criteria for specific learning disorder.
  • In giftedness, the combination rule changes who is identified, and each rule accepts a particular kind of error; universal screening and local norms widen access.
  • For learning disabilities, the Saudi regulations prescribe discrepancy, exclusion, and the ineffectiveness of regular instruction without a numerical cutoff; for giftedness, they use different combination rules, the most consequential being the cognitive abilities requirement of 120 for gifted classes.

Glossary of terms

Discrepancy model
Identifying a learning disability by a large gap between the ability score and the achievement score.
Regression-based discrepancy
Comparing actual achievement with the achievement predicted from the ability score by a regression equation, rather than with the ability score itself.
Standard error of estimate
The spread of actual scores around the scores predicted by a regression equation, used to judge how far achievement falls from what is expected.
Borderline intellectual functioning
Below-average intellectual functioning that lies above the range of intellectual disability; the DSM lists it as a condition that may be a focus of clinical attention rather than a disorder, and “slow learner” has no corresponding category in current classifications.
Response to intervention (RTI)
A model that provides tiered support and monitors the student's progress, using nonresponse as evidence of the need for a comprehensive evaluation.
Multi-tiered system of supports (MTSS)
A schoolwide framework for providing academic and behavioral support at increasing levels of intensity.
Dual discrepancy
A student's falling below peers in both level of performance and rate of growth.
Nonresponse criterion
The rule a team sets in advance for judging that a student has not responded to intervention, such as normative final status, slope, or dual discrepancy, together with a specified threshold.
Treatment fidelity
The extent to which an intervention is delivered as designed: the number and length of sessions and adherence to its steps.
Patterns of strengths and weaknesses (PSW)
A model that looks for a specific cognitive weakness explaining an academic weakness alongside good general abilities; it has three well-known versions.
Hybrid model
A model for identifying learning disabilities that combines inadequate response to intervention, low achievement, and the exclusion of other explanations.
Meta-analysis
A statistical method that pools the results of multiple studies on a single topic and analyzes them together to reach a more general conclusion.
Combination rule
The way scores on multiple criteria are combined into a single decision, including the “and” rule, the “or” rule, and the averaging rule.
Universal screening
Assessing all students in a grade or stage, rather than relying on nomination or family registration.
Local norms
Norms that compare a student with schoolmates in the same school or district rather than with a national sample.

Review questions

  1.   2. Why is the discrepancy model described as a “wait-to-fail” model? And why does regression-based discrepancy not solve this problem?
  3. True or false? Explain: “A student described as a slow learner does not need assessment, because the weakness is expected given the student's intelligence.”

  5. A second-grade girl remained at the 20th percentile on a standardized test after Tier 2, but her slope equals that of her peers. Judge her response using the normative final status criterion, then the slope criterion, then dual discrepancy. What do you conclude from the differing judgments?

  8. What does each of the three patterns of strengths and weaknesses models require? Why might these models be high in specificity and low in sensitivity? And what does this mean for a student who meets none of their patterns?

  10. A student scored 118 on the cognitive abilities test and passed the achievement, personal characteristics, and creative product criteria. What does the rule of the Guide to Gifted Classes decide for this student? And what does the standard error say about the difference between 118 and 120?

Learning activities

For any activity that uses real student data: it is conducted with the consent of the family and the school administration, without any data that identify the student, and under professional supervision; no decision is based on its results, and they are communicated only through official channels. All the activities can be carried out with hypothetical data.

  1. Simulating combination rules (about one hour): In a spreadsheet, create scores for twenty hypothetical students on two criteria and identify the gifted under each of the three rules. Then add a small random error (between −4 and +4) to each score and repeat the identification. Product: a table showing how many students each rule identified before and after the error, and a paragraph explaining which rules were most affected by the error and whose status changed.
  2. Designing tiers of support (about two hours): Design a response to intervention plan in reading for first grade in an elementary school you know or a hypothetical one. What happens at each tier? Who delivers it? How often is progress measured? What nonresponse criterion and threshold will you use, chosen from Table 6.2? How will implementation fidelity be documented? And when is a case referred for comprehensive evaluation without waiting? Product: a two-page outline.
  3. Debate (one class session): The group splits into two teams, one defending the response to intervention model and the other the patterns of strengths and weaknesses model, drawing on the evidence in this chapter, including Table 6.4 and the critique by Reynolds and Shaywitz. Product: a one-page paper from each team summarizing its two strongest arguments and the other team's strongest argument.
  4. From text to procedure (desk activity, about two hours): Read the text of Article 7 of the Regulations as presented in this chapter, and write a proposed procedure for a school team specifying how a “clear discrepancy” is to be estimated, how regular services are to be shown to be “of limited effectiveness,” the minimum duration of support and the data it requires, and how the exclusion requirement is to be met. Then apply your procedure to Lujain's case. Product: a one-page procedure and a paragraph on the result of applying it.
