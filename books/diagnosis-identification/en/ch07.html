Intellectual disability

An IQ score cannot carry the weight of diagnosis on its own. Intellectual disability is a judgment that rests on three pillars: a clear deficit in intellectual ability, deficits in the skills of everyday life, and onset of both during the developmental period. In this chapter we trace how each pillar is measured and how the evidence for all three is brought together, and we explain why the number 70 is not a hard dividing line.

Scene from the first grade

Halfway through the school year, Nawaf still cannot count to ten without a mistake, and he does not recognize most of the letters. He speaks in short sentences of two or three words and needs help opening his lunch box and closing his backpack. At recess he plays with the kindergarten children rather than with his classmates. When the teacher explains the rules of a new game, he looks at her with a smile and then does whatever the children around him are doing.

His mother says he “was a little late with everything”: he walked at eighteen months and began talking after the age of three. She asks the teacher, “Will he catch up with his classmates if we work hard with him?”

The mother's question is a fair one, but answering it requires more than the teacher's observations. Nawaf's delay may reflect an intellectual disability, but it may equally be a language delay, a hearing impairment, or the effect of limited opportunities. In this chapter we set out the path by which the team distinguishes among these possibilities.

Chapter objectives

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

  1. Determine, for a presented case, the extent to which the three pillars of diagnosis are met, and classify examples of adaptive deficits into the conceptual, social and practical domains.
  2. Determine the severity level for a presented case from its adaptive behavior data, and compare this with the approach of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11).
  3. Select a screening tool, an intelligence test or an adaptive behavior scale for a presented case, and justify the choice in terms of the student's age, the test's floor, the recency of its norms and the status of its Arabic version.
  4. Apply the eligibility criteria of the Saudi regulations to a presented case, and show where they agree with the DSM and where they depart from it.
  5. Judge whether the evidence suffices for a diagnosis in a new case with scores near the thresholds, taking measurement error and the age of the norms into account, and give reasons for the judgment.
  6. Distinguish intellectual disability from similar and co-occurring conditions, and determine when to recommend medical and genetic referral.

Introduction

Intellectual disability is among the categories most prone to having the judgment reduced to a single number, the IQ score, even though a sound diagnosis requires three criteria to be met together: a clear deficit in intellectual ability, deficits in adaptive behavior, and onset during the developmental period. The judgment has far-reaching consequences for a student's educational and social path, and errors are costly in both directions: the label may be attached to a student whose performance is better explained by weak hearing, language or opportunities, or it may be withheld from a student who needs the support it opens up. Sound judgment also requires that the IQ threshold be read in light of the confidence interval, as an approximate and flexible range.

We open the chapter by defining intellectual disability and its causes (Section 7.1), then present the three diagnostic criteria (Section 7.2), severity levels and support needs (Section 7.3), and early warning signs (Section 7.4). We then turn to the decision pathway and the place of genetic testing within it (Section 7.5), instrument profiles and the Arabic tools available (Section 7.6), and apply all of this to four cases (Section 7.7). From there we move to differential diagnosis (Section 7.8) and co-occurrence (Section 7.9), and we close with eligibility criteria in the Saudi context (Section 7.10).

7.1What is intellectual disability?

Intellectual disability is a developmental condition marked by a clear deficit in intellectual ability, that is, in reasoning, problem solving, planning, abstract thinking and learning from experience, accompanied by a clear deficit in adaptive behavior, that is, in the skills a person needs in everyday life, with onset during the developmental period. A broad review estimates its prevalence at about 1% of the population (Maulik et al., 2011).

Its name has changed over the decades. It was once called “mental retardation,” a term later replaced by “intellectual disability” in most manuals and legislation, both because the older term had become stigmatizing and because the newer one fits a view of disability as an interaction between the individual and the environment. The eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) calls it “disorders of intellectual development” (Disorders of intellectual development) (World Health Organization [WHO], 2024). In Saudi documents, the Regulations for Special Education Institutes and Programs use the term “mental retardation” (Ministry of Education [Al-Ma'arif], 1422 AH [Arabic]), while the Special Education Organizational Guide uses “mental impairment” (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). A teacher may therefore come across any of these names in student files; they all refer to the same thing.

What causes it?

Intellectual disability has many causes. The cause used to remain unknown in a large share of cases, especially mild ones, but that share has shrunk as modern genetic testing has spread (see 7.5). Known causes include:

  • Genetic and chromosomal causes: such as Down syndrome, fragile X syndrome (Fragile X), and recessive genetic disorders, including metabolic disorders such as phenylketonuria (PKU), whose effects can be prevented through early newborn screening and diet.
  • Prenatal causes: such as fetal exposure to alcohol, to certain infections, or to severe malnutrition.
  • Perinatal and postnatal causes: such as severe oxygen deprivation, extreme prematurity, brain infections, head injuries, lead poisoning and severe environmental deprivation.
Common misconceptions
  • “Intellectual disability means the child cannot learn.” Most children with intellectual disability, the majority of whom are at the mild level, learn to read, write and use functional arithmetic, and acquire vocational skills, but at a slower pace and with instruction that is more explicit and more repetitive.
  • “The IQ score determines everything.” Two children with the same IQ score may differ widely in their independence and social skills. That is why severity is determined by adaptive behavior, as we will see.
  • “Intellectual disability does not change.” Intellectual ability is relatively stable, but functioning improves considerably with appropriate support, and a person may need less support over time.
  • “Knowing the cause is of no use to the school.” The cause does not change the diagnostic criteria, but it may reveal health risks that require monitoring, indicate the likelihood of recurrence in the family, and in some metabolic disorders point to a specific treatment.

7.2Diagnostic criteria

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), and the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (AAIDD), in the twelfth edition of its manual, agree on three criteria that must all be met (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022; Schalock et al., 2021):

Criterion A: Intellectual ability

This criterion is measured with an individually administered standardized intelligence test of high reliability (Chapter 3 presents these tests in detail). A deficit means that the Full Scale IQ (Full Scale IQ, FSIQ) falls about two standard deviations below the mean, that is, around 70 on a test with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. Yet both manuals stress that this number is not a hard cutoff. Every score carries some measurement error and is therefore read within the confidence interval that surrounds it (see 2.5). A score of 72, for example, may have a confidence interval extending from 65 to 79. For this reason, a person whose score falls between 70 and 75 may be diagnosed if the adaptive deficits are very clear.

The test must also suit the student: in its language, in the recency of its norms, and in how it accommodates any other disabilities. A low score on a verbal test taken by a child who has not mastered the language of the test does not measure intellectual ability. Outdated norms in particular inflate scores near the threshold, a point we return to in Maryam's case (7.7).

Criterion B: Adaptive behavior

This is the criterion that keeps diagnosis from turning into a verdict on a number. The question it asks is whether the student can manage everyday life as the community expects of someone that age. It is measured in three domains: conceptual (such as language, reading, arithmetic, money and time), social (such as getting along with others and understanding the rules of interaction), and practical (such as self-care, getting around and safety):

Table 7.1: Examples of adaptive behavior deficits in the three domains.
AreaExamples of deficits in an elementary school student
ConceptualDoes not know the value of simple coins and notes, cannot tell time, cannot follow three-step instructions, and does not plan for what is needed in the next lesson.
SocialDoes not understand the rules of group games, believes everything he is told and is therefore easily exploited, does not read classmates' feelings, and behaves with strangers as he does with family.
PracticalNeeds help with dressing or personal hygiene, cannot find the way to the classroom without someone accompanying him, and does not behave safely when crossing the street.

A clear deficit in one of the three domains is enough, because a deficit in any of them hinders independent living. Adaptive behavior is measured with a standardized scale, and information should preferably be gathered from more than one source, such as the family and the teacher.

Criterion C: Onset during the developmental period

This criterion distinguishes intellectual disability from a decline in abilities after development is complete, as in brain injury in adults or dementia. What is required is that the deficits appear during childhood or adolescence, not that they were diagnosed at the time. An adult who was never assessed as a child can be diagnosed if the developmental history shows that the deficits were present from childhood. In its twelfth edition, the AAIDD raised the age of onset from 18 to before 22, on the grounds that brain development continues into the early twenties (Schalock et al., 2021). The DSM, for its part, simply states “during the developmental period” without specifying an age (APA, 2022).

Global developmental delay

In the first five years of life, intelligence is hard to measure reliably. The DSM therefore provides a provisional diagnosis, global developmental delay (Global developmental delay), for a child under five who shows delays in two or more developmental domains and cannot be fully assessed. The child is reassessed later (Shevell et al., 2003). Some of these children outgrow their delay, in which case the later diagnosis is something other than intellectual disability.

7.3Severity levels and support needs

One of the most notable changes introduced in the fifth edition of the DSM is that severity is now determined by adaptive behavior. Earlier manuals divided severity according to IQ ranges; today the question is how much support the person needs in everyday life. The DSM uses the phrase “severity levels” for intellectual disability and “support levels” for autism spectrum disorder. The former describes the depth of adaptive deficits in each domain; the latter describes how much support the symptoms call for.

The mild level accounts for the majority of cases. The AAIDD calls for moving beyond classification by severity to determining support needs intensity in each area of life, using tools designed for this purpose, such as the Supports Intensity Scale–Children's Version (SIS-C) (Thompson et al., 2016). The more important educational question goes beyond “What is his level?” to “What support does he need, where, and how much?”

Comparison with ICD-11

The ICD agrees with the DSM on the three pillars but differs from it in three respects that bear on decisions (WHO, 2024):

  • Severity on both dimensions: severity is determined by intellectual ability and adaptive behavior together. The mild level corresponds to performance roughly two to three standard deviations below the mean, and the moderate level to three to four. Severe and profound both lie more than four standard deviations below the mean and are distinguished by adaptive behavior alone, because standardized tests do not discriminate within this range.
  • Behavioral indicators: when suitable standardized tests are unavailable or cannot be administered, clinical judgment draws on detailed behavioral indicators for each level and age, set out in the ICD's descriptive guidelines.
  • The “provisional” category (Provisional): used for a child under four, or when a valid assessment is not possible because of a sensory or motor impairment or severe behavior. It corresponds to global developmental delay in the DSM, though with a different age limit.

7.4Early warning signs

Severe intellectual disability usually becomes apparent in the first year of life and is detected early, especially when it is associated with a known syndrome. Mild intellectual disability, by contrast, may go undetected until school entry, when difficulty with academic learning emerges. Indicators that call for a developmental evaluation include:

  • A clear delay spanning several developmental domains at once: motor skills, language, play and self-care.
  • Play resembling that of much younger children, such as exploring objects by mouthing or throwing them at an age when pretend play is expected.
  • Difficulty learning new skills, needing a great deal of repetition to learn a simple skill, and forgetting it quickly.
  • At school: difficulty that extends to nearly all subjects, together with difficulties in independence and social skills.
  • Loss of previously acquired skills: this sign is different from delay and calls for urgent medical referral before any educational evaluation.

A young child does not need a diagnosis in order to benefit from services. In the Kingdom, the Special Education Organizational Guide defines those eligible for early intervention services as children no older than six who show a delay “of two standard deviations below the mean in two or more domains” or of 25%, and it adds children “at risk” who have been exposed to at least three environmental risk factors (Ministry of Education, 1436 AH [Arabic]). The Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities provides for designing and implementing early intervention programs and for making modern assessment and diagnostic tools available (Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, 1445 AH [Arabic], Art. 8). A developmental screening tool in early childhood is thus a gateway to services before it is a gateway to a label.

For the teacher: a difference worth noticing

A student with learning disabilities usually struggles in one specific area and seems bright in others: he understands a story when it is read to him, solves problems orally, and behaves maturely with classmates. A student suspected of having an intellectual disability, by contrast, shows difficulties across several areas at once: academic, social and practical. This difference alone is not enough for a diagnosis, but it can guide your observations.

7.5The decision pathway and genetic evaluation

Genetic testing: a recommended first-tier referral

“Medical and genetic evaluation as needed” was once a common phrase in diagnostic reports. The current recommendation, however, is that genetic testing be offered to every child with unexplained intellectual disability or global developmental delay. A consensus statement identified chromosomal microarray (Chromosomal microarray) as a first-tier test for individuals with developmental disabilities or congenital anomalies, in place of conventional karyotyping (Miller et al., 2010). The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics later recommended that exome sequencing (Exome sequencing) or genome sequencing be considered as a first- or second-tier test for children with intellectual disability or developmental delay (Manickam et al., 2021).

The decision rests with the developmental pediatrician or the geneticist. The educational team's role is nonetheless clear: to recommend the referral in its report as a step endorsed by medical guidelines, and to document whatever in the family and developmental history supports it, such as parental consanguinity, relatives with similar conditions, and any loss of acquired skills. The test result does not change the diagnostic criteria; intellectual disability is diagnosed on the three criteria even when its cause is unknown. But the result may reveal health risks that need monitoring, and it answers a question that weighs heavily on families: will this happen again in our other children?

In the Saudi context: consanguineous marriage and national screening

Consanguineous marriage is common in the Kingdom. A study of 3,212 Saudi families found that 57.7% were based on consanguineous marriage, 28.4% of them between first cousins, with variation across regions (el-Hazmi et al., 1995). A later study of a national sample of 11,554 mothers found a similar rate of 56% (El Mouzan et al., 2008). Consanguinity raises the likelihood of recessive genetic disorders, many of which cause intellectual disability.

Three relevant programs operate in the Kingdom. The National Newborn Screening Program began in 1426 AH (2005 CE), covering sixteen metabolic and endocrine diseases, later expanded to seventeen. Its early years detected roughly one positive case per thousand births, and the program's manual attributed this high rate to the fact that these diseases are recessive genetic conditions whose frequency rises with consanguineous marriage (Ministry of Health, 2016 [Arabic]). Premarital screening has been mandatory since 2004, but it is limited to sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia, and acting on its results remains voluntary (Memish & Saeedi, 2011). The Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities guarantees screening and diagnostic services and programs for the early detection of disability (Law on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, 1445 AH [Arabic], Art. 9).

The lesson for the evaluator is that premarital screening does not cover the hundreds of recessive disorders that can cause intellectual disability, and that a normal newborn screen does not rule out a genetic cause. Parental consanguinity is information that strengthens the case for genetic referral; the answer comes from the test itself.

A question for reflection: In a developmental history interview, how can you ask the family about parental consanguinity and about similar conditions among relatives without the question being heard as blame?

ASQ-3Ages and Stages Questionnaires, Third EditionDevelopmental screening

The idea

A screening tool completed by parents, designed to be administered periodically during health care visits or in early childhood programs to identify children whose development may be delayed in one or more domains (Squires & Bricker, 2009).

What it looks like

Twenty-one questionnaires, each for a specific age interval. The questionnaires cover intervals from 2 to 60 months, but their range of use is somewhat wider, from 1 to 66 months, because each questionnaire has an age window within which it can be given before and after its target age. Each questionnaire contains thirty questions about behaviors the child can perform, six in each of five domains: communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem solving and personal-social. Parents answer each question with “yes,” “sometimes” or “not yet,” and many questions ask them to try the skill with the child before answering.

A scene from administration

At a primary health care center, the nurse gives the mother of a 30-month-old the questionnaire for the appropriate interval and asks her to try some of the tasks with him at home during the week, using simple household items, before answering. When the mother returns the questionnaire, the nurse totals the score for each domain and compares it with that domain's two cutoffs. The communication score falls below the lower cutoff, the problem-solving score lies in the monitoring zone, and the other domains are on track. The nurse avoids describing the child as “delayed” and explains to the mother that the questionnaire calls for a closer look: she refers the child first for a hearing test, then for a developmental and language evaluation, and gives the mother home activities for the domain that needs monitoring.

How are the results interpreted?

Each domain score is compared with two cutoffs: a score above the upper cutoff indicates development on track, a score between the two indicates a need for monitoring and home activities, and a score below the lower cutoff indicates a need for further evaluation. The questionnaire yields no total score; it is the pattern across domains that is interpreted.

Limitations

The tool depends on the accuracy of the parents' observations and their understanding of the questions. A delay in a single domain does not mean intellectual disability; it may reflect only a language or motor delay, or an undetected hearing loss. Tool website

SB5Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales, Fifth EditionIntellectual ability

The idea: why is it preferred for this population?

The Stanford-Binet extends down to very young ages, starting at two years, and contains many easy items at its lower end. This matters in intellectual disability, because a test whose items start out difficult produces what is called a floor effect (Floor effect): the student fails every available item, so the examiner cannot tell where the student's ability actually lies, and the subtest scores all look alike (see 2.5.3).

What it looks like

The examiner begins with two short subtests called routing subtests (Routing subtests), one verbal and one nonverbal. Their results determine the level at which the examiner starts the remaining subtests. The scale then measures five factors, fluid reasoning, knowledge, quantitative reasoning, visual-spatial processing and working memory, each in a verbal and a nonverbal domain, for a total of ten subtests. The materials consist of easel-mounted item books, blocks, toy pieces and pictures (Roid, 2003).

A scene from administration

The specialist sits with Nawaf in a quiet room one morning, having first confirmed the results of his hearing and vision screening. She begins with the nonverbal routing subtest. Nawaf answers the first items, then starts making errors, and she stops at the discontinue rule specified in the manual. His scores on the two routing subtests set the starting level for the remaining subtests, so he begins at the earliest levels, usually given to children younger than he is. This is what allows the scale to map his profile rather than hit its floor. The specialist notices that he loses attention after about twenty minutes, so she gives him a short break and records this in her behavioral observations, since it is part of interpreting the score.

How are the results interpreted?

The Full Scale IQ is read first, together with its confidence interval, and then the agreement between the Verbal and Nonverbal IQs is examined. Close agreement points toward a general intellectual deficit, whereas a large difference favoring the nonverbal side calls for checking language and hearing before reaching a judgment. If most scaled scores sit at the very bottom of their range, the overall number confirms the deficit but cannot distinguish among its levels, and severity is then based on adaptive behavior (see Al-Anoud's case in 7.7).

Its limits

Its original norms are more than twenty years old, and this matters more near the threshold of 70 than anywhere else (see the box after Maryam's case). The years in which the Arabic versions were standardized are not precisely known.

The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fifth Edition (WISC-V), is also used with this population, for ages 6 years to 16 years 11 months; its US norms were collected around 2013–2014 (Wechsler, 2014). Its lowest Full Scale IQ is also 40, but its floor is higher on some subtests, so it may be less precise with moderate and severe intellectual disability. We found no published Saudi standardization of its fourth or fifth editions; the closest we found is a Saudi standardization of its older revised edition (Al-Nafi et al., 2000 [Arabic], as cited in Ejbara, 2024).

Vineland-3Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third EditionAdaptive behavior

The idea

The Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition (Vineland-3; Sparrow et al., 2016), measure what a person typically does in everyday life, not what he or she can do in a testing situation. The source is therefore always someone who knows the person well, and the question is: does the person perform this skill independently and regularly when it is needed?

What it looks like

The scales come in three forms: a semi-structured interview with a parent or caregiver, a form completed by a parent, and a form completed by a teacher. Each has a comprehensive version and a shorter domain-level version. Items are distributed across three main domains, Communication, Daily Living Skills and Socialization, along with an optional Motor Skills domain administered to young children up to about age nine and an optional Maladaptive Behavior scale. Each item is scored 2 if the person usually performs the behavior, 1 if the person performs it sometimes or partially, and 0 if the person does not perform it.

A scene from administration

The specialist sits with the mother of a nine-year-old girl. Rather than reading the items to her one by one, he opens with a broad question: “Tell me about your daughter's morning on a school day, from the time she wakes up until she leaves the house.” From the mother's description he scores many items without asking about them directly. When she says her daughter “gets dressed on her own,” he asks, “Does she choose clothes that suit the weather? Does she need someone to remind her?” because the answer separates a 2 from a 1. He follows the start and discontinue rules in the manual, so he does not ask about skills far beyond the girl's level. The interview takes about an hour. At the end he asks, “Is there a skill she can do but doesn't get the chance to?” and notes the answer next to the score.

How are the results interpreted?

The Vineland domains do not map directly onto the three DSM domains. Socialization is closest to the social domain, Daily Living Skills is closest to the practical domain, and Communication covers part of the conceptual domain. Money and time skills, which are conceptual in the DSM, fall under Daily Living Skills in the Vineland. Linking the two systems is therefore a clinical judgment that the examiner must make and state explicitly in the report.

Limitations

A score is only as accurate as the respondent and his or her understanding of the questions. The interview requires a skilled examiner, because leading questions can raise or lower the score. The US norms also assume opportunities and expectations that may not be available to the child in his or her own setting (see the box below).

ABAS-3Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, Third EditionAdaptive behavior

The idea

The Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, Third Edition (ABAS-3; Harrison & Oakland, 2015), measures adaptive behavior through forms completed by people who know the child well. Its advantage for diagnosis is that it yields the same three domains used by the DSM and the AAIDD: conceptual, social and practical.

What it looks like

Five forms: for parents (birth to 5 years, and 5 to 21), for teachers or daycare providers (2 to 5, and 5 to 21), and for adults (16 to 89). The respondent rates each skill according to whether the child performs it when it is needed, always, sometimes, or never, or is not able to perform it. Items are grouped into eleven skill areas, including communication, functional academics, self-care, and health and safety, then into the three domains, and finally into a general composite.

A scene from administration

The specialist sends the parent form to Umm Nawaf with a short note explaining that the questions concern what Nawaf usually does in his day, and that “is not able” differs from “is not asked to.” She gives his teacher the teacher form. When both forms come back, she reviews the omitted items, since the manual sets a maximum number for each skill area beyond which the area cannot be scored. She then calls the mother to ask about two items she answered by guessing.

How are the results interpreted?

Deficits are considered clear when the general composite, or the score for at least one domain, falls approximately two standard deviations below the mean, again taking measurement error into account (Tassé et al., 2012). Its limits

Unlike an interview, the form does not let the respondent explain an answer, so guessing becomes common when the respondent does not know how the child behaves in a particular situation. A new teacher knows the student less well than the family does. And no Arabic norms for the general child population are known for the instrument (Table 7.7).

In both cases, it is preferable to combine more than one respondent, such as the mother and the teacher, because a child may do at home what he does not do at school, and vice versa. The discrepancy between the two sources is information in its own right, which the examiner interprets and keeps in the report.

For the specialist: A scale designed for decisions at the cut-off

Most adaptive behavior scales are designed to describe performance across its full range, so their items are spread over all levels of difficulty. The Diagnostic Adaptive Behavior Scale (Diagnostic Adaptive Behavior Scale, DABS), developed by the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities for ages 4 to 21, was instead built with item response theory (see 2.5.4) to reach its greatest precision in the critical region around two standard deviations below the mean, that is, where the diagnostic decision is made. It is administered through an interview with someone who knows the individual well, and yields scores for the conceptual, social, and practical domains and a total score. We know of no published standardized Arabic version at the time of writing. Its underlying idea is a general lesson in measurement: an instrument that measures everyone does not necessarily measure most precisely at the point where the decision is made.

Adaptive behavior and culture

What is expected of a child in everyday life varies across communities and families. A child in a family where children are spoon-fed until a late age may not eat independently simply because he has never been asked to. The examiner therefore always asks: Has the child had the opportunity to practice this skill? And is he expected to perform it in his environment?

Had Maryam scored 74 with adaptive behavior close to average, she would not have been diagnosed. This is what it means to say that the cut-off is a professional judgment that gathers all the evidence around the number.

What evidence could change the decision? If a careful interview with the mother revealed that her low ratings reflect the limited opportunities Maryam has at home, or if a test with more recent norms yielded a clearly higher score with wide index scatter resembling a learning disability pattern, the team would need to revisit the decision.

For the specialist: The age of norms near the cut-off

In many countries, average performance on intelligence tests has risen from one generation to the next, and a meta-analysis estimated this gain at about two to three standard score points per decade (Trahan et al., 2014; see also 2.7). If this holds in the child's environment, each decade that passes after the norms were collected raises the child's score by a similar amount, because the child is being compared with a generation that performed less well than his or her own.

Let us apply this to Maryam. The U.S. norms for the Wechsler scale were collected around 2013–2014, so if Maryam were tested in 2026 the norms would be about twelve years old, implying a possible inflation of between two and a half and three and a half points. Her score of 74 might therefore correspond to about 71 on contemporary norms, which strengthens the decision rather than changing it. If, however, the 74 had been obtained on the Stanford-Binet, whose norms were collected around 2001, the norms would be nearly twenty-five years old and the possible inflation between five and seven points, so the score might correspond to 67 to 69. This difference carries practical weight, since it falls on the very line that separates eligibility under the Saudi regulations (see 7.10).

Even so, the number is not subtracted mechanically. The effect varies from country to country, it has slowed or reversed in some, and we know of no studies estimating it in the Kingdom. The collection years of Arabic standardizations are, for the most part, not precisely known. Accepted practice is for the examiner to state the year of the norms in the report, to take their age into account when interpreting a score near the cut-off, as the AAIDD manual recommends (Schalock et al., 2021), and to weigh this alongside the rest of the evidence.

As she moves to secondary school, the team has requested a reevaluation. Her teacher says her progress has “stalled”: she reads about twenty familiar words, including her name and some signs, counts objects up to ten, and does not handle money. She speaks in short sentences that her family understands, dresses and eats on her own, needs reminders about personal hygiene, and does not leave the house alone. Her parents are first cousins, and she has a younger brother who is delayed in walking and talking. The family has never been referred for genetic testing.

The drop in her score from 56 to 43 does not mean that she has regressed. A standard score compares her with same-age peers, and the gap between her and them widens with age even as she continues to learn, while the test's floor compresses low scores together. The team recommends a transition plan for secondary school that focuses on functional academic skills (reading safety signs, using money with a calculator), supervised community mobility skills, early vocational exploration, and determining the intensity of her support needs in each domain.

The lesson

When a test reaches its floor, the IQ stops being informative: it confirms the presence of a deficit but says nothing about its level or about the support required. Both severity and planning then shift to adaptive behavior. This is why ICD-11 provides behavioral indicators for use when tests fall short. The difference between a standard score that declines with age, which is expected, and a loss of acquired skills, which is a medical red flag, is one the team must ask about explicitly.

What evidence could change the decision? If it emerged that Al-Anoud had begun to lose skills she had acquired, or if the geneticist determined that the identified cause is a progressive disorder, the question would shift from specifying severity to urgent medical follow-up and a comprehensive reevaluation of her level and needs.

7.9Co-occurrence

Differential diagnosis asks: which of the two conditions? Co-occurrence asks instead: is another condition present as well? In intellectual disability, co-occurrence is the rule rather than the exception. In a British national sample, 36% of children and adolescents with intellectual disability had a diagnosable psychiatric disorder, compared with 8% of their peers (Emerson & Hatton, 2007). The greatest risk here is what is known as diagnostic overshadowing (Diagnostic overshadowing): attributing every new symptom to the intellectual disability itself, so that anxiety, depression, pain, or epileptic seizures go unnoticed (Reiss et al., 1982).

This comparison prompts three observations. The first is that the two numbers in the Guide are not equivalent. A cut-off of 75 on the Wechsler lies about one and two-thirds standard deviations below the mean, whereas a cut-off of 69 on the Stanford-Binet Fifth Edition lies more than two standard deviations below it. In the older Regulations, the difference between 75 and 73 reflected the different standard deviation of earlier editions of the Stanford-Binet, which was 16 rather than 15. The fifth edition, however, has a standard deviation of 15, like the Wechsler, so this cannot explain the gap between 75 and 69, and we found no rationale for it in the Guide. Maryam, who scored 74 on the Wechsler, meets the intelligence requirement; had she obtained the same 74 on the Stanford-Binet, she would not, even though the two scores denote the same position relative to peers.

The second is that the nine adaptive behavior areas in the Guide (communication, self-care, home living, social skills, community use, self-direction, health and safety, functional academics, and leisure and work skills) are close to the ten areas in the 1992 AAIDD definition (Luckasson et al., 1992), which the association itself has since replaced with the three domains. Requiring two of nine areas differs from requiring one of three domains, and permitting “informal” instruments for this criterion is broader than the DSM's call for a standardized measure. The third is that the Guide requires instruments “approved by the Ministry of Education,” yet at the time of writing we found no published list of such instruments, nor any published Saudi standardization of the recent editions of the Wechsler scales or of adaptive behavior scales (Table 7.7).

A question for reflection: If Maryam's case came before you at your school, how would you write the team's decision so that it respects the eligibility criterion in the Organizational Guide while also reporting the confidence interval and the age of the norms?

Conclusion

The argument we have developed in this chapter centers on the claim that intellectual disability is a composite judgment that cannot be derived from an IQ score alone. Adaptive behavior is the criterion that guards against misdiagnosis; it determines the level of severity in the DSM, and it becomes the primary source for judging severity once an intelligence test reaches its floor. The cut-off of 70 is read in light of the confidence interval and the age of the norms, so a person scoring around 75 may be diagnosed if the adaptive deficits are clear. It follows that hearing and vision screening must precede every instrument, that referral for genetic testing is recommended in every case of unexplained intellectual disability, that the diagnosis of global developmental delay is used provisionally below age five, and that diagnostic overshadowing must be guarded against when other disorders accompany the disability.

Still, the chapter leaves questions that remain matters of professional judgment, beyond what the rules can settle. The Saudi criteria are eligibility criteria that differ from those of the DSM in the intelligence cut-off and in the number of adaptive areas, and within them the Wechsler cut-off differs from the Stanford-Binet cut-off, so a diagnosis may apply to a student who does not meet the eligibility criteria. The educational question therefore remains the one most deserving of attention: What support does the student need, where, and how much?

7.11Summary, key terms, and activities

Chapter summary

  • Intellectual disability is diagnosed when three criteria are met together: intellectual deficits, adaptive deficits, and onset during the developmental period. AAIDD has raised the age of onset to before 22.
  • The cut-off of 70 is not absolute; the confidence interval and the age of the norms are taken into account, so a person scoring up to about 75 may be diagnosed if the adaptive deficits are clear.
  • Adaptive behavior is the criterion that protects against misdiagnosis; it determines the level of severity in the DSM and shares that role with ability in ICD-11. When an intelligence test reaches its floor, adaptive behavior becomes the primary source for judging severity.
  • Below age five, global developmental delay is used as a provisional diagnosis; its ICD-11 counterpart is “disorder of intellectual development, provisional,” below age four.
  • Hearing and vision screening is a prerequisite before any instrument; referral for genetic testing is recommended in every case of unexplained intellectual disability, and becomes more important when the parents are related.
  • The Vineland domains do not map directly onto the DSM domains, whereas the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System yields them directly.
  • The Saudi criteria are eligibility criteria that differ from those of the DSM in the intelligence cut-off and the number of adaptive areas, and within them the Wechsler cut-off differs from the Stanford-Binet cut-off.
  • Co-occurrence is common, diagnostic overshadowing is a constant risk, and co-occurring symptoms are judged against mental age.
  • The most important educational question: What support does the student need, where, and how much?

Glossary of terms

Intellectual disability
Significant deficits in intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior that begin during the developmental period.
Adaptive behavior
The conceptual, social, and practical skills that people learn and perform in their everyday lives.
Global developmental delay
A provisional diagnosis for a child under five who is delayed in two or more developmental domains.
Disorder of intellectual development, provisional
An ICD-11 category for a child under four, or for a person who cannot be validly assessed.
Routing subtest
A short subtest that determines the starting level for the remaining subtests of a scale.
Support needs intensity
The amount and type of support a person needs to participate in life activities.
Borderline intellectual functioning
Below-average intellectual ability that does not meet the criteria for intellectual disability, for which the current DSM sets no numerical cut-off.
Chromosomal microarray
A genetic test that detects small deletions or duplications of chromosomal material that conventional karyotyping cannot see.
Exome sequencing
A genetic test that reads the protein-coding portions of nearly all genes in search of a causal variant.
Diagnostic overshadowing
Attributing new symptoms to the intellectual disability itself, so that a co-occurring condition that could be diagnosed and treated goes unnoticed.
