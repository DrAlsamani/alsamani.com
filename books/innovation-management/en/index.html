Innovation Management

This book addresses a central question in innovation management: how does an organization move from scattered initiatives that celebrate innovation to a sustained institutional capability to produce it? It follows the path of innovation from the first signal to a solution that is adopted in practice and creates value, and it examines what turns that path into an established organizational practice: strategy, systems, culture, governance, teams and measurement. Throughout, it places people at the center, at a time when artificial intelligence is accelerating change.

Dr. Omar A. Alsamani

PrefaceAbout this book

The word “innovation” now appears in plans, strategies, department names and awards, yet its presence has not always come with agreement on what it means. It may refer to creativity, new technology, improvement, entrepreneurship, or simply the launch of a new initiative. This breadth has a clear practical consequence: an organization that speaks in favor of innovation without a shared understanding of what it means by it, and without a system that captures opportunities, tests them and turns them into value.

The book starts from the premise that innovation is a managerial and institutional capability that can be built, learned and improved. It is more than a seasonal exercise in collecting ideas, and broader than the remit of a single department. Innovation management is the way an organization sets its directions and opportunities, receives ideas and initiatives, tests its assumptions, allocates its resources, decides whether to continue, adjust or stop, and then moves successful solutions into operation and scale.

The book brings together two perspectives: that of management, which builds systems, structures and governance, and that of psychology, which studies the innovator: their characteristics, their motivation and what they need from their environment. Many organizations have spent on centres, platforms and programs that remained empty, because they were designed around outputs and overlooked the people who were supposed to innovate within them.

The principles the book sets out apply to government bodies, companies and nonprofit organizations, even though the meaning of “value” differs from one context to another. Most of its examples, however, come from government bodies and universities, because this is the context we know closely and because the innovation challenges in it have received less attention in Arabic writing. The book follows a university case across several chapters and brings it together in Chapter 20 as an application of all its ideas. It draws on the international standards for innovation management (the ISO 56000 series) and the Oslo Manual, and on the literature of management and the psychology of creativity, reading all of this in light of the Saudi experience wherever possible.

The common thread

The chapters are organized around three pillars: the problem, which sets the direction, since innovation begins from a priority need rather than from scattered ideas; people, who innovate through their motivation and needs; and the system, which connects people to the problem and turns effort into results. Value is created when motivated people meet a clear problem within a system that enables them. A principle that recurs throughout the book links the first two pillars: leadership and the system define the “what” and the “why,” and people own the “how.”

Premises of the book

  • Innovation is an implemented outcome that creates value; a new idea alone is not enough to be called innovation.
  • The more accurate starting point is a signal, a need or a problem worth understanding; the idea is a hypothesis for a solution.
  • Innovation management begins with strategy and priority opportunities, before collecting ideas.
  • Uncertainty is a natural part of innovation, which is why learning and experimentation are the basis for decisions.
  • People are the core of the innovation system. Innovators have characteristics and needs, such as recognition, opportunity, deferred judgment, cognitive autonomy and meaning, and no system succeeds if it does not understand them and build its processes around them.
  • A supportive culture is necessary, but it is no substitute for governance, funding, roles and measurement.
  • An initiative succeeds fully when its users adopt it, it is integrated into operations and it achieves what it was intended to achieve.
  • An innovation system is built from within, starting from the organization’s own strengths, needs and character, not by borrowing other organizations’ models and goals.
