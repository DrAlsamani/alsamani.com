Chapter 1

What is innovation?

This chapter examines the concept of innovation through four elements drawn from the reference definitions: novelty, implementation, adoption and value. It aims to build a shared language that distinguishes innovation from creativity, invention and improvement, and that sets out its types and degrees, on the view that agreeing on the concept is a precondition for managing it.

Introduction

Opening case: the annual planning meeting (composite case)

At a meeting to prepare an organization’s annual plan, each department was asked to present its “innovative initiatives.” Human Resources presented an electronic leave-request form to replace the paper one; IT presented a project to pilot an AI assistant to answer enquiries; Services proposed an ideas competition for staff; and Quality showed how it had cut request processing time from ten days to six by reordering the steps of the procedure.

The meeting ended with a list of initiatives, all labeled “innovation,” without anyone asking the essential question: which of these is innovation in the strict sense? Which is an operational improvement that simply needs to be done well? Which is an activity that prepares the ground for innovation without being innovation itself? And what does this distinction imply for how each should be managed?

Versions of this case recur in many organizations. Some of these initiatives may be of high value, but without a shared definition the organization ends up managing initiatives of very different kinds in the same way: a highly uncertain initiative is asked for a detailed implementation plan, a well-understood improvement is treated as a bet that needs experimentation, and a motivational activity is counted as an innovation achievement before any of its outputs has become reality.

Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

  1. Define innovation in practical terms that bring together its four elements, and compare its main reference definitions.
  2. Determine the degree of novelty of an initiative: new to the organization, new to the sector, or new to the world.
  3. Distinguish innovation from creativity, invention, improvement, transformation and entrepreneurship.
  4. Classify an initiative by type and degree, and infer the management approach that suits it.
  5. Explain the eight principles of innovation management in the international ISO 56000 series of standards.
  6. Identify inaccurate notions of innovation in an organization’s discourse and practice.

1.1The need for a shared definition

Attention to definition may look like an academic concern, but in innovation management it is a practical decision with direct consequences: the definition an organization adopts, explicitly or implicitly, determines what it funds, what it measures and what it rewards. Organizations usually fall into one of two opposite errors.

The first error: defining too broadly. When every change is called innovation, it becomes impossible to distinguish initiatives that require exploration, experimentation and learning from improvements that can be managed with quality and continuous-improvement tools. The result is reports full of “innovative” initiatives while what the organization delivers to the people it serves stays much the same. Blank called this phenomenon “innovation theater”: activities that imitate innovation in appearance, such as workshops, competitions and labs, without changing products, services or ways of working (Blank, 2019).

The second error: defining too narrowly. When innovation is restricted to inventions, advanced technologies and patents, wide opportunities are neglected in services, procedures, the user experience, the operating model and the relationships between organizational units. Many of the highest-impact innovations in government and service organizations required no new technology; they rested on rethinking how the service is delivered.

A good definition avoids both errors: broad enough to include every area where value is created, and precise enough to distinguish innovation from what it is not.

1.2Innovation in the reference definitions

The modern economic use of the concept of innovation goes back to Joseph Schumpeter, who held that economic development comes from carrying out “new combinations” of resources and knowledge, and identified five forms: introducing a new good or a new quality of an existing good, introducing a new method of production, opening a new market, securing a new source of raw materials, and reorganizing an industry (Schumpeter, 1934). Two points in this early view deserve attention: innovation goes beyond the product to methods, markets and organization, and it is tied to implementation rather than completed by discovery alone.

Later definitions built on this foundation. This section looks at three of them, as they are the most widely used in public policy, standards and measurement tools.

The Oslo Manual

The Oslo Manual, published by the OECD and Eurostat, is the international reference for measuring innovation in national surveys. Its fourth edition defines an innovation as a new or improved product or process (or combination thereof) that differs significantly from the unit’s previous products or processes and that has been made available to potential users (product) or brought into use by the unit (process) (OECD/Eurostat, 2018).

The decisive condition in this definition is implementation: an idea that never left the document, a prototype that was never launched, or a system that was purchased but never switched on is not an innovation by the Oslo standard, however novel it is. The Manual adds a second condition, “significant difference,” so that a routine minor adjustment does not count as innovation.

The international ISO 56000 series

The International Organization for Standardization defines innovation as a new or changed entity that realizes or redistributes value (ISO, 2025). The notion of an entity here is deliberately broad: it may be a product, a service, a process, a model or a method. The definition places value at its center, since novelty is not enough unless it brings benefit, whether financial, social, environmental or otherwise. The phrase “redistributes” allows the inclusion of innovations that change who benefits from value without necessarily increasing its total, as when a service reaches a group that was previously excluded from it.

Innovation as what is perceived as new

In the literature on the diffusion of innovations, Rogers defined an innovation as an idea, practice or object that is perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption (Rogers, 2003). Damanpour’s definition of organizational innovation is close to this: the adoption of an idea or behavior that is new to the adopting organization (Damanpour, 1991). The value of this perspective is that it shifts the question from “is this new to the world?” to “is it new in this context, and does it require the people in it to change what they are used to?.”

The working definition used in this book

From these definitions the book derives a working definition that it uses in the chapters that follow:

Definition used in this book

Innovation is a new or significantly improved application that addresses a need or seizes an opportunity, is adopted by users or an operating unit, and creates observable value.

This definition has four elements, and the absence of any one of them is enough to keep an initiative short of innovation:

Novelty is a relative concept, discussed in the next section. Implementation is what separates innovation from ideas and inventions. Adoption is an element that is often overlooked, even though a good number of systems and platforms are officially launched and then left unused, or are used by staff at a bare minimum while they keep their old methods alongside. Value may be economic, social, operational, epistemic or environmental, and may accrue to the beneficiary, the employee, the organization or society.

The order of the elements has a temporal meaning: adoption and value come after implementation, so an initiative cannot be judged an innovation on the day it is launched. Launch day proves that a new application exists; innovation is proven only once its use and its effect have been verified. The book’s definition is therefore stricter than the minimum the Oslo Manual adopts for statistical measurement, which requires only that the new product be made available to users or the new process brought into use (OECD/Eurostat, 2018), because the book’s purpose is managerial: judging whether an initiative has done what it was launched to do.

1.3Relative novelty

A recurring question in management discussions is whether applying a solution that already exists elsewhere counts as innovation. Two practical consequences follow from this distinction:

First, transferring a successful solution from one context to another can be a genuine innovation, provided it requires meaningful adaptation and change in the adopting organization.

Second, the degree of novelty affects how the initiative should be managed. A solution new only to the organization can draw on others’ experience, and its uncertainty lies mainly in fit, adaptation and adoption. A solution new to the world is uncertain in almost every respect: whether it works, whether it is needed, and what it will cost.

Example

When a government agency adopts an appointment-booking system that other agencies introduced before it, it invents nothing new. But it may need to redistribute staff across time slots, change how walk-in visitors are received, and replace performance indicators that counted daily transactions. These changes are what make the application an innovation at the level of the organization, and they are also what determines its success.

1.4Innovation and neighboring concepts

In general usage the term innovation is used as a synonym for several related concepts. Distinguishing between them matters, because each has its own management logic, even if they overlap in practice.

Creativity and innovation

Researchers in the psychology of creativity agree that a creative product combines originality and appropriateness: it is new and unusual, and useful or suited to a given purpose (Amabile, 1996; Runco & Jaeger, 2012). Creativity in this sense is a main source of innovation, but there is a wide gap between them: a creative idea may stay in its author’s head or in the minutes of a meeting, whereas innovation requires what creativity does not: resources, organization, experimentation, decisions and adoption.

Rhodes proposed early on that creativity be viewed from four interrelated aspects: the creative person, with their abilities and motivation; the process by which ideas are generated and developed; the surrounding press or environment, with the pressures it imposes and the opportunities it offers; and the product in which creativity is embodied (Rhodes, 1961). The value of this view for innovation management becomes clear when it is set against the common practice of focusing on the product alone, such as the number of ideas, projects or patents, without attention to the people expected to innovate, the environment in which they work, and the processes that take up their ideas. Amabile showed that organizational innovation depends on the interaction between the creativity of individuals and teams and the work environment, including its resources, management practices and organizational motivation to innovate (Amabile, 1988). The book devotes Chapter 7 to this relationship.

Invention and innovation

Invention is the production of new knowledge, technology or combination, often documented by a patent. An invention becomes an innovation when it finds use and is incorporated into a product, service or process that creates value. A well-known example: in 1975 a Kodak engineer built an early prototype of the digital camera, yet the company was very slow to make digital photography central to its business, while the same technology became, in other companies’ hands, an innovation that transformed the industry. Not every invention is therefore an innovation, and not every innovation is an invention; many innovations, especially in services and business models, involve no new technology at all.

Improvement and innovation

Improvement raises the performance of what already exists: shortening the time a procedure takes, removing an unnecessary step, or raising the quality of a service. There is no conflict between improvement and innovation. An improvement rises to innovation when it differs substantially from previous practice, is brought into use and creates value. It remains operational development when it is a limited adjustment whose method is already known.

The most useful criterion for telling them apart is the degree of uncertainty, not the size of the change. If the solution is known, the cause understood and what is required is disciplined execution, continuous-improvement tools are sufficient. If the solution is unknown, or there are essential untested assumptions about the user, the implementation or the value, the initiative requires the logic of innovation: experimentation, learning and staged decisions.

Transformation and innovation

Transformation is a fundamental change in the business or operating model, in the relationship with those served, or in organizational capabilities. It requires a portfolio of integrated innovations and changes, and leadership and change management that extend over years. “Digital transformation” thus includes many innovations, but also work that involves no innovation, such as data migration, system consolidation and staff training.

Entrepreneurship and innovation

Entrepreneurship is concerned with discovering an opportunity and organizing resources around it to create a sustainable venture or activity. It may rely on innovation without being synonymous with it: innovation can take place within an existing organization without creating a new venture, and an entrepreneurial venture may apply a known model in a new market with little innovation. The term intrapreneurship (Intrapreneurship) describes entrepreneurial behavior within established organizations, and it is closely related to the subject of this book.

1.5Types of innovation

Innovation can occur in any element that contributes to creating or delivering value. The literature offers several classifications of its types; this section presents two that complement each other.

The Oslo Manual classification

The Oslo Manual groups innovation into two main types: product innovation, whether a good or a service, and business process innovation, which it subdivides into six functions (OECD/Eurostat, 2018).

Note that in this classification “product and process development” is itself a type of innovation: an organization that improves the way it explores opportunities and tests solutions is innovating in its very capacity to innovate, which is the subject of Part Two of the book.

The four Ps of Tidd and Bessant

Tidd and Bessant suggest looking at “innovation space” through four dimensions: product, what the organization offers; process, the way it is produced and delivered; position, the context in which it is offered and to whom; and paradigm, the underlying mental model of what the organization does (Tidd & Bessant, 2021). The last two dimensions are useful because they draw attention to innovations that change neither the product nor the process, such as offering an existing service to a new group, or redefining what the organization provides: a product, an outcome, or an integrated solution.

In Practice

Map your organization’s list of initiatives onto these two classifications. If the initiatives cluster in a single cell, such as technology or product, your organization’s view of innovation is narrower than the opportunities open to it. The largest opportunities often lie in the neglected areas: structure, networks, channels and the way decisions are made.

These types are complementary: an influential solution may combine a change in service, process, technology and operating model at the same time. Technology in particular is an enabler, and it counts as innovation only when it substantially changes value, experience or performance; digitizing a flawed procedure may speed up the problem rather than solve it.

1.6Degrees of innovation

Innovations differ in the amount of change they bring. The literature uses several classifications of these degrees, each with a different point of view.

Core, adjacent and transformational innovation

Incremental innovation builds on a known product, service, capability or group of beneficiaries, so its uncertainty is relatively low. Adjacent innovation takes an existing capability to a new group or market, or adds a new capability to a familiar field. Radical or transformational innovation changes the underlying assumptions, or offers substantially different value and a different model (Nagji & Tuff, 2012).

These degrees carry no value judgment: radical is not better in every case, and incremental is not minor. Successive improvements can accumulate into a large effect, and a radical idea may be unsuitable or impossible to adopt. What matters is that the organization identifies the uncertainty, risks and capabilities each initiative involves, and manages it accordingly. The book returns to this balance in the context of strategy and portfolio management in Chapters 4 and 10.

Architectural innovation is particularly important in service and government organizations, because many service problems stem from how the components are linked rather than from their weakness. Each department may do its work efficiently while the problem lies in how they connect: hand-offs between units, repeated requests for the same information, and fragmented ownership of the journey. Henderson and Clark showed that this type is especially challenging for established organizations, because it looks limited on the surface while requiring knowledge of the architecture of the whole system, knowledge that is usually embedded in structures and habitual communication channels.

Sustaining and disruptive innovation

Christensen distinguished between sustaining innovation, which improves product performance on the dimensions that existing customers value, and disruptive innovation, which often starts with lower performance on those dimensions but is simpler, cheaper or more accessible, attracts neglected or new groups, and then improves until it competes with the prevailing solutions (Christensen, 1997). The “dilemma” of successful organizations is that their very good practices, such as listening to their best customers and investing in the most profitable opportunities, lead them to ignore this type until it is too late.

1.7Innovation in the public and nonprofit sectors

The innovation literature grew up in the context of firms and markets and carried many of their assumptions, such as competition, profit and a customer free to choose. Yet innovation in government bodies and nonprofit organizations is just as important, and may be more so, because its effects reach large groups of beneficiaries who usually have no alternative to the service.

Mulgan and Albury defined successful public-sector innovation as the creation and implementation of new processes, products, services and methods of delivery that result in significant improvements in outcomes, efficiency, effectiveness or quality (Mulgan & Albury, 2003). Value in this context is what Moore called “public value”: what society and its members value, not merely what generates a financial return (Moore, 1995).

These differences mean that business tools cannot be transferred to the public sector without adaptation: return on investment alone is not enough to evaluate an initiative that aims to bring a service to an underserved group. The book uses the term “beneficiary” to include the customer, the citizen and the internal user, and the term “value” to include both economic and public value.

1.8Principles of innovation management

If innovation is the outcome, innovation management is the set of practices and capabilities that make that outcome possible and repeatable. It includes directing opportunities, ideas and initiatives, and managing their flow from the front end of innovation, where needs are explored and ideas generated, to implementation and the realization of value. The international ISO 56000 series rests on eight principles that summarize accumulated experience in the field (ISO, 2025; Fernandez & Swart, 2025); this section presents them because they offer a concise map of the book’s topics.

These principles do not prescribe particular tools such as design thinking or hackathons. Tools change; the principles determine when a tool is useful and when it becomes a formality.

1.9Common misconceptions

The conceptual part of the chapter closes with five inaccurate notions that recur in how organizations talk about innovation. Each leads to specific management decisions, so correcting them is not a matter of wording but of correcting practice.

Innovation starts with ideas

The more accurate starting point is a need, problem, opportunity or change that calls for a response; the idea comes later as a hypothesis for a solution. An organization that starts by collecting ideas without direction receives a large number of scattered proposals that are hard to evaluate, and few of them ever reach implementation. Chapter 11 discusses this in detail.

Innovation is technology

Technology is an important enabler, but many of the highest-impact innovations lie in services, procedures, business models and structures. Large technology projects have failed to deliver value because they were built for a problem that was not sufficiently understood.

Innovation is for creative people only

An organization needs people who spot problems, analyze evidence, design experiments, implement, sponsor and bring operational expertise. The person not known for a wealth of ideas may be the one who makes an idea workable.

Having a lab or platform proves innovative capacity

Labs, platforms and awards are tools, and they may become part of innovation theater if they are not linked to priorities, resources, decisions and an operating owner, or if they remain isolated from the daily work of units and their people. The test of innovative capacity is an organized flow of opportunities, learning from experiments, evidence-based decisions and results achieved.

Launch is the end of the road

Launch proves that a new application exists; innovation is proven only after it has been adopted and its effect realized. An organization that is content to celebrate the launch and then move on usually loses the hardest and most important part of the journey.

Applied cases: is this initiative an innovation?

This section returns to the initiatives in the opening case and applies the four elements to them. The cases are illustrative, and their details have been shaped to show the method of analysis.

The electronic form replaced the paper one, and staff used it because it was the only route available. But the approval path stayed the same, with five successive sign-offs, and the average completion time did not change. The novelty here is formal, and the value is limited to saving paper.

When would it rise to innovation? If the path itself were redesigned, for example by approving short leave automatically under clear rules and requiring a single approval in other cases, and a substantial reduction in time and workload were then measured.

Novelty is present at the level of the organization, but the initiative is still an idea: the reasons for the high volume of enquiries have not been studied, nor the quality of the information the assistant would rely on, nor who would be responsible for updating it. Calling it an innovation is therefore premature.

When would it rise to innovation? If it began by understanding why the enquiries arise, was then piloted on a limited scale, the reduction in repeated enquiries and user satisfaction were measured, and an owner were named to keep the content up to date.

The competition is neither an innovation nor its opposite; it is a mechanism for capturing opportunities, and its value depends on what follows it: whether it is linked to priority challenges, whether time and resources are available to evaluate the proposals and test the best of them, and whether participants receive a response to their proposals.

When would it be worthwhile? If it were framed around a specific challenge, if what would follow from selecting ideas were announced in advance, and if a modest budget were set aside to test some of them.

Quality reordered the steps of the procedure after finding that requests waited for days between two units without being processed. The two review stages were merged and a shared view of request status was created. Processing time fell from ten days to six, and the reduction held six months later. This is an architectural innovation new to the organization, even though it used no new technology.

What would strengthen the judgment? Comparing the time against a documented baseline, and checking that errors have not simply moved to a later stage.

What the cases show

The initiative whose name suggests innovation may be the furthest from it, and the initiative that looks modest may be the closest. Classifying an initiative determines how it is managed: improvement is managed through disciplined execution, a potential innovation through experimentation and staged decisions, and a preparatory activity through clarity about what will follow from it.

Chapter Summary

  • Innovation is a new or significantly improved application that addresses a need, is adopted by users and creates observable value. The absence of any of the four elements is enough to keep an initiative short of innovation.
  • The reference definitions (Schumpeter, the Oslo Manual, ISO 56000 and Rogers) agree that novelty alone is not enough, and differ in the aspect they emphasize.
  • Novelty is relative: to the organization, the sector or the world. Transferring solutions between contexts is genuine innovation when it requires adaptation and change.
  • Creativity produces ideas, invention produces knowledge or technology, improvement raises the performance of what exists, and transformation changes the whole model. The practical criterion for distinguishing improvement from innovation is the degree of uncertainty.
  • Creativity is not only about its product; it includes the person, the process and the environment, aspects on which an organization’s capacity to innovate depends.
  • Innovation occurs in products and services, in business processes, and in position and paradigm. Technology is one enabler, not a synonym for innovation.
  • Innovations range from incremental to radical; architectural innovation matters particularly in service organizations; and each degree has a management approach that suits it.
  • Public-sector innovation has a different logic of value, risk and diffusion, and requires adapted tools.
  • Innovation management rests on eight principles, the first of which is the realization of value and the last viewing innovation as a system.
